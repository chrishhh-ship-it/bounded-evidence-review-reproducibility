# Enhancing Report Quality through Reviewer Agents in Multi-Agent Collaborative Systems: A Comprehensive Survey

## 1 Introduction and Background

### 1.1 Defining Multi-Agent Collaborative Systems and Reviewer Agents

Multi-Agent Systems (MAS) represent a foundational paradigm in artificial intelligence, characterized by networks of autonomous, interacting software entities known as agents. These agents are endowed with the capability to perceive their environment, reason about their goals, and execute actions to achieve objectives, often operating in complex, dynamic domains where centralized control is impractical or inefficient. The collaborative nature of MAS emerges from the coordination and communication protocols that enable these autonomous agents to work collectively towards shared or individual goals, leveraging distributed knowledge and specialized capabilities [1]. This architecture offers significant advantages in adaptability, flexibility, and scalability, making it particularly suitable for intricate tasks such as automated customer service management, where multiple intelligent modules must cooperate to handle complex, multi-turn conversations [2]. The evolution towards more autonomous and goal-directed systems has given rise to the concept of "Agentic AI," where agents exhibit a higher degree of proactive problem-solving and environmental adaptation. This is exemplified in networking, where agentic frameworks aim to create ecosystems supporting diverse autonomous AI agents that interact and collaborate to fulfill networking goals [3].

Within this collaborative ecosystem, a critical specialization is the **reviewer agent**. Reviewer agents are specialized autonomous agents whose primary function is to evaluate, refine, and ensure the quality of outputs generated by other agents or the system as a whole. In the context of report generation—a key output in domains like healthcare, intelligence, and customer service—reviewer agents act as quality assurance modules. They systematically assess reports for dimensions such as factual accuracy, internal consistency, completeness, adherence to standards, and contextual relevance. This role draws directly from the principles of agentic systems, where different agents assume specific roles in a coordinated workflow. For instance, in a multi-agent decision support system, one can conceptualize specialized "evaluation" or "validation" agents that scrutinize the recommendations or analyses produced by "decision agents" [1]. The operationalization of reviewer agents is increasingly facilitated by advanced foundation models. As seen in proposed architectures, generative foundation models can be instantiated as interactive knowledge-base agents that, among other functions, can bootstrap and oversee the development of other agents, inherently incorporating a review-like capacity to ensure task alignment and quality [3]. Furthermore, the formalization of agent interactions and workflows, a hallmark of sophisticated multi-agent systems, is precisely what enables the structured integration of reviewer agents. By defining clear communication protocols and role specializations, these systems can institutionalize review steps, transforming ad-hoc checking into a systematic, automated process that enhances output consistency and reproducibility [4]. Thus, reviewer agents emerge not as an external add-on but as an integral, specialized component within the multi-agent collaborative fabric, dedicated to upholding and enhancing the integrity and utility of critical system outputs like analytical reports.

### 1.2 The Imperative of Report Quality in Critical Domains

The quality of analytical reports, characterized by their accuracy, consistency, completeness, and timeliness, is not merely a procedural benchmark but a critical determinant of outcomes in high-stakes domains. In fields such as healthcare, intelligence analysis, and customer service, the imperative for superior report quality is directly linked to operational efficacy, safety, and trust. Substandard reports—riddled with factual errors, inconsistencies, omissions, or delays—can precipitate severe risks, including misdiagnoses, flawed strategic decisions, financial losses, and erosion of public confidence. This establishes the foundational motivation for integrating specialized reviewer agents within multi-agent collaborative systems to systematically enhance these quality dimensions.

In healthcare, the consequences of poor report quality are particularly acute. Diagnostic inaccuracies or incomplete patient summaries can directly compromise patient safety and treatment efficacy. The deployment of next-generation agentic AI systems aims to transform this landscape by delivering context-aware, patient-centric care with heightened precision [5]. Similarly, in medical decision support, the integration of evidence through agentic systems is crucial for generating reliable reports that inform clinical workflows [6]. The timeliness of such reports is equally vital during public health emergencies. As highlighted in outbreak analytics, generating real-time, evidence-based situational reports is essential for limiting pathogen spread, yet manual processes create significant bottlenecks [4]. Delays or errors in these reports can have profound public health consequences.

Beyond healthcare, the imperative extends to intelligence and security domains, where reports must synthesize complex, multi-source information to support critical decisions. Inconsistencies or gaps in intelligence assessments can lead to catastrophic policy failures. In the commercial sphere, particularly in customer service and operations, report quality underpins organizational performance. AI agents enhance service efficiency and user experience by automating processes and ensuring accurate, consistent customer interactions [7]. The quality of service claims processing, especially in reaching rural populations, significantly impacts economic and market performance for companies, where efficiency and security in reporting are paramount [8]. Furthermore, in broader operations management, the effective application of artificial intelligence and multi-agent systems is key to optimizing performance and maintaining competitive advantage, which is inherently tied to the quality of generated operational reports and analyses [9].

These domains collectively underscore that report quality is a multi-faceted requirement. Accuracy ensures factual correctness and reduces hallucinations; consistency enforces standardization for reliable interpretation; completeness guarantees all relevant contextual data is integrated; and timeliness enables dynamic responsiveness to evolving situations. The failure to meet any of these criteria can trigger a cascade of adverse outcomes. Therefore, the core motivation for deploying reviewer agents within collaborative AI systems is to institutionalize rigorous, automated mechanisms for evaluation and refinement, thereby mitigating these risks and transforming report generation from a potential vulnerability into a source of robust, decision-grade intelligence.

## 2 Architectures and Mechanisms of Reviewer Agents

### 2.1 Foundational Architectures and Agent Typologies

The foundational architectures of multi-agent systems (MAS) that underpin reviewer agents can be broadly categorized into hierarchical and heterarchical (or decentralized) paradigms, each offering distinct advantages for collaborative report generation and review. Hierarchical architectures organize agents in a top-down structure, where a central controller or manager agent delegates subtasks to specialized subordinate agents and synthesizes their outputs. This paradigm is particularly effective in domains requiring strict adherence to protocols and centralized oversight, such as clinical workflows where a master agent might orchestrate diagnostic reasoning and report generation [6]. Conversely, heterarchical architectures feature a more decentralized, peer-to-peer network of autonomous agents that negotiate and coordinate through mutual communication protocols. This design enhances flexibility and robustness, allowing the system to adapt dynamically to new information or agent failures, which is crucial in complex, evolving environments like automated customer service management [2].

Within these overarching architectural paradigms, specialized typologies of reviewer agents emerge, each fulfilling a specific quality assurance function. **Evaluation Agents** are tasked with assessing the initial output against predefined metrics such as factual accuracy, logical coherence, and adherence to format. They often employ cross-referencing mechanisms against trusted knowledge sources to identify inconsistencies or hallucinations. **Validation Agents** take a more stringent role, verifying that the report's conclusions are supported by the integrated evidence and that the reasoning process aligns with domain-specific rules or guidelines. For instance, in a clinical multi-agent system, a validation agent might check diagnostic suggestions against established medical ontologies or patient data [12]. **Transparency Agents** constitute a critical typology focused on generating explanations for the system's outputs and decisions. They enhance trust and interpretability by elucidating the reasoning chain or highlighting the evidence used, which is essential for human-in-the-loop oversight in high-stakes domains [12]. Other typologies include **Integration Agents**, which synthesize inputs from multiple specialized agents (e.g., lab analysis and contextual reasoners) into a cohesive narrative, and **Refinement Agents**, which orchestrate iterative improvement loops based on feedback from other reviewer agents or human supervisors. The selection and orchestration of these agent typologies within a chosen architectural paradigm fundamentally shape the efficacy and reliability of the overall review process in collaborative systems.

### 2.2 Knowledge Integration and Management Mechanisms

The efficacy of a reviewer agent in a multi-agent collaborative system is fundamentally contingent upon its capacity to access, integrate, and manage relevant, high-fidelity knowledge. This subsection analyzes the core mechanisms that enable reviewer agents to ground their evaluations and refinements in evidence, ensuring reports are both accurate and contextually informed. A primary mechanism is the integration of structured knowledge representations, such as knowledge graphs and ontologies. These provide a semantic framework that allows agents to reason over relationships between entities. For instance, in clinical decision support, multi-agent systems leverage medical ontologies to integrate heterogeneous knowledge bases from semiology, pharmacology, and clinical cases, enabling coherent and semantically grounded reasoning [13]. Similarly, the HACID-CSR system utilizes a domain-specific knowledge graph (CS-DKG) for climate services, which maps relationships between climate variables, hazards, and datasets, providing a curated knowledge backbone for workflow generation [14].

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is another pivotal mechanism, dynamically fetching relevant information from external corpora to augment an agent's contextual knowledge before generation or review. This is crucial for mitigating factual inaccuracies or "hallucinations." Research in dialog systems demonstrates that integrating RAG with agent architectures significantly improves factual accuracy by providing agents with real-time, domain-specific knowledge [15]. The agentic RAG paradigm extends this by orchestrating autonomous agents to coordinate complex workflows, such as retrieving and integrating evidence into clinical decision-making cycles [6]. Advanced implementations like GraphRAG further refine this by retrieving and reasoning over subgraphs from a larger knowledge structure, as seen in the recipe generation agent of HACID-CSR, which combines LLM reasoning with retrieved graph context [14].

Furthermore, specialized multi-agent data retrieval (MDR) systems are designed to manage knowledge acquisition at scale. These systems employ dedicated agents to refine, summarize, and infer knowledge from vast bases, effectively expanding the knowledge boundaries of the core LLMs. The CommLLM system for 6G communications, for example, uses condensate and inference agents within its MDR component to process and summarize communication-specific knowledge, ensuring the subsequent planning and evaluation agents operate on high-quality, relevant information [16]. In financial e-services, effective knowledge integration across heterogeneous agent knowledge bases is identified as a critical challenge, necessitating automated methods to synthesize diverse information into a coherent basis for final decisions [17]. Ultimately, these integrated knowledge management mechanisms—spanning graphs, RAG, ontologies, and multi-agent retrieval—form the foundational layer that empowers reviewer agents to perform evidence-based validation, cross-referencing, and contextual enrichment, directly contributing to the observable improvements in report quality detailed in Section 3.

### 2.3 Inter-Agent Communication and Coordination Frameworks

Effective communication and coordination are foundational to the operation of reviewer agents within multi-agent collaborative systems. These frameworks ensure that specialized agents, including those tasked with evaluation and refinement, can reliably exchange information, align their tasks, and collectively contribute to enhancing report quality. This subsection examines the key protocols and models that underpin this critical functionality.

A primary enabler is the development of standardized APIs and agent-to-agent protocols, which provide a common language for interaction. Initiatives like the Open Voice Interoperability initiative propose Universal Open APIs based on Natural Language to establish interoperable interactions among diverse conversational AI agents, including chatbots and voicebots [18]. Similarly, protocols such as the agent-to-agent protocol aim to standardize communication, though challenges in cross-vendor service discovery persist [19]. To address this, service discovery systems like AgentDNS introduce a structured mechanism for agents to autonomously discover, resolve, and securely invoke third-party services across organizational boundaries, drawing inspiration from traditional DNS principles [19]. This is complemented by frameworks for authenticated delegation, which extend established protocols like OAuth 2.0 to manage authorization and accountability for AI agents, ensuring secure and scoped interactions [20].

Beyond syntactic interoperability, reliable coordination requires robust mathematical and logical models. Mathematical modeling using coordination mechanisms is employed to validate and verify the accuracy of responses in cross-platform communications within Service-Oriented Architectures, addressing a critical need in complex, multi-participant systems [21]. For higher reliability in knowledge exchange, some systems move beyond prompt-based communication. The paradigm proposed in [22] utilizes a shared knowledge base mediated by formal reasoning tools like Answer Set Programming (ASP). This ensures communication consistency and security, as information is encapsulated and not directly exposed in unreliable prompts, a method demonstrated in collaborative service management systems.

Furthermore, advanced architectures integrate these communication and coordination layers into cohesive workflows. For instance, in systems designed for complex domains like 6G communications, multi-agent collaborative planning (MCP) components rely on underlying coordination frameworks to allow multiple planning agents to generate solutions from different perspectives [16]. The coordination mechanisms ensure that the outputs from these planning agents, as well as data from retrieval agents, are effectively synthesized and passed to subsequent evaluation and refinement agents, creating a seamless pipeline for producing high-quality, evidence-based reports. Thus, the interplay of standardized interfaces, discovery services, formal models, and integrated workflows forms the backbone that allows reviewer agents to perform their quality assurance role effectively within a collaborative ecosystem.

### 2.4 Planning, Reasoning, and Collaborative Workflow Engines

The operational efficacy of reviewer agents within multi-agent collaborative systems hinges on sophisticated planning, reasoning, and collaborative workflow engines. These mechanisms enable the systematic decomposition of complex review tasks, the formulation of coherent action plans, and the orchestration of collaborative reasoning among specialized agents. A core architectural paradigm is multi-agent collaborative planning (MCP), where multiple planning agents generate and evaluate solutions from diverse perspectives [16]. This approach is exemplified in systems like CommLLM, where planning agents leverage retrieved domain knowledge to devise feasible solutions for communication tasks, ensuring plans are grounded in specialized information. Similarly, frameworks such as P1GPT implement a **structured reasoning pipeline** that systematically fuses multi-modal insights through coordinated agent communication, moving beyond simple role simulation to enforce a logical workflow for analysis [23]. This structured approach is critical for reviewer agents to ensure that the evaluation process is transparent, reproducible, and follows a defensible chain of logic.

Capability collaboration models provide another vital mechanism, where the review process is decomposed into subtasks handled by agents with distinct, complementary skills. The CACA Agent framework operationalizes this by using an open, service-inspired architecture that integrates a set of collaborative capabilities, reducing dependence on a single LLM and enhancing the extensibility of both planning abilities and available tools [24]. This allows reviewer agents to dynamically assemble teams where one agent might validate factual claims, another checks for consistency with standards, and a third assesses contextual completeness. Furthermore, these planning and reasoning engines are deeply integrated with external tools, databases, and Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs). For instance, in geoscientific applications, LLM-driven agents orchestrate the execution of specialized models (e.g., HEC-RAS for flood simulation) and retrieve data according to predefined operational workflows, synthesizing insights into coherent reports [25]. In outbreak analytics, the formalization of agent interactions within a pipeline is highlighted as a key advantage for optimizing workflows, enhancing consistency, and enabling quality control through multiple review steps [4]. These integrations ensure that the reviewer agent's planning is not abstract but is concretely tied to executable actions, authoritative knowledge sources, and domain-specific protocols, thereby grounding the final report in verified processes and evidence.

### 2.5 Evaluation, Reflection, and Iterative Refinement Loops

The efficacy of a reviewer agent within a multi-agent collaborative system hinges on its structured capacity for critical assessment and iterative improvement. This subsection delineates the specific architectural components and processes that enable reviewer agents to evaluate, reflect upon, and refine outputs, thereby directly enhancing the quality of final reports. These mechanisms often manifest as dedicated modules or specialized agents within a larger workflow, forming closed-loop systems for quality assurance.

A foundational component is the multi-agent evaluation module, a formalized process where outputs from generating agents are systematically assessed against predefined criteria such as factual accuracy, logical coherence, and adherence to format. As illustrated in the CommLLM system for 6G communications, a dedicated evaluation agent is employed to assess proposed solutions generated by planning agents [16]. This modular separation of generation and evaluation roles ensures an objective review, preventing confirmation bias that might arise if a single agent performed both tasks. The evaluation can leverage cross-referencing against retrieved knowledge bases or involve multiple reviewer agents for consensus-building, a process that enhances consistency and reproducibility in outputs like outbreak analytics reports [4].

Beyond a single-pass assessment, sophisticated systems incorporate reflection and iterative refinement loops. Here, the evaluation output is not merely a score but actionable feedback. A reflection agent analyzes the evaluation results to diagnose root causes of deficiencies—such as missing data, flawed reasoning, or ambiguous language—and generates specific improvement suggestions. Subsequently, a refinement agent, or the original generating agent, uses these suggestions to revise the output. The CommLLM system explicitly chains an evaluation agent with a reflection agent and a refinement agent to provide and implement improvement suggestions for communication task solutions [16]. This creates a multi-step iterative cycle where reports are progressively enhanced.

Furthermore, reinforcement learning (RL) provides a paradigm for embedding long-term improvement feedback cycles into reviewer agent mechanisms. Agents can learn from the outcomes of their review actions; for instance, a reviewer agent's policy for identifying errors can be optimized based on whether its flagged issues lead to measurable improvements in the final report's downstream utility. Research on intelligent learning techniques for multi-agent systems proposes improved cooperative learning methods where agents share strategies through continuous learning [26]. In a credit risk context, an Agentic AI framework employs continuous feedback learning cycles, allowing agents to adapt their decision-making and review criteria over time based on new data and outcomes [27]. This enables reviewer agents to evolve and improve their assessment heuristics autonomously, moving from static rule-based checking to adaptive, context-sensitive quality control.

### 2.6 Human-Agent Interaction and Oversight Interfaces

The integration of human judgment into the review process is a critical architectural consideration for ensuring the reliability and ethical alignment of multi-agent systems. Reviewer agents, therefore, are often embedded within frameworks that feature dedicated Human-Agent Interaction (HAI) and oversight interfaces. These components are designed not merely for passive monitoring but for active, strategic collaboration, enabling a "Human-in-Command" (HIC) paradigm where human oversight is a systemic property of the interaction design [28]. A core architectural element is the interface for strategic handoffs, which defines clear operational triggers—such as low-confidence assessments, ethical dilemmas, or requests for domain-expert validation—that prompt the reviewer agent to cede control or seek input from a human operator. This ensures that critical decisions retain human decisional ownership, a principle emphasized in high-stakes domains like customer service and clinical support [28].

To make these handoffs meaningful and to build user trust, explainability modules and transparency agents are integral. These components translate the often-opaque multi-agent reasoning processes into human-interpretable justifications. For instance, a transparency agent can be tasked with generating audit trails that document the contributions and validation steps of each specialized agent in the workflow [12]. This addresses user concerns about the opacity of AI decision-making, which is a known barrier to trust in conversational systems [29]. Furthermore, governance frameworks provide the structural backbone for this oversight, establishing protocols for accountability, compliance, and ethical alignment. These frameworks operationalize concepts from regulations like the EU AI Act by embedding requirements for human oversight, data provenance tracking, and bias mitigation directly into the agent communication and review protocols [28]. Ultimately, these architectural components—handoff interfaces, explainability tools, and governance layers—work in concert to create a robust human-in-the-loop system that leverages the scalability of reviewer agents while preserving essential human judgment and oversight in the report refinement process.

## 3 Observable Improvements in Report Quality

### 3.1 Enhancement of Accuracy and Factual Correctness

A primary and empirically demonstrable improvement conferred by reviewer agents in multi-agent collaborative systems is the significant enhancement of report accuracy and factual correctness. This is achieved through structured multi-agent validation and cross-referencing mechanisms that systematically reduce errors, including the notorious "hallucinations" prevalent in single large language model (LLM) applications. By formalizing review steps within a collaborative workflow, reviewer agents act as specialized validators that scrutinize the outputs of generation agents against authoritative knowledge sources and the reasoning of peer agents. For instance, in clinical diagnostics, an agentic retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) system can deploy a dedicated validation agent to cross-check a diagnostic report generated by a reasoning agent against integrated knowledge graphs and clinical guidelines [6]. This multi-layered verification process ensures that medical assertions are evidence-based, directly mitigating the risk of factual inaccuracies that could lead to detrimental patient outcomes. Empirical evidence supports this architectural advantage; a study implementing a multi-agent system for clinical decision support demonstrated superior prediction accuracy for patient mortality compared to a single-agent system, a gain attributable to the validation and integration functions performed by specialized reviewer agents [12].

The mitigation of hallucination risks is particularly critical in customer-facing and analytical domains where erroneous information can erode trust and lead to operational failures. A multi-agent architecture designed for customer service explicitly employs reviewer agents with fuzzy logic to assess and verify responses before they are communicated to users, thereby containing the propagation of incorrect information generated by primary LLM agents [30]. This creates a "safety net" that enhances the factual precision of final service reports. Similarly, in the high-stakes domain of outbreak analytics, the integration of specialized LLM-based agents into pipelines allows for multiple review steps before an analytical report is finalized. This formalized team interaction, where one agent's output becomes the input for a reviewing agent, provides more opportunities to catch inconsistencies and factual errors than direct prompting of a single LLM, leading to higher-quality, more reliable situational reports [4]. The core mechanism underpinning these improvements is the distributed cross-referencing capability. Reviewer agents can access different data modalities or knowledge repositories, enabling them to challenge assumptions, fill informational gaps, and flag contradictions that a single agent might overlook. This collaborative scrutiny ensures that the final synthesized report is not merely a compilation of data but a vetted, coherent, and factually robust document, thereby substantially elevating the reliability of AI-generated content in critical applications.

### 3.2 Improvement of Consistency and Standardization

A primary and observable improvement facilitated by reviewer agents in multi-agent collaborative systems is the significant enhancement of report consistency and standardization. In high-stakes domains where reports must be uniform, reproducible, and adhere to strict protocols, the formalized workflows and structured communication enforced by reviewer agents are instrumental. Unlike direct human or single-model generation, which can suffer from stylistic drift and terminological inconsistency, reviewer agents operationalize predefined standards. They achieve this by acting as custodians of format and terminology, systematically evaluating intermediate and final outputs against established templates and knowledge schemas. This function is critical in fields like public health and emergency management, where consistent reporting is foundational for coordinated action and longitudinal analysis. For instance, in outbreak analytics, the manual synthesis of diverse information sources often leads to bottlenecks and variability in report structure [4]. The integration of specialized LLM-based agents, including dedicated reviewer agents, formalizes team interactions to optimize workflows and enforce quality control, directly enhancing the consistency and reproducibility of situational reports [4].

The underlying mechanism for this improvement is twofold. First, reviewer agents often leverage or enforce standardized communication and service discovery protocols that govern inter-agent interactions. Frameworks like the proposed AgentDNS system aim to provide a root domain naming and service discovery layer, which, by creating a structured mechanism for service registration and semantic discovery, inherently promotes standardized data exchange formats and terminologies across heterogeneous agents [19]. This reduces ambiguities and ensures that information passed between a data retrieval agent, an analysis agent, and a reviewer agent maintains a consistent semantic structure. Second, reviewer agents are frequently architected to work within or enforce templated workflows and knowledge representations. In disaster management, for example, converting semi-structured Disaster Management Plans (DISPLANs) into a unified repository of knowledge units using agent-based analysis creates a standardized foundation for information retrieval and report generation [31]. A reviewer agent operating within such a system can validate that generated response reports correctly map to the formalized concepts and relationships within this repository, ensuring terminological precision and structural alignment with the official plan template.

The resultant benefits are profound. In service management, the application of AI Agents leads to improved service consistency by automating previously manual interaction processes [7]. A reviewer agent within such a service pipeline can ensure that customer interaction logs, incident reports, or performance summaries follow identical formatting rules and use approved classification codes, making them easily comparable and analyzable at scale. This standardization is equally vital in scientific and operational contexts. The Microsoft Academic Services (MAS) platform, which employs AI for large-scale scholarly analysis, relies on consistent extraction and assembly of factoids into a knowledge graph to ensure the reliability of its outputs [32]. A reviewer agent in a similar multi-agent research synthesis system would enforce consistency in how data points are extracted, coded, and reported, directly contributing to the reproducibility of the analytical findings. Ultimately, by institutionalizing standards through automated review checkpoints, reviewer agents transform ad-hoc, variable reporting into a streamlined, uniform, and highly reliable process.

### 3.3 Augmentation of Completeness and Contextual Depth

Reviewer agents play a pivotal role in augmenting the completeness and contextual depth of reports generated by multi-agent systems. This improvement is achieved primarily through two interconnected mechanisms: the systematic integration of multi-modal data sources and the intelligent orchestration of specialized knowledge agents. By ensuring that reports are not merely summaries but are deeply informed by a rich tapestry of relevant information, reviewer agents elevate the utility and reliability of the final output, particularly in complex, dynamic domains.

A key function of reviewer agents is to oversee the fusion of diverse data types—text, structured databases, sensor data, and imagery—into a coherent narrative. This process mitigates the risk of reports being based on incomplete or siloed information. For instance, in emergency medical settings, a comprehensive clinical decision requires integrating patient vitals, medical history, real-time symptoms, and pharmacological data. A reviewer agent within a multi-agent clinical decision support system can validate that all these data streams have been consulted and synthesized. The system described in [33] exemplifies this, where agents emulating a Triage Nurse, Emergency Physician, and Pharmacist contribute specialized perspectives. The reviewer agent (potentially embodied by the ED Coordinator role) ensures the final triage and treatment plan is comprehensive, checking for critical omissions in diagnosis, medication interactions, or resource needs, thereby producing a contextually deep report tailored to the specific emergency.

Furthermore, reviewer agents orchestrate the contributions of specialized knowledge agents to fill informational gaps and apply domain-specific reasoning. This is crucial in fields like agriculture, where effective planning depends on hyper-localized context. A reviewer agent can manage a workflow where one agent analyzes soil sensor data, another consults pest management ontologies, and a third retrieves market forecasts. By coordinating these agents, the reviewer ensures the final agricultural advisory report is complete, addressing not just immediate crop health but also long-term sustainability and economic factors. The importance of integrating such diverse, context-specific knowledge is highlighted in participatory agricultural approaches [34]. ### 3.4 Gains in Generation Efficiency and Scalability

The integration of reviewer agents within multi-agent collaborative systems demonstrably enhances the efficiency and scalability of report generation processes. A primary mechanism for this improvement is the automation of manual review tasks. In traditional workflows, human experts are required to meticulously check reports for errors, consistency, and completeness, which constitutes a significant bottleneck. Reviewer agents automate these evaluative functions, performing continuous validation and cross-referencing at computational speeds. This automation directly reduces the operational workload on human personnel. Empirical evidence from service innovation contexts indicates that AI agents can reduce operational workload by up to 40% and accelerate service response time by up to 60% [7]. In the domain of incident management, the automation of root cause analysis (RCA)—a critical and time-intensive component of incident reports—through LLM-based agents presents significant time savings for engineers [38]. By dynamically collecting diagnostic information via retrieval tools, these agents perform RCA without constant human intervention, thereby streamlining the overall incident reporting pipeline.

Furthermore, the multi-agent architecture inherently enables parallel processing and optimized workflows, which are key to scalability. Unlike linear, human-dependent processes, a collaborative system can deploy specialized agents—including dedicated reviewers—to operate concurrently on different sub-tasks of report generation. For instance, while one agent drafts a section, a reviewer agent can simultaneously validate the factual claims of a previously completed section, and another agent can ensure formatting consistency. This parallelization drastically cuts down the total generation time. The formalized interactions between LLM-based agents are noted to optimize workflows, enhance consistency, and allow multiple tasks to be performed simultaneously and at scale [4]. This capability is crucial in high-pressure domains like outbreak analytics, where rapid situational updates are required. Similarly, in operational environments, the use of multi-agent systems for optimization and load balancing demonstrates how decentralized coordination can improve resource utilization and reduce service execution delays, principles that directly translate to more efficient report generation workflows [39].

The scalability gains are also evident in the ability of reviewer agents to manage increasing volumes and complexity of data without a proportional increase in human labor. As systems grow, the reviewer agent's role in orchestrating knowledge integration from diverse sources—such as logs, metrics, and databases—ensures that the report generation process does not degrade in speed or quality [38]. This is complemented by the learning and memory capabilities of advanced agents, which allow them to become more effective over time, further optimizing the workflow for subsequent reporting cycles [4]. Consequently, organizations can scale their reporting operations to meet fluctuating demands, such as during major incidents or periodic financial reporting cycles, while maintaining high quality and timeliness [10].

### 3.5 Enhancement of Adaptability and Dynamic Responsiveness

The integration of reviewer agents imbued with learning capabilities and real-time data integration fundamentally enhances the adaptability and dynamic responsiveness of report generation systems. This allows for outputs that are not static documents but living artifacts that can be continuously updated and tailored to rapidly evolving operational contexts. In critical domains where situational awareness is paramount, this capability transforms reports from retrospective summaries into proactive decision-support tools. Reviewer agents achieve this by orchestrating continuous data streams, applying context-aware reasoning, and initiating iterative refinement cycles without requiring manual re-triggering.

In network management and disaster response, reviewer agents demonstrate significant value by integrating live sensor data, model outputs, and situational reports to maintain report currency. For instance, in natural hazard assessment, LLM-driven agents can be embedded within geoscientific workflows to synthesize real-time data from hydrodynamic models, geospatial datasets, and policy documents [25]. A reviewer agent in such a system can continuously validate new model runs against incoming sensor data, update risk projections in an ongoing situational report, and highlight discrepancies that require expert attention. This creates a dynamically responsive reporting loop crucial for emergency command centers. Similarly, the conversion of static Disaster Management Plans (DISPLANs) into an active knowledge repository, facilitated by agent-based analysis, allows reviewer agents to dynamically map real-time incident data to relevant plan clauses and generate tailored action reports [31]. This moves beyond static document retrieval to context-aware report generation that adapts to the specific phase and scale of an unfolding disaster.

Within financial services, the need for real-time, adaptive reporting is acute due to volatile markets and evolving risk profiles. Reviewer agents within agentic AI frameworks enable autonomous and real-time credit risk decision-making by continuously absorbing new transaction data, market news, and borrower information [27]. A reviewer agent can monitor this pipeline, dynamically updating a borrower's risk assessment report to reflect new liabilities or market shocks, thereby providing lenders with a constantly current view rather than a snapshot from a periodic review. This concept of dynamic responsiveness extends to investment management, where LLM-based agents must adapt reports to swiftly changing financial contexts [10]. A reviewer agent could orchestrate specialized agents analyzing news sentiment, time-series data, and fundamental analysis, ensuring the final investment report synthesizes the latest information and revises prior conclusions based on new evidence.

The underlying mechanism enabling this adaptability often involves advanced policy composition and agent ensembles. Reviewer agents can request or assemble context-aware policies on-demand, allowing the report generation system to switch seamlessly between different operational contexts—such as shifting from routine monitoring to crisis response—without lengthy retraining [37]. Furthermore, by incorporating feedback loops and learning from historical interactions and corrections, reviewer agents improve their future review heuristics, making the overall system more adept at handling novel scenarios and reducing the need for manual intervention over time. This culminates in report generation systems that are not only reactive to real-time data but also proactively adaptive to new patterns and contexts, significantly enhancing their utility in dynamic, high-stakes environments.

## 4 Challenges and Ethical Considerations

### 4.1 Security Vulnerabilities and System Integrity Risks

The deployment of reviewer agents within multi-agent collaborative systems introduces a complex attack surface, raising significant security vulnerabilities and system integrity risks. These risks stem from the autonomous, interconnected nature of agents and their reliance on dynamic, often natural language-driven, workflows. A primary concern is the threat of adversarial attacks, where malicious inputs are crafted to manipulate an agent's reasoning or output. This is particularly acute for systems where reviewer agents assess and refine reports based on LLM-generated content. For instance, research on automated web vulnerability reproduction highlights that LLM agents exhibit high sensitivity to input guidance, with performance degrading significantly under incomplete or manipulated information [40]. This susceptibility suggests that reviewer agents could be similarly misled by poisoned data or adversarial prompts, leading to the validation of erroneous or malicious content within a final report, thereby compromising its factual correctness.

Furthermore, the multi-agent architecture itself creates vectors for unauthorized access and privilege escalation. The flexible, prompt-driven behavior of LLM agents introduces non-deterministic execution paths that can be exploited. As noted in security analyses, an attacker could potentially hijack the communication flow between agents to escalate privileges and gain unauthorized access to sensitive data or system functions [41]. This risk is amplified in systems handling confidential information, such as financial or healthcare reports. The need for authenticated delegation is critical, as unauthorized agent actions can lead to data breaches. Frameworks for authenticated and authorized AI agents emphasize that without clear chains of accountability and robust access control, agents acting on behalf of users could perform inappropriate actions, accessing or exfiltrating sensitive data contained within reports [20].

Data breaches also pose a substantial risk, arising from insecure inter-agent communication and data storage. Standardized communication frameworks aim to enhance interoperability but must be designed with security as a core principle. Insecure APIs or protocols can become points of data leakage. The integration of multiple specialized agents, each potentially accessing different data sources (e.g., knowledge bases, real-time feeds), increases the number of endpoints that must be secured. A compromise in any single agent or communication channel within the collaborative chain—from data retrieval to final review—can jeopardize the confidentiality and integrity of the entire report generation process. Therefore, ensuring system integrity requires a holistic security approach encompassing adversarial robustness, strict access control, and secure communication protocols to protect the multi-stage workflow that reviewer agents oversee.

### 4.2 Transparency, Explainability, and Trust Deficits

The deployment of reviewer agents within multi-agent collaborative systems introduces significant challenges concerning transparency, explainability, and the consequent erosion of user trust. A core issue stems from the inherent opacity of the complex decision-making processes employed by these AI agents, particularly when they are built upon large language models (LLMs) or intricate multi-agent reasoning pipelines. The "black-box" nature of such systems makes it difficult for human users, including domain experts and end-users, to understand *how* a reviewer agent arrived at a specific critique, validation, or refinement suggestion for a report. This lack of interpretability directly undermines trust, as stakeholders cannot verify the reasoning chain or identify potential flaws in the agent's logic. As highlighted in research on conversational systems, users express a clear need for a clearer understanding of how LLMs process information and make decisions, with perceived opacity hindering trust, especially in sensitive applications [29]. This trust deficit is critically amplified in high-stakes domains like healthcare, where reviewer agents are tasked with validating clinical reports or diagnostic suggestions. Without transparent and explainable operations, healthcare professionals may be reluctant to rely on agent-generated feedback, potentially negating the system's intended benefits [6].

The challenge is further compounded in multi-agent settings where a reviewer agent's output is the result of interactions with other specialized agents (e.g., data retrieval agents, analysis agents). Tracing responsibility and understanding the collaborative workflow becomes exponentially more difficult. While frameworks like Multi-Agent-Based Decision Support Systems (MADSS) coordinate various agent types, the complexity of their interactions often lacks built-in mechanisms for comprehensive explanation generation [1]. This opacity can lead to a cascade of negative effects. In conversational customer service, for instance, a lack of transparency in how a reviewer agent assesses and improves response quality can frustrate users and damage the perceived reliability of the service [42]. Furthermore, in contexts requiring rigorous audit trails, such as intelligence analysis or outbreak analytics, the inability to explain a reviewer agent's modifications to a report compromises accountability and reproducibility [4]. Addressing these deficits requires the development of integrated explainability modules and transparency agents within the system architecture, which can articulate the "why" behind agent decisions in a human-comprehensible manner, thereby rebuilding the essential foundation of trust for effective human-agent collaboration.

### 4.3 Algorithmic Bias and Fairness Concerns

The integration of reviewer agents into multi-agent collaborative systems introduces significant risks related to algorithmic bias and fairness, which can systematically propagate into final reports and lead to discriminatory outcomes. These biases often originate from the training data and the architectural design of the underlying agents. For instance, if the language models powering these agents are trained on historical datasets containing societal prejudices or skewed representations, the agents may inadvertently learn and replicate these patterns. In a multi-agent review process, a reviewer agent tasked with validation might fail to correct, or even reinforce, biased assertions generated by a primary reporting agent, thereby institutionalizing unfairness in the final document. This is particularly perilous in high-stakes domains where reports directly influence critical decisions.

In financial applications, biased reviewer agents could produce investment or credit reports that systematically disadvantage certain demographic groups. Research on [10] highlights the transformative potential of LLMs in finance but also implicitly underscores the need to scrutinize the data and methodologies behind these models to prevent perpetuating existing inequities. Similarly, in inventory management, an agentic system might generate operational reports that reflect suboptimal or biased ordering decisions. The study [43] provides direct empirical evidence, revealing that LLM agents exhibit human-like decision-making biases, such as the pull-to-centre effect, in inventory replenishment scenarios. A reviewer agent that lacks mechanisms to detect and mitigate these cognitive biases would allow flawed reasoning to be presented as a finalized, authoritative report, potentially exacerbating supply chain inefficiencies like the bullwhip effect.

The healthcare domain presents perhaps the most sensitive context for such biases. Reviewer agents in clinical support systems that assess diagnostic or treatment reports risk amplifying biases present in non-representative medical training data. This could lead to reports with varying accuracy or recommendations across different patient populations, directly impacting care quality and equity. The framework proposed in [44] categorizes applications and identifies fundamental limitations like lack of real-world understanding, which can be a conduit for bias. If a reviewer agent operates without sufficient oversight (Category 1 in the framework), biased outputs may reach patients or providers unchecked. Furthermore, the survey on [6] discusses architectural patterns and the imperative for clinical validation, which must include rigorous bias auditing to ensure fairness.

Addressing these concerns requires a multi-faceted approach. It involves curating diverse and representative training datasets, implementing bias detection and mitigation modules within the reviewer agent's evaluation loop, and ensuring human oversight, especially for reports impacting marginalized groups. Without such safeguards, the deployment of reviewer agents risks automating and scaling discrimination under a veneer of objective, systematic review.

### 4.4 Data Privacy and Confidentiality Risks

The deployment of reviewer agents within multi-agent collaborative systems inherently amplifies data privacy and confidentiality risks due to the extensive and often sensitive data required for their operation. These risks permeate the entire data lifecycle—from initial collection and processing to inter-agent sharing and final output generation. In high-stakes domains like healthcare, where reviewer agents are employed for tasks such as diagnostic support and report generation [6], the systems must handle protected health information (PHI). The autonomous coordination and evidence integration performed by these agents create multiple points of vulnerability where PHI could be exposed through insecure communication channels, unauthorized access by a compromised agent, or improper logging within the collaborative workflow. The survey on agentic RAG systems highlights that deploying such technology in resource-limited settings further complicates ensuring robust data protection [6].

In customer service and retail contexts, the interaction between AI agents and consumers generates vast amounts of personal data. Research indicates that while consumers may express lesser immediate privacy concerns with AI agents compared to human agents [45], this perceived lower risk does not eliminate the actual technical vulnerabilities. The very architecture enabling multi-agent collaboration—featuring service discovery, inter-agent communication protocols, and shared knowledge bases—can become a vector for data breaches if not meticulously secured. The data practices of these collaborative systems, often opaque to users, necessitate transparent management. This is underscored by work on LLM-based agents designed to parse privacy policies, which demonstrates the complexity of data usage even in systems built to enhance user privacy awareness [46]. The agent's own need to process policy text and user queries introduces another layer of data processing that must be safeguarded.

Furthermore, the fundamental mechanisms that empower reviewer agents, such as retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) and continuous learning from feedback loops, pose significant confidentiality challenges. A RAG system's external knowledge retrieval could inadvertently leak query context or internal data. Feedback cycles used for refinement may store sensitive corrections or user interactions. Studies on conversational agents reveal that user trust is heavily influenced by perceptions of data security and transparency in how information is processed [29]. The opacity of multi-agent decision-making complicates providing users with clear explanations of data flows, potentially violating principles of data minimization and purpose limitation. Moreover, as noted in broader analyses of AI systems, the large-scale data collection required for training and operating these agents carries inherent risks of misuse, re-identification, and profiling [47]. Therefore, mitigating these risks requires a holistic approach combining technical safeguards like federated learning or homomorphic encryption, rigorous access controls within the agent network, and ethical governance frameworks that enforce privacy-by-design principles across the entire multi-agent architecture.

### 4.5 Coordination Failures and Operational Inconsistencies

Coordination failures and operational inconsistencies represent a critical class of technical limitations that can severely degrade the performance of multi-agent collaborative systems and, consequently, undermine the reliability of the final reports they produce. These issues stem from inherent complexities in orchestrating autonomous agents, which must reliably communicate, align their tasks, and adapt to dynamic environments. A primary challenge lies in the reliability of inter-agent communication protocols. While frameworks like the agent-to-agent protocol aim to standardize interactions [19], the practical implementation of these protocols in complex, real-world scenarios can be fragile. Breakdowns in message delivery, misinterpretation of shared context, or latency in synchronization can lead to agents operating on outdated or conflicting information, directly introducing errors and inconsistencies into the collaborative report generation process. This is corroborated by research in service-oriented architectures, where the accuracy of responding in cross-platform communications is a fundamental concern, and mathematical modeling of coordination mechanisms is required to validate system interactions [21].

Furthermore, the challenge of environment adaptation poses a significant risk to operational consistency. Reviewer agents often depend on a stable and correctly configured operational environment to access tools, data, and other agents. However, as highlighted in studies on automated vulnerability reproduction, agents can fail dramatically when faced with complex, multi-component environments requiring specific configurations or authentication [40]. An agent tasked with validating data or code within a report may produce incorrect assessments if it cannot properly interface with the necessary external databases or simulation platforms, leading to a false sense of security in the report's conclusions. This gap between an agent's perceived capability and its actual operational effectiveness in a dynamic setting is a major source of inconsistency.

Coordination failures also manifest in task decomposition and workflow alignment. In systems employing multi-agent collaborative planning [16], a misalignment in sub-task dependencies or a failure in handoff procedures between specialized agents (e.g., a retrieval agent and an evaluation agent) can result in incomplete analyses or the propagation of unchecked errors through the pipeline. Without robust fault-tolerance and rollback mechanisms, such a failure can corrupt the entire report generation workflow. Ultimately, these technical coordination shortcomings directly translate to observable deficits in report quality, including internal contradictions, missing sections due to dropped tasks, or conclusions based on a partial and unintegrated view of the available evidence. Ensuring reliable coordination is therefore not merely an engineering concern but a foundational prerequisite for trustworthy automated report generation.

### 4.6 Ethical Governance and Accountability Gaps

The deployment of reviewer agents within multi-agent collaborative systems introduces profound ethical governance and accountability gaps that current organizational and legal frameworks are ill-equipped to handle. A primary concern is the ambiguity surrounding liability when a reviewer agent, or the collective system it operates within, produces a flawed report leading to adverse outcomes. The distributed and often opaque nature of decision-making across multiple autonomous agents complicates the attribution of responsibility, creating a "responsibility gap" where neither the developers, the deploying organization, nor the AI agents themselves can be clearly held accountable. This challenge is exacerbated by the lack of robust, standardized governance frameworks specifically designed for agentic systems. As highlighted in the context of customer service, a sustainable model requires a transition toward frameworks like "Human-in-Command" (HIC), which aim to preserve human dignity and decisional ownership by mandating meaningful human oversight as a systemic property, not merely a technical feature [28]. The HIC framework underscores that effective governance must integrate interaction design, cognitive load management, and psychological trust to maintain organizational accountability and ethical alignment.

Furthermore, achieving this oversight is contingent on sufficient transparency into agent operations. The current lack of "visibility" — information about where, why, how, and by whom AI agents are used — represents a critical governance deficit [48]. Without measures such as agent identifiers, real-time monitoring, and comprehensive activity logging, it becomes impossible to audit reviewer agents' decisions, understand their coordination failures, or detect emergent biases. This opacity directly undermines the trust necessary for human reviewers to effectively exercise their oversight role. The problem is particularly acute in high-stakes domains like healthcare, where next-generation agentic AI promises transformative benefits but also introduces significant ethical and regulatory challenges that demand interdisciplinary collaboration to address [5]. Similarly, in outbreak analytics, while LLM-based agents offer efficiency gains, the development of necessary governance structures and oversight is explicitly noted as a prerequisite to prevent misuse and unintended consequences [4]. These gaps collectively point to an urgent need for the co-development of technical visibility measures and ethical governance protocols that clearly define liability, enforce human oversight triggers, and ensure the entire multi-agent system remains aligned with human values and societal norms.

### 4.7 Societal and Psychological Impacts

Beyond the immediate technical and operational challenges, the deployment of reviewer agents within multi-agent collaborative systems poses profound societal and psychological risks that demand careful scrutiny. A primary concern is the potential for these agentic systems to exacerbate epistemic instability, particularly among vulnerable populations. As highlighted in [49], AI agents designed to maximize engagement and affirmation may inadvertently mirror, validate, or amplify delusional or grandiose content. In the context of reviewer agents, which are tasked with evaluating and refining reports, this risk translates into a potential feedback loop where flawed or biased informational outputs are systematically reinforced rather than corrected, blurring the boundaries between reliable and unreliable information. This dynamic can undermine collective trust in information systems and erode the shared factual foundations necessary for societal decision-making.

Furthermore, the increasing delegation of complex tasks to autonomous agents raises critical questions about human agency and decision-making autonomy. The pervasive integration of reviewer agents could lead to automation complacency, where human operators over-rely on automated assessments, diminishing their critical oversight and analytical skills. This deskilling effect is particularly perilous in high-stakes domains where final accountability remains human. The paper [48] underscores that increased delegation may exacerbate societal risks, emphasizing the need for governance structures that preserve human oversight. Moreover, the interaction patterns enforced by multi-agent systems could subtly shape human behavior and organizational processes, potentially privileging efficiency and standardization over nuanced judgment and ethical deliberation. For instance, in financial services, while agentic systems promise enhanced decision-making [27], they also risk creating opaque decision pathways that users feel powerless to question or override, thus negatively influencing human autonomy.

The psychological impact extends to the workplace dynamics and trust within professional ecosystems. As Multi-Agent Based Decision Support Systems (MADSS) become more prevalent, the traditional roles of human experts may be reconfigured, potentially leading to job displacement anxieties or a devaluation of human expertise. The dynamics of trust and opinion propagation, as discussed in [50], become crucial; if the "decisions" of reviewer agents are not transparent or are perceived as biased, it can fracture trust within teams and organizations. Ultimately, mitigating these societal and psychological impacts requires a sociotechnical approach that prioritizes human-in-command paradigms, invests in digital literacy, and designs systems that augment rather than replace human cognitive and social capacities.

## 5 Future Directions and Conclusion

### 5.1 Standardized Validation and Benchmarking Methodologies

A critical frontier for advancing reviewer agents in multi-agent collaborative systems is the development of robust, standardized validation and benchmarking methodologies. The current landscape lacks comprehensive, domain-specific frameworks capable of holistically assessing the nuanced dimensions of report quality that reviewer agents are designed to enhance, such as factual consistency, reasoning transparency, and robustness against adversarial inputs. Future research must prioritize creating multi-faceted benchmarks that move beyond simple accuracy metrics to evaluate the entire collaborative process and its outputs. This is particularly urgent in high-stakes domains like outbreak analytics, where the integration of specialized LLM-based agents promises efficiency gains but requires rigorous validation against established benchmarks to ensure reliability and prevent erroneous conclusions [4]. Similarly, in clinical decision support, while multi-agent systems show improved prediction accuracy, the need for consistent and transparent evaluation of their collaborative reasoning processes remains a key challenge [12].

These benchmarks should be multi-dimensional, assessing not only the final report's factual correctness but also the traceability and explainability of the inter-agent review process. Frameworks like Agent-Diff, which evaluate agentic LLMs on real-world API tasks via code execution with a state-diff-based contract, offer a promising direction by separating process from outcome and providing a controlled, sandboxed environment for evaluation [51]. This approach could be extended and specialized for report-generation systems. For instance, validation frameworks must include stress tests for adversarial robustness, probing how reviewer agent coordination withstands attempts to inject misinformation or manipulate communication channels—a concern highlighted in discussions of security vulnerabilities in multi-agent networks [52]. Furthermore, benchmarks should measure consistency and standardization enforcement, quantifying how effectively reviewer agents apply formalized workflows, such as those defined by Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs), to produce uniform outputs across different instances and operators [53].

Ultimately, the creation of such standardized methodologies is foundational for building trust, facilitating regulatory compliance, and enabling the fair comparison of different architectural paradigms. It will provide the empirical grounding needed to move from promising prototypes to reliable, deployable systems that demonstrably enhance report quality through measurable improvements in accuracy, transparency, and resilience.

### 5.2 Advanced Human-Agent Collaboration Paradigms

The evolution of reviewer agents within multi-agent collaborative systems necessitates a parallel advancement in human-agent collaboration paradigms. Moving beyond simplistic human-in-the-loop models, future research must focus on developing sophisticated "Human-in-Command" (HIC) frameworks that strategically integrate human oversight to preserve accountability and enhance system performance [28]. These frameworks are not about constant human monitoring but about designing intelligent systems that recognize their own limitations and proactively solicit human judgment at critical junctures. A core research direction involves the formalization of strategic handoff protocols. These are dynamic mechanisms that determine when and how control should be transferred between autonomous agents and human reviewers. Triggers for handoffs must be context-aware, moving beyond simple confidence thresholds to include factors such as novelty detection, ethical ambiguity, potential for high-impact errors, or requests for human expertise as seen in collaborative cognitive architectures [54]. Developing standardized, interpretable signaling for these handoffs is crucial for seamless integration into collaborative workflows.

Furthermore, managing the cognitive load on human overseers is paramount for effective HIC systems. As reviewer agents handle more complex validation and refinement tasks, the interface must present distilled, actionable insights rather than raw data dumps. Research should explore adaptive interfaces that prioritize information based on the human's role, the criticality of the decision, and the current operational context. This aligns with findings on enhancing user experience in AI systems, where reducing cognitive load is directly linked to higher satisfaction and more effective oversight [29]. The ultimate goal is to preserve human decisional ownership, particularly in high-stakes domains. This requires architectures where humans retain final authority over consequential judgments, with AI agents serving as empowered, transparent advisors. Future paradigms must embed mechanisms for explainability and rationale provision, allowing humans to understand the agent's reasoning path before endorsing or overriding its suggestions. This approach, transitioning towards a "Humanity-in-the-Loop" model, ensures that technological augmentation enhances rather than diminishes human agency and ethical responsibility in the report generation lifecycle [28].
