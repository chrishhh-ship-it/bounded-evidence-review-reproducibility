# The Role of Evidence Agents in Multi-Agent AI Systems: A Survey on Architectural Independence versus Integration with Writer Agents

## 1 Introduction: Defining Evidence Agents and Writer Agents in AI Systems

### 1.1 The Rise of Agentic AI and Specialized Roles in Multi-Agent Systems

The paradigm shift towards agentic AI represents a fundamental evolution in artificial intelligence, moving beyond static models to dynamic systems where autonomous agents perform complex, goal-oriented tasks with a degree of independence. This shift is driven by the recognition that monolithic AI systems often struggle with adaptability, scalability, and handling multifaceted problems. In contrast, agentic AI frameworks decompose complex objectives into subtasks managed by specialized, collaborative entities. This approach is exemplified by the growing adoption of Multi-Agent Systems (MAS), where multiple intelligent agents interact within a shared environment to achieve common or individual goals, offering significant advantages in adaptability, flexibility, and scalability [1]. The rise of such systems is evident across diverse domains, from automated customer service, where multiple agents cooperate to manage complex, multi-turn dialogues [2], to industrial automation, where large language model (LLM)-based agents enable intelligent planning and control of flexible production processes [3].

Within these sophisticated MAS architectures, the principle of specialization has become paramount. Just as human teams divide labor based on expertise, agentic systems increasingly delegate specific functions to dedicated agent roles to optimize overall performance and reliability. This specialization mitigates the cognitive overload and error risks associated with single agents attempting to master all aspects of a complex workflow. For instance, in high-stakes domains like outbreak analytics, the synthesis of real-time, policy-relevant evidence requires the coordination of multiple distinct analytical techniques and data sources. Here, the use of specialized LLM-based agents formalizes team interactions, optimizes workflows, and enhances consistency and reproducibility, allowing for simultaneous task execution and more rigorous review steps [4]. Similarly, in networking, the vision for agentic AI in 6G involves creating an ecosystem of diverse autonomous agents that collaborate to improve performance and enable new services, moving beyond passive learning frameworks [5].

It is within this context of increasing complexity and demand for robust, trustworthy AI that specialized roles like evidence agents and writer agents have emerged. The evidence agent role crystallizes around the critical functions of information acquisition, validation, and synthesis—tasks that require rigorous attention to source credibility, traceability, and logical reasoning. Conversely, the writer agent specializes in narrative construction, content generation, and audience-aware communication. Their emergence is not incidental but a direct response to the need for systems that can not only generate outputs but also ground those outputs in verifiable information and communicate them effectively. This specialization reflects a broader architectural trend towards modularity, where agents with defined responsibilities interact through clear protocols, as seen in frameworks supporting context-aware policy composition [6] and those addressing authorization and accountability for autonomous agents [7]. The debate on whether these roles should remain architecturally independent or be functionally merged into a unified agent is thus rooted in this foundational shift towards agentic AI and the subsequent drive for role-based specialization within collaborative multi-agent ecosystems.

### 1.2 Core Definitions: Evidence Agents vs. Writer Agents

At the heart of the architectural debate lies the precise functional distinction between evidence agents and writer agents within multi-agent AI systems. An **evidence agent** is a specialized autonomous entity whose primary mandate is the rigorous management of information to support fact-based reasoning. Its core functions are centered on the acquisition, validation, synthesis, and curation of data from diverse sources. This involves targeted information retrieval from structured databases or unstructured corpora, critical assessment of source credibility and relevance, and the synthesis of multi-modal evidence into a coherent, traceable knowledge base. The agent's output is not final narrative content but rather validated, structured evidence intended to inform downstream decision-making or content creation processes. This role is crucial in domains requiring high-stakes, auditable decisions, such as in agentic systems for medical decision support [8] or in generating robust workflows for climate services [9]. The design of such agents often incorporates mechanisms for maintaining provenance and enabling evidence-based reasoning, positioning them as foundational modules for trust and accuracy.

In contrast, a **writer agent** is a specialized entity tasked with the generation, structuring, and communication of content. Its core responsibilities involve transforming inputs—which may include raw data, synthesized evidence, or high-level instructions—into coherent, stylistically appropriate narratives. This encompasses tasks such as summarization, report generation, narrative structuring, and the adaptation of tone, style, and complexity for specific audiences. The writer agent's strength lies in linguistic fluency, rhetorical organization, and the ability to craft compelling communications from provided information. Its role is prominently featured in systems designed to automate analytical reporting, as seen in pipelines for outbreak analytics where generating situational updates is a key bottleneck [4], or in creating developer-aligned test scenarios in software engineering [10].

The fundamental divergence is thus between *evidence handling* and *content generation*. While an evidence agent is concerned with the epistemological rigor of the information substrate—asking "what is known and how do we know it?"—a writer agent focuses on the rhetorical effectiveness of the derived narrative—asking "how is this knowledge best communicated?" This distinction underpins the entire debate on architectural separation, as the cognitive processes, required competencies, and failure modes for ensuring factual fidelity versus narrative coherence are significantly different.

### 1.3 The Architectural Debate: Independence versus Integration

The architectural design of evidence agents within multi-agent AI systems presents a fundamental and consequential debate: should these agents exist as architecturally independent modules, or should their functions be functionally merged into writer agents? This question is central to the design of robust, trustworthy, and efficient agentic systems. Proponents of architectural independence argue that a dedicated evidence agent, as a specialized entity focused solely on information retrieval, validation, and synthesis, is essential for ensuring the integrity and traceability of the knowledge base upon which decisions and narratives are built. This separation of concerns aligns with foundational principles of modularity and specialization in Multi-Agent Systems, as discussed in literature on [1]. Such a design enhances system transparency, allows for rigorous validation processes, and is particularly critical in high-stakes domains where evidence quality directly impacts outcomes, such as in [4]. An independent evidence agent can act as a verifiable source of truth, mitigating risks associated with hallucinations or uncorroborated data being passed to the writer agent responsible for content generation.

Conversely, the integrationist perspective advocates for merging evidence-handling capabilities directly into the writer agent to create a more unified and streamlined architecture. This approach is often motivated by desires for operational efficiency, reduced latency in task execution, and simplified agent coordination. In applications where speed and workflow optimization are paramount, such as in dynamic [2], a single, more capable agent might be preferable to manage the end-to-end process from information gathering to response formulation. Integration can minimize communication overhead and potentially allow for more nuanced, context-aware generation where evidence curation and narrative construction are deeply intertwined.

The implications of this architectural choice are profound, extending beyond mere software design to impact system performance, scalability, security, and governance. An independent model may offer superior auditability and facilitate compliance with regulatory standards by clearly delineating the evidence pipeline, a concern highlighted in studies on user trust like [11]. However, it may introduce complexity and coordination challenges. A merged architecture, while potentially more efficient, risks creating opaque, monolithic agents where evidence validation steps are obscured, compromising transparency and making error diagnosis difficult. This debate is therefore not merely technical but touches on core requirements of the application domain, whether it demands the rigorous, traceable evidence handling of a clinical decision support system or the rapid, adaptive response of a network orchestration agent as envisioned in [12]. This survey will systematically analyze these competing viewpoints, their theoretical underpinnings, and their practical manifestations across diverse domains.

## 2 Theoretical and Architectural Foundations

### 2.1 Foundational Theories of Specialization in Multi-Agent Systems

The foundational theories underpinning the design of multi-agent systems (MAS) strongly advocate for the specialization of agent roles, a principle that directly informs the debate on whether evidence agents should exist independently from writer agents. At its core, MAS theory posits that complex problems are best solved by a collective of autonomous, specialized entities that collaborate, rather than by a single, monolithic intelligence [1]. This approach leverages the core MAS advantages of adaptability, flexibility, and scalability, as heterogeneous agents can be designed with distinct capabilities to handle specific sub-tasks within a larger workflow. The principle of specialization is deeply intertwined with the concepts of modularity and autonomy. Modularity allows for the decomposition of a system into well-defined, interchangeable components—or agents—each encapsulating specific knowledge and functions. This separation of concerns simplifies design, enhances maintainability, and facilitates system evolution, as individual agents can be updated or replaced without overhauling the entire architecture. Autonomy grants each specialized agent the authority to control its own actions and internal state, enabling it to operate proactively towards its designated goals without continuous external direction. This is particularly critical for evidence agents, which must independently navigate information landscapes, validate sources, and synthesize findings.

These theoretical principles are operationalized through role-based design, a prevalent paradigm in MAS where agents are assigned specific roles that define their responsibilities, permissions, and interaction protocols. For instance, a typical Multi-Agent-Based Decision Support System (MADSS) might comprise distinct user agents, decision agents, and knowledge agents, each fulfilling a specialized function within the collaborative decision-making process [1]. This role-based specialization is not merely an organizational convenience but a response to cognitive and practical limits. It reduces the cognitive load on any single agent, minimizes the risk of error propagation across disparate functions, and allows for deeper optimization within a narrow domain. The theoretical justification extends into decision support systems (DSS), where the integration of specialized knowledge sources is paramount for informed outcomes. As seen in clinical domains, a multi-agent ontologies-based system employs agents specialized in different fields of knowledge—such as semiology or pharmacology—each operating its own knowledge base and ontological framework [16]. This modular, specialized approach is deemed suitable for realizing robust MADSS across many areas, underscoring the theoretical argument that complex, evidence-intensive decision-making benefits from dedicated agents focused solely on information acquisition, validation, and reasoning, thereby providing a solid foundation for considering evidence agents as architecturally independent entities.

### 2.2 Architectural Patterns for Evidence-Centric Agent Design

The architectural independence of evidence agents is not merely a theoretical preference but is substantiated by several recurring design patterns in multi-agent AI systems. These patterns systematically position evidence agents as distinct, specialized entities within larger workflows, emphasizing their unique role in information acquisition, validation, and synthesis. A prominent pattern emerges from agentic Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems, particularly in high-stakes domains. For instance, in medical decision support, architectures often delineate a clear separation between agents responsible for evidence retrieval and validation and those handling report generation and communication [8]. This separation is crucial for ensuring clinical guidelines are adhered to and that decision-making frameworks remain interpretable and auditable. The evidence agent in such a design operates as a dedicated module that queries knowledge graphs, assesses source credibility, and synthesizes multi-modal data before passing curated evidence to a writer agent for narrative construction.

Another foundational pattern is derived from knowledge management frameworks for multi-agent systems. Here, the architecture explicitly defines specialized knowledge or information agents whose sole function is to manage, retrieve, and serve validated knowledge to other agents within the enterprise [17]. This design promotes scalability, reusability, and system adaptability by allowing the evidence-handling logic to be updated independently of the content generation modules. The automatic inter-agent communication protocols in such systems facilitate efficient knowledge dissemination, where an evidence agent acts as a centralized or federated source of truth.

Furthermore, the MAPE-K (Monitor-Analyze-Plan-Execute over a shared Knowledge base) control loop, a staple in autonomic computing, provides a robust architectural template that inherently supports evidence-centric agents. In complex network management scenarios, such as in Space-Air-Ground Integrated Networks (SAGIN), specific agents are designed as semantic resource perceivers and analyzers [18]. These agents continuously monitor the environment, analyze data, and populate a shared knowledge base with evidence about network states and intents. This evidence is then utilized by separate planner and executor agents. This pattern enforces a clear separation of concerns: evidence agents handle the "Monitor" and "Analyze" phases, focusing on data fidelity and situational awareness, while other agents manage planning and execution. Similarly, in proposed future Internet architectures like FlexNGIA 2.0, LLM-based agents are designed to autonomously perceive and reason about network conditions, effectively functioning as evidence agents that feed into decision-making processes for protocol design and traffic engineering [12]. These patterns collectively demonstrate that architecturally distinct evidence agents are a recurrent solution for ensuring rigor, traceability, and adaptability in complex, dynamic AI systems.

### 2.3 Domain-Specific Motivations for Architectural Independence

The architectural independence of evidence agents is not merely a design preference but a critical requirement driven by the unique demands of various high-stakes domains. These domains necessitate specialized agents dedicated to evidence handling due to stringent needs for rigorous validation, comprehensive traceability, and the management of complex, often multimodal, data streams. In healthcare, for instance, the consequences of erroneous information are profound. Agentic AI systems designed for diagnostics and clinical decision support must integrate and validate evidence from diverse sources such as medical records, lab results, and research literature [8]. A dedicated evidence agent ensures this validation process is systematic, traceable, and adheres to clinical guidelines, thereby mitigating risks like model "hallucinations" that could lead to patient harm [19]. The need for architectural separation is further underscored in outbreak analytics, where rapid, evidence-based responses are vital. Specialized agents can formalize workflows for synthesizing information from multiple distinct analytical techniques and sources, enhancing consistency, reproducibility, and quality control before narrative reporting [4].

Similarly, in technical domains like network management and root cause analysis (RCA) of software incidents, the complexity of diagnostic data necessitates an independent evidence-gathering module. RCA requires dynamically collecting and correlating diagnostic information from logs, metrics, and databases, a task ill-suited to a unified writer agent focused on narrative generation [20]. An independent evidence agent can systematically retrieve and validate this technical evidence, ensuring factual accuracy for subsequent analysis. This pattern extends to business and customer service operations, where governance frameworks like "Human-in-Command" (HIC) emphasize accountability and auditability [21]. A separate evidence agent creates a clear audit trail for data sourcing and decision logic, which is essential for compliance and ethical oversight. Furthermore, the principles of Multi-Agent Based Decision Support Systems (MADSS) highlight how specialized knowledge agents improve decision-making in complex environments by analyzing vast amounts of data under various criteria [1]. This functional specialization, where evidence agents act as dedicated knowledge curators, is crucial for maintaining system robustness, trust, and safety across healthcare, legal, financial, and operational domains, compelling their architectural independence from writer agents.

### 2.4 Knowledge Representation and Integration Mechanisms

The architectural independence of evidence agents is fundamentally enabled by sophisticated knowledge representation and integration mechanisms. These technical components allow evidence agents to operate as specialized modules for retrieving, synthesizing, and integrating evidence before it is passed to writer agents for narrative construction. A cornerstone of this capability is the use of structured knowledge representations, such as knowledge graphs and ontologies, which provide a semantic framework for organizing and querying complex information. For instance, systems like Microsoft Academic Services (MAS) leverage knowledge graphs assembled through natural language understanding and knowledge-assisted inference to capture scholarly communications at scale [22]. This structured representation allows an independent evidence agent to perform precise, context-aware retrieval and trace the provenance of information, a critical function for validation. Similarly, in clinical decision support, multi-agent systems utilize medical ontologies to enable cooperation between specialized agents operating on heterogeneous knowledge bases, ensuring semantic alignment and rigorous reasoning [16]. The Climate Service Recipes (HACID-CSR) system further exemplifies this, employing a domain knowledge graph for climate services (CS-DKG) to guide specialized agents in retrieving relevant subgraphs, thereby filling knowledge gaps and enhancing the traceability of generated workflows [9].

Beyond static knowledge structures, integration mechanisms like federated learning support evidence agents in synthesizing insights from distributed, privacy-sensitive data sources without centralizing raw data. This is particularly vital in domains like healthcare and network management, where evidence must be curated from disparate, secure repositories. While not explicitly detailed in all provided papers, the principle aligns with the collaborative, decentralized learning approaches discussed for intelligent edge networks, where multi-agent systems coordinate to improve strategies without sharing raw data [23]. For an evidence agent, such mechanisms facilitate evidence synthesis from multiple federated nodes, preserving data confidentiality while building a comprehensive evidence base. Furthermore, the use of Graph Retrieval-Augmented Generation (GraphRAG), as seen in HACID-CSR, represents a key integration technique where an evidence agent combines retrieved subgraph context with an LLM's reasoning to generate enriched, evidence-backed content for subsequent stages [9]. These representation and integration mechanisms collectively empower evidence agents as independent entities capable of ensuring evidence quality, maintaining audit trails, and handling complex, multi-modal data, thereby justifying their architectural separation from writer agents focused on narrative fluency and style.

## 3 Functional Analysis: Core Responsibilities of Evidence and Writer Agents

### 3.1 Core Functions of Evidence Agents: Information Acquisition and Curation

Evidence agents are specialized modules within multi-agent AI systems whose primary mandate is the rigorous acquisition, validation, and curation of information to support evidence-based reasoning. Their core functions are distinct from content generation, focusing instead on building a reliable knowledge foundation. A primary responsibility is **targeted information retrieval**. Unlike simple search, this involves understanding the context and specific evidentiary needs of a task, querying diverse and often domain-specific databases, knowledge graphs, and real-time data streams. In complex domains like outbreak analytics, this function is critical for synthesizing information from many different sources to generate timely, policy-relevant evidence [4]. Architectures like Agentic Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems explicitly formalize this role, where dedicated agents are responsible for fetching and integrating relevant clinical or research data [8].

Following retrieval, **validation of source credibility** is a non-negotiable function. Evidence agents must assess the authority, timeliness, and potential biases of information sources. This is especially vital in high-stakes domains such as healthcare and legal analysis, where erroneous data can lead to severe consequences. The agent must cross-reference facts, check for consensus among reputable sources, and flag inconsistencies. This rigorous validation process is a key argument for architectural independence, as it requires a focused, systematic approach separate from the creative or narrative pressures of content generation. The need for such validation is underscored in discussions about the cautious integration of LLMs, where misinterpretation of data must be prevented [4].

A third critical function is the **synthesis of multi-modal evidence**. Information is rarely homogeneous; it may include structured data (e.g., databases, spreadsheets), unstructured text (e.g., research papers, news articles), and even images or sensor data. Evidence agents are tasked with integrating these disparate data types into a coherent, contextualized evidence package. This synthesis often involves reasoning across modalities, such as linking textual findings with numerical trends or visual indicators. Frameworks like Multi-Agent Based Decision Support Systems (MADSS) utilize specialized knowledge agents for this very purpose, analyzing vast amounts of data from various alternatives to inform decisions [1].

Finally, **maintaining traceability** is a foundational responsibility. For an AI system's outputs to be trustworthy and auditable, it must be possible to trace any claim or conclusion back to its source evidence. Evidence agents are responsible for meticulously logging the provenance of all retrieved information, the steps taken in validation and synthesis, and any transformations applied. This creates an audit trail that is crucial for debugging, improving system performance, and meeting regulatory or ethical standards in fields like finance and healthcare. This function directly supports transparency and accountability, which are harder to ensure if evidence handling is deeply entangled with narrative construction within a single, monolithic writer agent.

### 3.2 Core Functions of Writer Agents: Content Generation and Communication

Writer agents serve as the communicative and narrative engine within multi-agent AI systems, with their core responsibilities centered on transforming processed information into coherent, structured, and audience-appropriate content. Their primary duty is content generation, which encompasses a wide range of outputs from concise summaries and detailed analytical reports to comprehensive narratives and explanatory text. This involves not merely transcribing data but synthesizing insights into a logical flow, structuring arguments, and ensuring the final output is both informative and accessible. For instance, in contexts like outbreak analytics, writer agents are tasked with generating regular situational updates and policy briefs from complex, evolving data, a process that requires distilling technical findings into actionable intelligence for decision-makers [4]. The ability to autonomously produce such written material is a key driver for operational efficiency, as highlighted in studies showing AI agents can accelerate service response times significantly [14].

Beyond raw generation, a critical function is narrative structuring and communication adaptation. Writer agents must organize information according to established rhetorical patterns—such as problem-solution frameworks or executive summaries—and tailor the tone, style, and complexity of the content for specific audiences. This requires sophisticated natural language capabilities to shift from technical jargon suitable for domain experts to plain-language explanations for the general public or stakeholders. The effectiveness of this adaptation directly impacts user trust and the practical utility of the system's output. Furthermore, writer agents often engage in iterative refinement, potentially collaborating with other agents to polish drafts, ensure consistency, and align the message with overarching goals. In agentic systems designed for domains like network management or scientific research, the writer agent's role in clearly communicating designed protocols, analysis results, or system states is fundamental for human-in-the-loop oversight and actionable intelligence [12]. Ultimately, the writer agent bridges the gap between evidence-based reasoning and human comprehension, making it an indispensable component for knowledge dissemination and decision support within collaborative AI architectures.

### 3.3 Analysis of Functional Overlap and Synergy

While evidence agents and writer agents are defined by distinct primary functions—information acquisition/curation versus content generation/communication—their operational boundaries are not rigidly siloed. A critical analysis reveals significant functional overlap and interdependencies that necessitate tight coupling for effective task completion, particularly in complex, multi-step workflows. This overlap is most pronounced in tasks requiring iterative refinement, where evidence retrieval informs narrative structure, and narrative goals, in turn, guide subsequent evidence searches. For instance, in generating a comprehensive outbreak analytics report, the writer agent's initial draft may identify gaps in temporal data, prompting the evidence agent to perform targeted retrieval of time-series datasets [4]. Conversely, the evidence agent's synthesis of conflicting study results may require the writer agent to adapt its communication strategy to present nuanced conclusions clearly. This cyclical interaction exemplifies a synergistic feedback loop, where separation of roles does not imply isolation but rather enables a more disciplined and traceable collaboration.

The synergistic benefits of this collaboration are manifold. First, it enhances output quality and reliability. By having a dedicated evidence agent rigorously validate and synthesize information before passing it to the writer agent, the risk of the writer agent generating content based on unverified or hallucinated data is substantially reduced. This is crucial in domains like finance and legal analysis, where accuracy is paramount [13]. The multi-agent review process inherent in this separation adds a layer of quality control, as noted in the context of LLM-based agents improving consistency and reproducibility over direct prompting [4]. Second, this collaboration enables the handling of more complex tasks by decomposing them into specialized subtasks. A writer agent can focus on structuring a persuasive argument or tailoring language for a specific audience, while concurrently, an evidence agent mines relevant case law or financial precedents. This parallel processing, facilitated by clear interfaces between agents, can improve overall system efficiency and scalability for intricate problems.

However, this synergy relies on effective communication protocols and shared context. The overlap necessitates interfaces where evidence agents must not only pass raw data but also metadata concerning source credibility and uncertainty—information critical for the writer agent to appropriately qualify statements. Similarly, writer agents must communicate narrative constraints and audience needs to guide the evidence retrieval process. Architectures that formalize this interaction, such as those using shared knowledge bases or structured communication languages, are essential to realize the benefits. For example, in conversational agent systems, delegating intent recognition and context management to specialized agents allows for more robust dialogue [2]. The Manager-Customer-Service Dual-Agent paradigm further illustrates how shared, consistent knowledge bases enable reliable collaboration between agents with overlapping concerns in task execution [24]. Thus, the functional overlap does not argue for merging the agents into a monolithic entity but rather for designing sophisticated coordination mechanisms that leverage their specialized strengths while managing their necessary interdependencies.

### 3.4 Task Complexity and the Case for Specialization

The argument for maintaining evidence agents as architecturally independent entities from writer agents is strongly reinforced by an analysis of task complexity, stakes, and domain-specific requirements. Complex tasks, particularly those in high-stakes domains, impose significant cognitive demands that are best managed through deep specialization, thereby reducing error risks and system failure. The core premise is that the cognitive processes for rigorous evidence handling—involving retrieval, validation, and synthesis from heterogeneous sources—are fundamentally distinct from those required for effective narrative construction and communication. Merging these disparate functions into a single agent risks overwhelming its operational capacity, leading to cognitive overload, which can manifest as oversimplification, overlooked data inconsistencies, or logical fallacies in the final output. This separation is crucial because the failure modes differ: an evidence agent's error might involve sourcing incorrect or biased data, while a writer agent's error might involve misrepresenting valid evidence through poor structuring or ambiguous language. Independent specialization allows each agent type to develop and optimize its unique cognitive stack—evidence agents for critical evaluation and traceability, writer agents for rhetorical coherence and audience adaptation—without cross-contaminating their respective failure probabilities.

The necessity for this separation is vividly illustrated in domains like outbreak analytics and healthcare. In outbreak response, generating timely, policy-relevant evidence requires synthesizing multimodal data streams and applying diverse analytical techniques under extreme time pressure [4]. A unified agent attempting to both curate this complex evidence base and simultaneously draft a situational report would likely compromise on the depth of validation or the clarity of communication, with potentially severe public health consequences. Similarly, the application of domain-specific Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) necessitates agents that can meticulously follow complex, branching decision graphs for evidence-based actions, a function distinct from generating explanatory narratives [25]. Furthermore, in customer service and network management, the real-time, dynamic nature of tasks requires agents to coordinate interference or infer user intent from contextual behavior, processes that benefit from focused specialization rather than amalgamated responsibilities [2] [26].

Ultimately, architectural separation based on specialization directly addresses the trade-off between efficiency and robustness. While a merged agent might offer streamlined workflows for simple tasks, the cognitive load and error risks escalate non-linearly with task complexity. Maintaining independent evidence agents ensures a dedicated focus on the fidelity of the information foundation, which is a prerequisite for trustworthy content generation by writer agents. This division of labor not only enhances system reliability but also aligns with governance frameworks that require clear audit trails and validation checkpoints, particularly as emphasized in discussions on human oversight and trust in sensitive applications [21] [11].

## 4 Arguments for and against Merging Evidence Agents with Writer Agents

### 4.1 Efficiency and Workflow Optimization: The Case for Integration

A primary argument for architecturally merging evidence agents with writer agents centers on achieving significant gains in operational efficiency and streamlined workflows. Proponents of integration posit that combining the information retrieval, validation, and synthesis functions of an evidence agent with the narrative construction and communication functions of a writer agent into a single, unified agent can reduce systemic latency and automate task coordination, leading to faster end-to-end task completion. This is particularly compelling in domains where rapid response is critical. For instance, in outbreak analytics, the need for real-time evidence generation to inform public health policy creates major bottlenecks when workflows involve multiple manual, time-intensive steps [4]. A unified agent could, in theory, seamlessly transition from gathering and validating the latest epidemiological data to drafting a situational update report, eliminating the overhead of inter-agent communication and data serialization/deserialization. This direct integration mirrors the efficiency gains observed in broader AI Agent implementations, where automating service processes can accelerate response times substantially [14].

Furthermore, integration facilitates automated task coordination within a single cognitive architecture. Instead of requiring explicit orchestration protocols to manage handoffs between a separate evidence provider and a content writer, a merged agent can internally manage the sequence and dependencies of subtasks. This reduces the complexity of the overall system design and can minimize failures due to miscommunication or synchronization errors between autonomous entities. The workflow becomes a continuous, internalized process within the agent's reasoning loop. This concept aligns with architectures designed for autonomous systems, where a single intelligent agent orchestrates sequences of actions to accomplish a given task by directly interpreting available interfaces and knowledge [3]. In such a model, the distinction between "gathering evidence" and "reporting on it" blurs into a singular goal-directed process. The result is a potentially leaner system with fewer moving parts, where resource allocation for computation and memory can be dynamically optimized for the monolithic task at hand, rather than being statically partitioned between specialized modules. This architectural simplicity argues for integration as a path to more efficient and responsive agentic systems, especially for well-defined, sequential tasks where the evidence-to-narrative pipeline is linear and tightly coupled.

### 4.2 Scalability and System Performance: Benefits of a Unified Architecture

Integrating evidence agents and writer agents into a unified architectural module presents compelling advantages for enhancing system scalability and overall performance. A primary benefit lies in the dynamic allocation of computational and memory resources. In a merged architecture, a single agent entity can internally prioritize and shift resources between evidence-gathering subtasks (like data retrieval and validation) and content-generation subtasks (like narrative structuring and summarization) based on real-time demand, without the overhead of inter-agent communication protocols. This fluid internal resource management can lead to more efficient utilization of system capacity, particularly under variable workloads. For instance, during periods requiring heavy evidence synthesis, the agent can allocate more cycles to validation processes, and subsequently reallocate focus to narrative generation once the evidence base is solidified. This adaptability is crucial for scaling operations to handle large volumes of concurrent tasks, as seen in domains like automated customer service or large-scale data reporting [14].

Furthermore, a unified design inherently facilitates more streamlined parallel task execution. While specialized independent agents can operate in parallel, their collaboration requires explicit coordination, message passing, and synchronization, which introduces latency and potential bottlenecks. A merged agent, operating with a shared internal state and objective, can orchestrate evidence collection and content drafting phases in a tightly coupled, pipelined manner. This reduces the coordination overhead and can significantly decrease end-to-end task completion time. The efficiency gains from such an integrated workflow are highlighted in research on multi-agent systems for complex, dynamic environments, where minimizing communication latency is key to performance [1]. The potential for scalable, round-the-clock operation is also noted in contexts like outbreak analytics, where rapid, large-scale evidence synthesis and reporting are critical [4].

From a system performance perspective, integration can simplify optimization. Developers can fine-tune a single, more complex model or a cohesive set of sub-modules for the combined task, rather than optimizing two separate agents for interaction. This can lead to improvements in inference speed and reduce the system's aggregate memory footprint by eliminating redundant components. The concept of centralized learning for coordinated action, as discussed in multi-agent deep reinforcement learning frameworks for resource allocation, supports the performance benefits of a more unified control strategy [27]. Ultimately, by reducing architectural complexity and inter-agent dependencies, a unified evidence-writer agent can offer a more scalable and performant solution for applications where operational throughput and latency are paramount over the need for strict functional separation and audit trails.

### 4.3 Security, Privacy, and Governance Risks of Integration

Integrating evidence agents with writer agents into a unified architectural module introduces significant security, privacy, and governance risks that can undermine system integrity and trust. A primary concern is the heightened vulnerability to privilege escalation attacks. In a merged agent, the boundaries between evidence retrieval (which may require access to sensitive or high-privilege data sources) and content generation (which outputs to users) become blurred. This creates a larger attack surface where malicious inputs or prompt injections could trick the single agent into performing unauthorized actions or leaking confidential information retrieved during its evidence phase. Research on securing LLM agents highlights that their runtime behavior, determined by natural language prompts, is inherently non-deterministic and vulnerable to such exploitation [28]. A merged architecture complicates the implementation of robust guardrails, as opposed to an independent evidence agent which can be isolated and subjected to stricter input/output validation and access controls.

Furthermore, data confidentiality and privacy breaches become more likely. An independent evidence agent can be designed with a principle of least privilege, accessing only the necessary data for validation and synthesis before passing curated evidence to the writer. Merging the roles risks exposing all internal data flows and retrieved raw materials (which may contain personal or proprietary information) to the content generation module, increasing the chance of inadvertent disclosure in the final output. This is particularly critical in domains like healthcare or legal analysis. The need for clear authorization and audit trails is emphasized in frameworks for accountable AI agent deployment [7]. A unified agent obscures the provenance of information and the decision trail, reducing auditability. It becomes difficult to discern whether a factual claim in the generated text originated from a validated source or an unverified assumption made during the writing process. This lack of transparency hinders accountability and complicates compliance with regulatory standards requiring explainability and data governance. Therefore, architectural separation acts as a security and governance control, enabling better oversight, minimizing the risk of privilege escalation and data leaks, and preserving a verifiable chain of custody for evidence.

### 4.4 Trust, Transparency, and Validation in Critical Domains

The architectural independence of evidence agents is a critical enabler of user trust, system transparency, and rigorous validation, especially in high-stakes domains where errors can have severe consequences. In fields like healthcare and legal analysis, the credibility of an AI system's output is paramount. An independent evidence agent, dedicated to information retrieval, validation, and synthesis, creates a clear separation of concerns that facilitates auditability and error tracing. This separation allows for the explicit logging of evidence sources, the methods used for credibility assessment, and the reasoning chain from raw data to synthesized evidence. Such traceability is foundational for transparency, as it provides users and regulators with a verifiable path to understand how conclusions were reached, directly addressing user concerns about the opacity of AI decision-making processes [11]. When evidence handling is merged into a monolithic writer agent, this chain of custody can become obfuscated, making it difficult to distinguish between source-derived facts and generative embellishments, thereby increasing the risk of undetected hallucinations.

Empirical studies in clinical settings underscore the value of this modular separation for both performance and trust. Research comparing multi-agent systems (MAS) to single-agent systems (SAS) in clinical decision support found that a MAS with specialized agents, including those for lab analysis and validation, improved prediction accuracy for critical outcomes like mortality [29]. While the transparency scores were comparable, the architectural pattern of specialized agents inherently supports a more interpretable framework by design. This aligns with the broader vision for agentic AI in healthcare, where systems must integrate and validate multimodal data with high precision to support patient-centric care [30]. The survey on Agentic Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems for medical decision support further identifies architectural patterns that leverage knowledge graphs and dedicated reasoning agents to create interpretable decision-making frameworks, which are essential for clinical validation and provider collaboration [8].

In legal analysis or outbreak analytics, the need for rigorous, reproducible validation is equally acute. The synthesis of policy-relevant evidence during public health emergencies requires the coordination of multiple analytical tasks and data sources [4]. Specialized LLM-based agents formalize workflows and introduce review steps, enhancing consistency and reproducibility—a quality control mechanism that is more robust when evidence curation is an independent, accountable process rather than a subsumed function. Ultimately, maintaining evidence agents as distinct entities institutionalizes validation checkpoints, fosters stakeholder confidence through demonstrable rigor, and provides the structural transparency necessary for responsible AI deployment in critical domains.

### 4.5 Architectural Flexibility and Interoperability Considerations

The architectural choice between integrating evidence agents into writer agents or maintaining them as independent modules has profound implications for a system's flexibility and its ability to interoperate within broader ecosystems. A modular, multi-agent architecture, where evidence and writer agents are distinct, inherently promotes greater flexibility. This separation allows each agent type to be developed, updated, or replaced independently according to its specialized function. For instance, an evidence agent's retrieval and validation algorithms can be refined without necessitating changes to the writer agent's narrative generation models, and vice versa. This modularity is a core principle in frameworks like the CACA Agent (Capability Collaboration based AI Agent) [31], which advocates for an open architecture where collaborative capabilities are integrated to enhance extensibility and reduce dependence on any single monolithic model. Such a design aligns with the need for scalable and adaptable systems, as seen in complex simulation platforms like SimMobility [32], which integrates diverse agent roles within a flexible, event-based architecture to model intricate real-world interactions.

From an interoperability perspective, independent agent roles are crucial for seamless integration into heterogeneous multi-agent systems and standardized communication protocols. The vision for universal interoperability, as outlined in work on Conversational AI multi-agent frameworks [33], relies on agents with well-defined, discrete functions that can be discovered and composed dynamically via open APIs. A dedicated evidence agent with a clear service manifest for "information validation and synthesis" is far more interoperable than a monolithic writer agent attempting to expose a convoluted set of mixed capabilities. This separation facilitates the agent discovery and composition processes that are foundational to scalable agentic ecosystems. Conversely, merging the roles creates a more monolithic architecture, which can streamline internal workflows for specific, closed-loop tasks but at the cost of reduced external interoperability and increased system brittleness. A unified agent becomes a single point of failure and is harder to integrate with other specialized agents that may offer superior evidence curation or content generation in specific domains.

The trade-off, therefore, lies between the internal efficiency of a tightly integrated, potentially simpler monolithic design and the long-term adaptability and collaborative potential of a modular, multi-agent collaborative design. For systems requiring integration into larger, evolving digital ecosystems—such as industrial automation [3] or autonomous cloud operations [34]—the flexibility and clear service boundaries afforded by independent evidence agents are paramount. These domains demand systems that can adapt to new tools, data sources, and partner agents. A monolithic writer agent that subsumes evidence handling may become a bottleneck, unable to easily plug into new evidence sources or delegate specialized validation tasks to a more competent external agent. Thus, while integration might offer short-term development simplicity, architectural separation is a strategic enabler for sustainable flexibility, robust interoperability, and the collaborative intelligence envisioned in advanced Multi-Agent Based Decision Support Systems (MADSS) [1].

## 5 Case Studies and Domain-Specific Implementations

### 5.1 Scientific Research and Outbreak Analytics

The domain of scientific research, particularly outbreak analytics, provides a compelling case study for examining the architectural separation of evidence agents from writer agents. Outbreaks such as mpox and COVID-19 necessitate swift, evidence-based responses, which rely on the synthesis of diverse information sources and multiple analytical techniques [4]. The traditional workflow involves numerous time-intensive manual tasks, creating bottlenecks that hinder the delivery of analysis at scale. The integration of Large Language Model (LLM)-based agents into these pipelines presents a significant opportunity to enhance efficiency. Crucially, the literature advocates for the use of *specialized* LLM-based agents, which formalize team interactions, optimize workflows, and enable quality control to improve consistency and reproducibility [4]. This architectural choice directly supports the argument for independent evidence agents.

In this context, an independent evidence agent would be responsible for the core functions of information acquisition and curation. It would perform targeted retrieval and validation of data from epidemiological reports, genomic databases, and scholarly literature. Systems like Microsoft Academic Services (MAS) demonstrate the power of AI in comprehensively collecting and analyzing scholarly communications at scale, using natural language understanding to extract factoids and assemble them into a knowledge graph [22]. A dedicated evidence agent could leverage such technologies to synthesize multi-modal evidence, maintain traceability of sources, and ensure the credibility of the information foundation. This separation allows the evidence agent to focus on rigorous validation, a critical need given that misinterpretation of data could lead to erroneous conclusions with serious consequences [4].

Conversely, a writer agent would utilize the curated, high-quality evidence provided by the evidence agent to generate situational reports, policy briefs, and public communications. The impact of this architectural separation is threefold. First, it enhances **response speed** by allowing evidence retrieval and synthesis to occur in parallel with the drafting of initial narrative structures, and through the simultaneous deployment of multiple specialized agents [4]. Second, it improves **evidence quality** by instituting formal review steps where the evidence agent's output is scrutinized before being passed to the writer agent, reducing the risk of hallucinations and errors propagating into final outputs. This creates a system of checks analogous to human peer review. Third, it bolsters **reproducibility**. A clear separation of concerns means the evidence trail—the sources, validation steps, and synthesis logic—is maintained independently of the narrative construction. This makes the analytical pipeline more transparent and auditable, allowing other researchers to verify the evidence base even if the final report's phrasing is altered. Therefore, in high-stakes, fast-moving domains like outbreak analytics, the case for architecturally independent evidence agents is strong, as it directly addresses the core requirements for speed, quality, and rigorous scientific reproducibility.

### 5.2 Legal Analysis and Judicial Assistance

The legal domain presents a quintessential high-stakes environment where the architectural separation of evidence agents from writer agents is critically examined. In applications such as legal analysis and judicial assistance, accuracy, the reduction of hallucinations, and the cultivation of user trust are paramount. Case studies from this field demonstrate that a multi-agent architecture with distinct evidence-handling roles significantly enhances system reliability. For instance, the Chatlaw system exemplifies this approach by employing a multi-agent collaborative framework specifically designed to mitigate the hallucination problem, which poses substantial legal risks [35]. In this architecture, specialized agents are responsible for evidence retrieval and validation against structured legal knowledge graphs, while separate writer agents generate the final legal consultation text. This separation ensures that the content generation is grounded in verified legal precedents and statutes, directly improving response accuracy. The system's use of Standardized Operating Procedures (SOP), modeled on real law firm workflows, further institutionalizes the distinct roles of evidence gathering and content drafting, leading to a documented outperformance of models like GPT-4 in legal benchmarks.

The independent evidence agent in such a setup acts as a dedicated validator, cross-referencing claims against a curated legal database before information is passed to the writer agent. This process directly addresses user trust concerns highlighted in broader studies on LLM-based conversational agents, where transparency in decision-making is a key factor for adoption, especially in sensitive fields [11]. By making the evidence retrieval and validation step an explicit, auditable module, the system provides a clearer provenance for its conclusions, which is essential for legal professionals who must assess the reliability of the AI's output. The alternative—a merged agent performing both evidence synthesis and narrative generation—risks conflating these processes, potentially allowing unverified information or speculative reasoning to taint the final output without a clear internal checkpoint. Furthermore, the principles of trust and reputation management, as discussed in the context of networked systems, underscore the importance of verifiable processes for building credibility [36]. An independent evidence agent contributes to a "trust-based" system architecture where the quality of the foundational information can be assessed separately from its presentation. Therefore, for high-stakes legal applications, the case studies strongly support an architectural paradigm where evidence agents operate as independent roles to enforce rigorous validation, provide traceability, and ultimately foster the trust necessary for practical judicial assistance.

### 5.3 Financial Services and Investment Management

The domain of financial services and investment management presents a compelling case study for examining the architectural separation of evidence agents from writer agents, as it demands a delicate balance between decision autonomy, rigorous auditability, and operational efficiency. In this high-stakes environment, evidence agents are tasked with the critical function of sourcing, validating, and synthesizing complex, multi-modal data—from real-time market feeds and regulatory filings to economic indicators and news sentiment [13]. The independent operation of such agents ensures a dedicated focus on data integrity and traceability, which is paramount for compliance and audit trails. For instance, in credit risk assessment, an autonomous evidence agent can continuously ingest and evaluate borrower data, applying consistent validation rules independent of the narrative construction process, thereby reducing the risk of biased or unsubstantiated conclusions being generated [37]. This separation supports the explainability mandates of modern financial governance, as the evidence retrieval and reasoning chain remains distinct and inspectable.

Conversely, arguments for integration highlight gains in workflow optimization. A unified agent that both gathers evidence and drafts investment summaries or client reports could reduce latency, enabling faster responses to market events. This is particularly relevant for high-frequency trading insights or personalized customer communications where speed is a competitive advantage [14]. However, this integration introduces significant trade-offs. Merging roles can obscure the provenance of information, complicating regulatory audits and eroding trust. In complex cognitive decision-making tasks like investment planning, the risk of the agent conflating evidence curation with persuasive narrative generation increases, potentially leading to "hallucinated" justifications for decisions [38]. The multi-agent decision support system (MADSS) paradigm, which often employs specialized knowledge agents, suggests that separation enhances system robustness and allows for more effective coordination among specialized entities, each accountable for a specific facet of the decision-making pipeline [1].

Empirical observations from agentic AI implementations in finance indicate that architectural independence is favored in scenarios demanding high accountability, such as regulatory reporting and fiduciary advice, where auditability is non-negotiable. In contrast, more integrated designs may be pragmatically deployed for internal analytics or customer-facing chatbots where operational efficiency and personalization are prioritized over stringent evidence traceability. The choice ultimately hinges on the specific task's risk profile and the required strength of the human-in-the-loop oversight mechanisms [21].

### 5.4 Healthcare Diagnostics and Clinical Decision Support

The domain of healthcare diagnostics and clinical decision support represents a critical testbed for evaluating the architectural separation of evidence agents from writer agents. In this high-stakes environment, the reliability of diagnostic outputs, the cultivation of clinician trust, and strict adherence to safety protocols are paramount. Implementations in this field strongly advocate for maintaining evidence agents as independent, specialized modules. As surveyed in [8], agentic RAG systems in healthcare often employ distinct evidence agents responsible for rigorous information retrieval, validation against trusted medical knowledge bases (e.g., clinical guidelines, drug databases), and synthesis of multi-modal patient data. This separation ensures that the evidence-gathering and validation process is insulated from the narrative generation performed by writer agents, directly enhancing diagnostic reliability by minimizing the risk of the writer agent "hallucinating" or fabricating medical facts—a fundamental limitation of LLMs noted in [19].

The independence of the evidence agent is crucial for building clinician trust, a significant barrier to AI adoption. A dedicated evidence module can provide clear provenance for its outputs, citing specific guidelines, research papers, or patient data points. This traceability allows clinicians to audit the AI's reasoning, moving from a "black box" to a more transparent, evidence-based tool. The framework for assessing LLMs highlights the necessity of control mechanisms and referencing external sources to ensure appropriateness of output, a function naturally served by a specialized evidence agent [19]. Furthermore, specialized architectures like the multi-agent intuitionistic fuzzy logic system described in [39] demonstrate how dedicated agents for data processing and uncertainty quantification (acting as evidence agents) contribute to accurate health status detection, supporting the case for role specialization.

Regarding safety protocols, an independent evidence agent enforces a critical "checkpoint" in the clinical workflow. It can be designed to strictly adhere to validation rules, cross-reference contraindications, and flag inconsistencies before evidence is passed to the writer agent for summarization or report drafting. This creates a systemic guardrail, ensuring that generated content is anchored in vetted information. The vision of next-generation agentic AI for healthcare, as outlined in [30], emphasizes autonomy, precision, and reduced error rates—goals that are bolstered by an architecture where evidence handling is a dedicated, accountable function. Conversely, merging the roles risks conflating evidence retrieval with persuasive narrative generation, potentially obscuring source credibility and compromising the rigorous validation processes required in clinical settings. Therefore, the healthcare domain provides a compelling argument for architectural independence, prioritizing safety, auditability, and trust over the potential workflow efficiencies of a merged model.

### 5.5 Business Intelligence and Customer Service Operations

The deployment of multi-agent AI systems in business intelligence (BI) and automated customer service offers a critical lens through which to examine the architectural debate surrounding evidence agents. In these domains, the tension between achieving highly personalized customer interactions and maintaining robust, scalable operations is paramount. Implementations often reveal that the separation of evidence-gathering and content-generation functions can significantly enhance both dimensions. For instance, in customer service, a dedicated evidence agent can retrieve and validate customer history, product information, and policy details from disparate databases and knowledge graphs [40]. This curated, validated evidence is then passed to a specialized writer agent, which synthesizes it into a coherent, personalized response tailored to the customer's tone and context [41]. This separation ensures that the personalization is built upon a foundation of accurate, traceable information, reducing the risk of hallucinations or inconsistent service that could erode trust. The specialization allows the writer agent to focus on the nuanced task of relational communication, adapting conversation styles based on psychological and contextual knowledge, a process heavily supported by the reliable data provided by its evidence-oriented counterpart [41].

From an operational scalability perspective, an independent evidence agent architecture proves advantageous. It allows for the evidence retrieval and validation module to be optimized and scaled independently of the narrative generation layer. As noted in studies on AI agent-based service innovation, such specialization can accelerate service response times and reduce operational workload by streamlining complex information processing pipelines [14]. A unified agent attempting to perform both evidence curation and personalized writing may become a bottleneck, especially during peak loads, as the cognitive and computational demands of each task conflict. Conversely, a modular system enables parallel execution; evidence agents can pre-fetch and update knowledge bases continuously, while writer agents generate responses based on the latest validated evidence pool. This design aligns with the need for "agility and user-oriented features" in dynamic business environments [40]. However, this separation necessitates sophisticated integration mechanisms and can introduce latency in the handoff between agents. The governance model also becomes crucial, as highlighted by the "Human-in-Command" framework, which emphasizes the need for human oversight to ensure accountability and ethical alignment in these automated, multi-stage workflows [21]. Ultimately, in business intelligence and customer service, the architectural independence of evidence agents appears to fortify the reliability underpinning personalized interactions while providing a more resilient and scalable foundation for large-scale operations.

### 5.6 Network Management and Telecommunications

The domain of network management and telecommunications presents a compelling case study for examining the architectural independence of evidence agents, as the inherent complexity, dynamism, and criticality of modern networks demand systems capable of robust, evidence-based decision-making and rapid adaptation. Implementations in this field, particularly within next-generation architectures like space-air-ground integrated networks (SAGIN) and software-defined infrastructures, highlight how specialized evidence agents enhance system adaptability, reduce failure response times, and manage operational complexity. For instance, in the context of SAGIN resource management, an agentic AI framework embeds specialized agents within a MAPE-K control plane to autonomously interpret operator intents and optimize network execution [18]. Here, distinct agents such as semantic resource perceivers function akin to evidence agents by continuously monitoring and validating heterogeneous, time-varying network conditions—a task requiring dedicated focus on information acquisition and credibility assessment. This separation allows the orchestrator agent (analogous to a writer agent responsible for planning and generating configuration commands) to base its decisions on a curated, validated evidence stream, thereby improving the system's adaptability to dynamic topologies and reducing latency in service orchestration.

The benefits of this architectural separation become particularly evident when considering failure response and management complexity. A unified agent attempting to both gather multi-domain evidence and execute reconfiguration plans would face significant cognitive load, potentially delaying critical responses to network anomalies or security threats. The specialized evidence agent model, by providing a dedicated module for observability and validation, directly contributes to faster and more reliable failure diagnosis. This is supported by research into optimizing agent-environment interactions, where enhancing environment observability—a core function of an evidence agent—is identified as a key method for improving agent success rates without modifying the core reasoning agent [42]. In more ambitious visions like FlexNGIA 2.0, where AI agents autonomously design and deploy network protocols, the implicit need for evidence gathering and validation at each stage of the design-test-deploy cycle suggests that embedding this function within a distinct agent role would enhance traceability and auditability, crucial for managing the complexity of self-evolving networks [12].

Conversely, arguments for integration surface in scenarios prioritizing ultra-low latency and streamlined workflows for specific, well-defined orchestration tasks. Some resource orchestration mechanisms employ a more integrated agent model where a single agent handles both perception and action based on a learned policy, as seen in certain deep reinforcement learning approaches for multi-domain virtual network embedding [43]. This can reduce coordination overhead. However, such integrated models often struggle with explainability and may lack the rigorous, evidence-traceable validation required for high-stakes network decisions or when integrating semantic, intent-based instructions from human operators. Therefore, the choice between independent evidence agents and integrated writer agents in telecommunications hinges on the specific trade-off between raw operational speed and the need for adaptable, transparent, and verifiable decision-making in increasingly autonomous and complex network environments.

### 5.7 Agricultural and Environmental Management

The domain of agricultural and environmental management presents a compelling case study for examining the architectural separation of evidence and writer agents, particularly in the context of climate-responsive adaptation. This domain is characterized by high-stakes decisions under uncertainty, involving complex interactions between climatic data, soil conditions, crop models, and socio-economic factors. The implementation of agentic AI systems here demonstrates how independent evidence agents enhance decision robustness and improve communication with diverse stakeholders, from smallholder farmers to policy-makers. For instance, frameworks like the "AGENTIC AI–DRIVEN DECISION-SUPPORT FRAMEWORK FOR CLIMATE-RESPONSIVE AGRICULTURAL ADAPTATION USING REINFORCEMENT LEARNING" leverage multi-agent reasoning, where specialized agents handle distinct functions. In such a system, an independent evidence agent is responsible for the continuous retrieval, validation, and synthesis of multi-modal data—including real-time climate forecasts, soil sensor readings, and historical yield patterns [44]. This separation ensures the evidence base is rigorously curated and traceable before being passed to a writer agent tasked with generating actionable advisories or reports for farmers.

The need for this architectural independence is underscored by studies on agricultural extension, which highlight the critical importance of credible, validated information for building resilient systems [45]. When evidence gathering is a distinct, auditable process, it increases the trustworthiness of the final recommendations, a factor crucial for farmer adoption. Furthermore, participatory approaches emphasize the co-creation of knowledge, requiring systems that can separate raw data and evidence synthesis from the narrative tailoring needed for different audiences [46]. An independent evidence agent can maintain a consistent, validated knowledge graph, while a separate writer agent adapts the communication style—whether as a simple SMS reminder, an interactive voice response, or a detailed plan for a cooperative—based on stakeholder needs and literacy levels, as explored in studies on ICT-mediated advice [47].

This separation also mitigates risks in dynamic environments. Climate adaptation decisions must integrate complex variables, and bundling evidence retrieval with content generation in a single agent could lead to cognitive overload, increasing the risk of "hallucinating" unsupported recommendations during narrative construction. The conceptual design of smart farming solutions, often built as multi-agent systems with ontology-driven knowledge bases, inherently supports this modularity [48]. By maintaining an architecturally independent evidence agent, the system ensures that the foundational data and reasoning processes remain transparent and verifiable. This is vital not only for operational decisions but also for stakeholder communication, enabling clear explanations of *why* a specific adaptation strategy (e.g., adjusting planting dates or conserving soil moisture) is recommended, thereby enhancing both the robustness of the decision and the efficacy of the agricultural extension process.

### 5.8 Supply Chain and Logistics Optimization

The domain of supply chain and logistics optimization presents a compelling case study for examining the architectural independence of evidence agents. In this high-stakes, dynamic environment, decisions regarding inventory management, route planning, demand forecasting, and disruption response rely on synthesizing vast, real-time data streams from IoT sensors, market feeds, ERP systems, and external partners. The core argument for maintaining evidence agents as distinct entities hinges on the need for rigorous, auditable, and specialized data validation and synthesis before any prescriptive or communicative action is taken. An integrated writer-evidence agent might prioritize generating a coherent narrative or action plan, potentially at the expense of thoroughly vetting the underlying data for anomalies, credibility, or contextual relevance. In contrast, a dedicated evidence agent can focus on continuous information acquisition, curating evidence from disparate sources [49], and constructing a validated, traceable knowledge base. This separation ensures that the subsequent "writer" or "orchestrator" agent, responsible for generating reports, alerts, or executable plans, operates on a foundation of high-fidelity evidence, thereby reducing the risk of cascading errors from flawed data.

The principles of multi-agent coordination and cooperative game theory, as discussed in research on service composition [49], are directly applicable. In a complex supply network, various agents (e.g., for procurement, transportation, warehousing) must cooperate. An independent evidence agent can act as a neutral arbiter, providing a shared, validated situational awareness to all coordinating actors, which is critical for achieving global optimization rather than local sub-optimal decisions. This architectural pattern enhances system resilience; when a disruption like a port closure or supplier failure occurs, the evidence agent can independently gather and correlate multimodal evidence (e.g., satellite imagery, news reports, carrier updates) to confirm the event's scope and impact. The writer agent then utilizes this synthesized evidence to communicate contingency plans to stakeholders with clear provenance for each recommendation, fostering trust and enabling rapid, informed human oversight.

Furthermore, the auditability and compliance requirements in global logistics necessitate transparent decision trails. An independent evidence agent maintains logs of its retrieval sources, validation checks, and synthesis logic, which is crucial for regulatory compliance, performance analysis, and post-mortem reviews of decision-making processes. Merging this function into a writer agent could obfuscate the lineage from raw data to final recommendation, making it difficult to pinpoint whether a failure originated from poor evidence or flawed narrative construction. Therefore, for supply chain and logistics optimization—where decisions have direct financial, operational, and reputational consequences—the architectural separation of evidence handling from content generation and action planning is not merely beneficial but often essential for robustness, transparency, and effective human-AI collaboration.
