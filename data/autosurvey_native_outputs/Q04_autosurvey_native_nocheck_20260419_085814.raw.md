# Designing Multi-Stage Retrieval-Evidence-Writing Workflows for Intelligent Information Services: A Survey of Architectures, Applications, and Challenges

## 1 Introduction: From Passive Retrieval to Proactive Cognitive Support

### 1.1 The Evolution of Information Retrieval: From Passive Matching to Active Support

The evolution of information retrieval (IR) systems has been marked by a persistent focus on the core task of matching user queries to relevant documents or items. For decades, the dominant paradigm has been the retrieval-ranking model, where systems passively respond to a query by retrieving a set of candidate items from a corpus and then ranking them based on estimated relevance. While effective for simple fact-finding or straightforward product lookups, this model is fundamentally misaligned with the complex, multi-stage cognitive decision processes that characterize many real-world user tasks [1]. This misalignment introduces critical limitations that become starkly evident in domains like e-commerce search. Here, users often engage in intricate decision-making involving product comparison, feature evaluation, and trust assessment, which a simple list of ranked products fails to support. The reliance on query-item matching leads to semantic gaps when users pose complex queries involving negation, multiple constraints, or implicit reasoning demands [1]. Consequently, users are left to shoulder high decision costs, frequently resorting to cross-platform information foraging to complete their cognitive journey, a process that generates significant cognitive friction [2].

The passive nature of conventional IR creates a service gap, as these systems lack the capacity to provide proactive, evidence-based guidance. They function as reactive databases rather than active cognitive partners. This limitation extends beyond e-commerce. In healthcare, a simple retrieval system might find relevant medical literature but cannot synthesize evidence across papers, reconcile conflicting findings, or generate a patient-specific diagnostic summary. Similarly, in public health outbreak analytics, traditional workflows require skilled staff to manually perform time-intensive tasks like data extraction, cleaning, and synthesis from multiple sources, creating bottlenecks in generating timely, evidence-based responses [3]. The core deficiency is the absence of a structured workflow that integrates retrieval with evidence synthesis and reasoned generation. As highlighted in discussions on Multi-Agent Based Decision Support Systems (MADSS), complex decision-making in dynamic environments requires systems that can analyze vast amounts of data, evaluate alternatives, and consider multiple criteria collaboratively [4]. The conventional retrieval-ranking paradigm, being a single-step, relevance-centric process, is ill-equipped for this holistic support. It treats information finding as the end goal, rather than the first step in a larger cognitive workflow aimed at producing actionable insights and reducing user burden. This historical context underscores the imperative for a paradigm shift from passive matching systems to architectures capable of delivering active, multi-stage cognitive support.

### 1.2 Defining the Retrieval-Evidence-Writing Paradigm

The retrieval-evidence-writing paradigm represents a fundamental shift from traditional information retrieval systems, which primarily focus on single-step query-document matching and ranking. In contrast, this new paradigm conceptualizes intelligent information services as a multi-stage cognitive workflow that systematically integrates structured information retrieval, evidence synthesis, and reasoned writing or generation to produce actionable, context-aware insights. It moves beyond merely returning a list of potentially relevant items—a hallmark of the conventional "retrieval-ranking" approach criticized for its passivity and misalignment with complex user decision processes [1]. Instead, the paradigm orchestrates a sequence of deliberate steps: first, actively gathering and retrieving pertinent information from diverse, often heterogeneous sources; second, critically analyzing, correlating, and synthesizing this retrieved information into coherent evidence; and third, articulating this synthesized evidence into a reasoned, written output that directly supports decision-making.

This workflow is inherently agentic and collaborative, often realized through multi-agent system architectures where specialized software agents assume distinct roles to decompose complex queries, retrieve knowledge, and generate outputs [5]. For instance, in clinical decision support, agentic systems can autonomously coordinate workflows to integrate patient data with medical literature, synthesize evidence, and generate diagnostic suggestions or reports [6]. Similarly, in outbreak analytics, LLM-based agents can be deployed to perform dedicated tasks such as data extraction and synthesis, formalizing team interactions to enhance the consistency and reproducibility of evidence generation [3]. The final "writing" stage is thus not mere summarization, but the culmination of a reasoning process grounded in retrieved and validated evidence, aimed at delivering proactive cognitive support. This paradigm, therefore, redefines the objective of information systems from finding information to constructing understanding, marking a critical evolution towards holistic decision intelligence.

### 1.3 The Imperative for Agentic AI and Multi-Stage Workflows

The limitations of passive, single-step retrieval systems underscore a critical imperative: the transition to proactive, multi-stage cognitive workflows powered by agentic AI frameworks. Traditional systems, which primarily match queries to static documents, fail to support the complex, iterative reasoning required for high-stakes decision-making. They lack the ability to decompose ambiguous user intents, synthesize evidence from disparate sources, and generate contextually grounded, actionable insights. This gap is particularly evident in domains like healthcare, where diagnostic reasoning requires integrating patient data with clinical guidelines and latest research, or in public health, where epidemic response demands real-time synthesis of heterogeneous data streams for policy formulation [3]. Agentic AI, characterized by autonomous goal-directed behavior, adaptability, and collaborative problem-solving, provides the architectural foundation to bridge this gap. By orchestrating multi-agent coordination, deep knowledge integration, and dynamic tool orchestration, these frameworks transform information services from reactive repositories into proactive cognitive partners.

The core necessity lies in moving beyond monolithic models to systems where specialized AI agents collaborate. As explored in [4], Multi-Agent-Based Decision Support Systems (MADSS) leverage the principles of adaptability, flexibility, and scalability inherent in multi-agent systems to enable collaborative decision-making. In an agentic retrieval-evidence-writing workflow, this translates to a coordinated pipeline: one agent may refine a user's initial query, another retrieves and filters evidence from structured and unstructured sources, a third synthesizes and reasons over this evidence, and a final agent composes a coherent, evidence-backed narrative. This division of labor mirrors expert human teams and allows for formalized, optimized interactions that enhance consistency and reproducibility compared to direct prompting of a single LLM [3]. Furthermore, such multi-agent coordination is essential for managing the complexity of modern networked environments, as seen in proposals for AI-native 6G networks where agents autonomously interpret intents and adapt to dynamic conditions [7].

Crucially, agentic frameworks enable dynamic knowledge integration and tool use, moving systems from closed-loop learning to open-world engagement. Agents are not confined to a fixed knowledge base; they can actively invoke tools and APIs to gather real-time data, execute code for analysis, or query specialized databases. This capability is fundamental for evidence-based support, ensuring that generated insights are grounded in the most current and relevant information. For instance, in medical decision support, agentic RAG systems can integrate live clinical data streams with knowledge graphs of medical literature to provide up-to-date recommendations [6]. Similarly, the vision for future internet architectures involves LLM-based agents that autonomously design and adapt network protocols based on real-time conditions [8]. This orchestration of external tools and services allows the workflow to be context-aware and responsive to evolving situations.

Therefore, the imperative for agentic AI and multi-stage workflows is clear: they are necessary to achieve proactive, evidence-based cognitive support. By decomposing complex tasks through multi-agent collaboration, deeply integrating and reasoning over knowledge, and dynamically orchestrating tools, these systems address the fundamental shortcomings of passive retrieval. They empower intelligent information services to not only find information but to understand context, evaluate evidence, and construct reasoned, actionable guidance—transforming them into indispensable partners for decision-making across healthcare, public policy, commerce, and beyond.

## 2 Architectural Foundations: Multi-Agent Coordination and Knowledge Integration

### 2.1 Multi-Agent System Architectures for Workflow Orchestration

Multi-Agent System (MAS) architectures serve as the central coordination engine for retrieval-evidence-writing workflows, enabling the decomposition of complex user queries into manageable subtasks and orchestrating their execution through specialized agent collaboration. These systems move beyond monolithic AI models by distributing cognitive labor across autonomous, goal-oriented software entities that communicate and coordinate to achieve a common objective [14]. The fundamental advantage lies in their adaptability, flexibility, and scalability, allowing the workflow to integrate diverse tools, knowledge sources, and reasoning capabilities [4]. A common architectural pattern is the supervisor-agent model, where a central planning or managerial agent decomposes a high-level task, such as generating a comprehensive market analysis, and delegates specific retrieval, synthesis, or writing actions to subordinate functional agents. This hierarchical coordination ensures a structured progression through the retrieval, evidence evaluation, and writing stages.

More sophisticated control mechanisms are exemplified by hierarchical MAPE-K (Monitor-Analyze-Plan-Execute over a shared Knowledge base) control loops, which provide a formal framework for adaptive workflow orchestration. As demonstrated in network resource management, such loops can be instantiated through specialized LLM-based agents—like semantic perceivers and intent-driven orchestrators—that collaboratively monitor the workflow state, analyze needs, plan subsequent actions, and execute tool calls [7]. This creates a dynamic, feedback-driven pipeline capable of adjusting its strategy based on intermediate results. In contrast, fully decentralized heterarchical architectures offer an alternative paradigm for scenarios demanding high robustness and flexibility. In these systems, agents operate without a central orchestrator, coordinating directly through peer-to-peer negotiations and information exchanges, as seen in agent-based manufacturing systems where holons and agents collaborate in a distributed fashion [15]. This architecture is particularly suitable for environments with dynamically changing resources or requirements.

Regardless of the specific topology, the core operational principle involves specialized agents assuming distinct roles aligned with the workflow stages. For instance, a retrieval agent might query databases and APIs, an evidence agent could synthesize and validate gathered information, and a writing agent would structure the final output. Their collaboration is underpinned by formal coordination mechanisms, which are critical for ensuring coherent, conflict-free task execution and accurate information handover between stages [16]. The choice of architecture—supervisor-led, MAPE-K, or heterarchical—involves trade-offs between control, scalability, and resilience, fundamentally shaping the workflow's ability to deliver proactive, evidence-based cognitive support.

### 2.2 Knowledge Integration: Graphs, Retrieval, and Augmentation

Effective knowledge integration is the cornerstone of intelligent information services, enabling systems to move beyond static retrieval to dynamic, evidence-based reasoning. This process involves synthesizing both structured and unstructured knowledge sources to provide accurate, contextual, and actionable insights. A primary method is the construction and utilization of domain-specific knowledge graphs (KGs), which offer a semantic framework for representing entities and their relationships. Systems like AGENTiGraph demonstrate how KGs enable intuitive, multi-turn interaction and management of domain-specific data through natural language, providing a visual solution for non-technical users to build and refine knowledge bases [17]. The value of such structured knowledge is further highlighted in cyber defense, where a domain-agnostic knowledge graph-as-a-service infrastructure supports the creation of custom graphs for intelligent agents to perform situational reasoning and deploy active defenses [18].

Complementing structured graphs, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) techniques enhance large language models (LLMs) by grounding their responses in dynamically retrieved, relevant documents from external knowledge bases. This paradigm is crucial for improving factual accuracy in knowledge-intensive dialogues. However, prompting LLMs with retrieved knowledge alone may be insufficient, as models are not inherently trained to effectively utilize external information for specific domains. Research indicates that knowledge augmented finetuning (KAFT)—finetuning LLMs with domain-specific data alongside external knowledge—substantially surpasses mere prompting in both RAG and agent-based systems, particularly in factual accuracy [19]. This underscores the importance of deep integration rather than superficial augmentation.

Advanced paradigms like Graph Retrieval-Augmented Generation (GraphRAG) further bridge the gap between structured knowledge and generative reasoning. GraphRAG leverages the interconnected nature of knowledge graphs to retrieve not just isolated facts but relevant subgraphs of related entities, providing richer context for generation. This approach is exemplified in climate services, where an agentic system uses a domain knowledge graph to retrieve contextual subgraphs, which a recipe generation agent then combines with LLM reasoning via GraphRAG to create detailed, traceable workflows [5]. Similarly, in healthcare, agentic RAG systems integrate knowledge graphs to represent medical knowledge and support multi-modal reasoning for clinical decision support [6]. The synergy of graphs, retrieval, and targeted augmentation thus forms a robust foundation for contextual grounding, enabling workflows that are both informed by deep domain knowledge and capable of coherent, evidence-driven synthesis.

### 2.3 Dynamic Tool and Service Orchestration

Dynamic tool and service orchestration is a critical architectural component that enables retrieval-evidence-writing workflows to transcend the limitations of static knowledge bases. This mechanism empowers AI agents to actively invoke external tools, APIs, and services in real-time, thereby gathering live data, executing complex analyses, and performing concrete actions as part of a reasoned cognitive process. Unlike traditional systems reliant on pre-indexed information, dynamic orchestration allows the workflow to adapt to evolving contexts and user intents, significantly enhancing its proactive support capabilities. The core of this functionality lies in enabling agents to perceive the need for external computation or data, select the appropriate tool from a registry, formulate a valid query or command, and integrate the returned evidence seamlessly into the ongoing reasoning and writing pipeline.

A primary architectural approach to achieve this is through multi-agent systems (MAS), where specialized agents are delegated the responsibility of tool use. As discussed in [4], such systems leverage the coordination and communication protocols among autonomous agents to decompose complex tasks. One agent, acting as an orchestrator or planner, can analyze a user query, determine that real-time financial data or a specific simulation is required, and delegate the execution to a dedicated "tool-use" agent. This agent then interacts with the external service API, processes the result, and passes the synthesized evidence back for integration. This division of labor mirrors human team collaboration and is essential for handling the heterogeneity and dynamism of external services [20]. The research in cloud-based service composition highlights the need for such dynamic and adaptable architectures to respond to both functional and non-functional parameters, ensuring the composed service workflow meets quality-of-service requirements.

Furthermore, the paradigm of LLM-based agents significantly advances dynamic orchestration. As highlighted in [3], specialized LLM-based agents can be designed to perform dedicated tasks such as querying databases, writing and executing code for statistical analysis, or scraping the latest research publications. Their ability to understand natural language instructions and generate structured API calls lowers the barrier to tool integration. Moreover, the agentic framework allows for formalized team interactions where one agent's output becomes the next agent's input, creating a robust, multi-step analytical pipeline. This is crucial in domains like public health, where generating evidence requires synthesizing multiple data sources and analytical techniques in real time.

The orchestration layer must also manage context-aware policy composition to guide tool selection and usage. The work in [21] addresses the challenge of operating in rapidly changing environments. By representing contexts through knowledge graphs and using agent ensembles, systems can compose policies on-demand for tool invocation without lengthy retraining, allowing for immediate response to new scenarios. This ensures that the tool-use strategy is appropriate for the specific context of the user query, whether it involves credit risk assessment [22] or personalized e-commerce recommendations.

Ultimately, dynamic tool and service orchestration transforms the intelligent information service from a passive retrieval engine into an active cognitive partner. It extends the system's operational knowledge boundary, allowing it to perform actions, run calculations, and incorporate the very latest information, which is foundational for delivering actionable, evidence-based, and timely written insights.

### 2.4 Learning and Adaptation Mechanisms

A critical enabler for robust and evolving retrieval-evidence-writing workflows is the integration of adaptive learning mechanisms that allow the system to improve its reasoning accuracy and long-term performance through experience. These mechanisms move beyond static, pre-programmed pipelines, endowing AI agents with the capacity to learn from interactions, optimize their policies, and refine their knowledge bases. Three primary adaptive learning paradigms are central to this evolution: knowledge-augmented fine-tuning, reinforcement learning for policy optimization, and memory-enabled learning from interaction.

Knowledge-augmented fine-tuning involves continuously updating the underlying models (e.g., LLMs) with curated, domain-specific evidence and successful reasoning traces retrieved during operation. This process grounds the models more deeply in the task context, reducing reliance on generic pre-training and mitigating hallucination. Complementing this, reinforcement learning (RL) provides a powerful framework for optimizing the sequential decision-making policies of agents within the workflow. Agents can learn to select the most effective retrieval strategies, synthesize evidence more coherently, or orchestrate tools dynamically by maximizing a reward signal tied to final output quality or user feedback. For instance, in complex, multi-agent environments, RL enables agents to learn cooperative strategies, as demonstrated in frameworks for resource allocation where agents learn to share intelligence or coordinate actions to maximize system utility [23] [24]. This approach is also pivotal in dynamic domains like climate-responsive agriculture, where agents learn adaptation policies through interaction with digital twin simulations [25].

Furthermore, memory-enabled agents that learn from interactions are essential for personalization and consistency. By maintaining a memory of past user queries, retrieved evidence, and generated outputs, agents can avoid repetitive reasoning, build upon previous conclusions, and adapt their behavior to user preferences over time. This capability enhances efficiency and user experience. The integration of these adaptive techniques addresses a key limitation of static systems: their inability to improve post-deployment. However, implementing such learning loops introduces challenges, including the need for safe exploration in high-stakes domains, the management of non-stationary environments when multiple agents learn concurrently, and the design of robust reward functions that accurately proxy for complex cognitive task success. Ultimately, these learning and adaptation mechanisms transform the workflow from a fixed procedure into a self-improving cognitive system, capable of delivering increasingly accurate and context-aware intelligent support.

### 2.5 Evaluation of Architectural Efficacy and Trade-offs

Evaluating the efficacy of multi-stage retrieval-evidence-writing workflows necessitates a systematic analysis of architectural performance, focusing on the comparative advantages of multi-agent systems over monolithic designs and the inherent trade-offs in scalability, latency, and robustness. Empirical findings from various domains underscore that a multi-agent architecture, which decomposes complex tasks into specialized roles, generally outperforms monolithic LLM applications in handling intricate, evidence-based reasoning. For instance, in medical decision support, agentic systems that coordinate retrieval, synthesis, and writing agents demonstrate superior performance in diagnostic accuracy and guideline adherence by dynamically integrating diverse knowledge sources [6]. Similarly, in operational contexts like root cause analysis, LLM-based agents equipped with retrieval tools show highly increased factual accuracy compared to static models, as they can proactively gather diagnostic information from logs and metrics [26]. This structured coordination, formalized through multi-agent frameworks, enhances consistency and reproducibility, a key advantage highlighted in outbreak analytics where agentic pipelines optimize workflows and enable quality control [3].

However, the adoption of multi-agent designs introduces significant trade-offs. A primary concern is increased computational latency and resource consumption due to inter-agent communication and sequential tool invocations, which can hinder real-time responsiveness—a critical factor in domains like credit risk assessment requiring instantaneous decisions [22]. Conversely, monolithic systems, while often faster for simple queries, struggle with scalability and robustness when faced with complex, multi-faceted tasks requiring evidence synthesis. Scalability in multi-agent systems is enhanced through decentralized coordination, allowing parallel task execution as noted in multi-agent decision support systems that improve efficiency in dynamic environments [4]. Yet, this scalability can be offset by coordination overhead and potential failure points if agent communication protocols are not robustly designed. Robustness, or the system's ability to maintain performance under varying conditions, is another critical metric. Agentic frameworks that incorporate learning and adaptation mechanisms, such as reinforcement learning for policy optimization, show improved long-term robustness by learning from interactions [22]. However, they also face challenges in maintaining reproducibility and managing prompt sensitivity, which can lead to unpredictable outputs.

Evaluation metrics from case studies further illuminate these trade-offs. In service innovation, AI agent implementations are assessed based on operational efficiency gains, such as reduced response time and workload, and user experience metrics like satisfaction [27]. For instance, AI service agents in rural insurance services demonstrated significant impacts on economic and market performance by improving service quality dimensions like efficiency and security [28]. In technical evaluations, metrics often include task completion accuracy, reasoning coherence, and the ability to handle out-of-distribution queries, as seen in root cause analysis studies [26]. Ultimately, the choice between architectural paradigms depends on the specific application requirements: multi-agent systems excel in complex, evidence-intensive domains where accuracy and structured reasoning are paramount, despite potential latency costs, whereas monolithic designs may suffice for simpler retrieval tasks where speed and resource efficiency are primary concerns.

## 3 Domain-Specific Implementations and Performance Evaluation

### 3.1 E-Commerce Search and Personalized Recommendation

The domain of e-commerce search and personalized recommendation has witnessed a paradigm shift from passive retrieval-ranking systems toward proactive, multi-stage cognitive frameworks. Traditional models, which rely primarily on query-item matching, are fundamentally misaligned with the complex, multi-stage decision processes of users, leading to semantic gaps in complex queries and high cognitive costs due to extensive information foraging [1]. To address these limitations, recent implementations leverage multi-agent cognitive frameworks that decompose intricate user intents, orchestrate knowledge retrieval, synthesize evidence, and generate actionable guidance, thereby transforming search into a collaborative decision support service.

For instance, the Multi-Agent Cognitive Decision Framework (MACDF) is designed to move beyond passive retrieval by proactively supporting user decision-making. It addresses critical limitations like handling queries involving negation or multi-constraint reasoning, demonstrating significant offline improvements in recommendation accuracy and user satisfaction [1]. Similarly, the CogSearch framework synergizes four specialized agents to mimic human cognitive workflows, fusing heterogeneous knowledge from internal and external sources to deliver highly actionable insights [2]. These architectures exemplify the core retrieval-evidence-writing workflow, where structured retrieval is followed by evidence synthesis and reasoned generation of personalized recommendations.

Empirical evaluations confirm substantial performance gains. Offline benchmarks validate the excellence of these frameworks in consultative and complex search scenarios [2]. More critically, online A/B testing on major platforms like JD.com provides concrete evidence of transformative impact. CogSearch reduced user decision costs by 5% and achieved a 0.41% increase in overall UCVR (User Conversion Value Rate), with a remarkable 30% surge in conversion rates specifically for decision-heavy queries [2]. These metrics underscore the framework's success in enhancing both decision efficiency and commercial outcomes. Furthermore, the broader application of AI agents in digital services, including e-commerce, has been shown to accelerate service response, improve information accuracy, and create personalized interactions, thereby enhancing overall user satisfaction and operational efficiency [27].

This evolution aligns with findings on user experience, where LLM-based conversational agents can offer higher satisfaction and lower cognitive load compared to traditional interfaces, a benefit directly applicable to shopping assistance contexts [29]. The transition thus represents a fundamental shift from relevance-centric retrieval toward holistic, evidence-based decision intelligence, setting a new standard for intelligent information services in e-commerce.

### 3.2 Healthcare Diagnostics and Clinical Decision Support

The healthcare domain represents a critical frontier for the application of retrieval-evidence-writing workflows, where the stakes for accuracy, reliability, and adherence to established protocols are exceptionally high. Agentic systems, particularly those built on Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) and multi-agent coordination, are being actively developed to transform diagnostics, triage, and treatment planning. These systems move beyond simple information lookup to orchestrate complex, evidence-based reasoning processes that mirror clinical workflows. A core architectural pattern involves multi-agent clinical decision support systems (CDSS) where specialized AI agents emulate distinct clinical roles—such as triage nurse, emergency physician, and pharmacist—to collaboratively assess patient data, retrieve relevant medical knowledge, and generate diagnostic and therapeutic recommendations [30]. This multi-agent approach allows for a more nuanced and comprehensive analysis than monolithic systems, decomposing complex clinical queries into subtasks handled by role-specific experts.

Empirical evaluations demonstrate the performance gains of such agentic frameworks. For instance, a multi-agent CDSS for emergency department triage showed high accuracy in triage decision-making, primary diagnosis, and critical findings identification when benchmarked against a single-agent baseline [30]. Similarly, another study comparing multi-agent and single-agent systems for intensive care prediction found that the multi-agent system achieved superior mortality prediction accuracy and reduced error in forecasting length of stay [31]. These performance metrics directly speak to improvements in diagnostic accuracy and clinical workflow efficiency, key objectives for intelligent information services in healthcare.

A critical function of these systems is their ability to integrate and adhere to clinical guidelines and structured knowledge. Agentic RAG systems achieve this by grounding their reasoning in retrieved evidence from authoritative sources, such as medical literature, drug databases (e.g., via RxNorm API), and institutional protocols [30]. The integration of knowledge graphs further enhances this capability, allowing agents to navigate relationships between symptoms, diseases, and treatments, thereby supporting more interpretable and guideline-compliant decision pathways [6]. This structured evidence retrieval and synthesis is fundamental to the "evidence-writing" phase, where the system must produce actionable, justified insights for clinicians.

However, the deployment of these systems is fraught with challenges that impact their real-world efficacy. Performance is contingent on rigorous clinical validation and the mitigation of risks like model hallucinations, which can lead to unpredictable and dangerous errors [32]. Furthermore, ensuring transparency and interpretability in multi-agent decisions remains a significant hurdle, as the complexity of agent interactions can obscure the reasoning trail, though some frameworks incorporate dedicated transparency agents to address this [31]. Ultimately, the promise of agentic AI in healthcare diagnostics lies in its potential to provide scalable, context-aware support that augments clinical expertise, reduces cognitive load, and standardizes care, but its success is predicated on overcoming these validation, safety, and transparency gaps [9].

### 3.3 Public Health Policy and Epidemic Management

The domain of public health policy and epidemic management presents a quintessential challenge for intelligent information services, characterized by dynamic data, complex interdependencies, and high-stakes decision-making under uncertainty. The retrieval-evidence-writing paradigm, operationalized through multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) frameworks and LLM-based agentic pipelines, offers a transformative approach to optimizing intervention strategies and resource allocation. These systems move beyond static data retrieval to actively synthesize evidence from disparate sources, model epidemiological dynamics, and generate actionable, evidence-based policy recommendations.

A primary application is in optimizing large-scale public health initiatives, such as the U.S. 'Ending the HIV Epidemic' program. Traditional decision models often fail to capture critical cross-jurisdictional interactions, leading to suboptimal resource distribution. To address this, a [33] proposes an MARL framework where each jurisdiction is modeled as an intelligent agent. These agents learn jurisdiction-specific policies while accounting for epidemiological spillover effects, effectively functioning as a coordinated resource optimization system. Experimental results in California and Florida demonstrated that MARL-driven policies outperformed single-agent approaches by reducing new infections under fixed budget constraints, highlighting the framework's effectiveness in intervention optimization.

Simultaneously, the need for rapid, real-time evidence synthesis during outbreaks like mpox and COVID-19 has spurred the development of LLM-based agentic workflows. As argued in [3], specialized LLM-based agents can formalize and accelerate the analytical pipeline. These agents can autonomously perform tasks such as literature review, data extraction, cleaning, coding for analysis, and report drafting. The multi-agent collaboration allows for parallel task execution, iterative review, and quality control, significantly enhancing the efficiency and reproducibility of generating situational updates and policy-relevant evidence. This addresses critical bottlenecks, enabling a more scalable and equitable distribution of analytical capacity during crises.

The performance of these systems is evaluated on metrics central to public health impact: intervention effectiveness and resource optimization. MARL frameworks are assessed by their ability to minimize disease incidence or maximize quality-adjusted life years within operational constraints [33]. LLM-based pipelines are measured by their acceleration of the evidence-generation timeline, the accuracy of synthesized information, and the reduction in analyst cognitive load and task completion time [3]. Together, these agentic workflows exemplify how the retrieval-evidence-writing paradigm can evolve from providing passive information to enabling proactive, data-driven cognitive support for complex public health decision-making.

### 3.4 Climate Services, Agriculture, and Urban Planning

The application of agentic AI frameworks to climate services, agriculture, and urban planning represents a paradigm shift towards proactive, data-driven, and adaptive decision-making in complex environmental systems. These frameworks integrate components like reinforcement learning (RL), digital twins, and multi-agent reasoning to transform static data analysis into dynamic cognitive support workflows. In climate-responsive agriculture, for instance, an agentic AI-driven framework combines RL with digital twin simulations to optimize crop management under variable climatic conditions [25]. This system employs specialized agents trained via algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization to autonomously adjust irrigation, fertilization, and planting schedules. Performance evaluations demonstrate significant improvements in key indicators such as crop yield and water-use efficiency under both normal and stressful climatic scenarios, alongside enhanced policy adaptability and reduced environmental footprint compared to conventional decision support systems. The statistical robustness of these gains is often validated through methods like ANOVA, underscoring the framework's capacity to balance productivity with sustainability.

For natural hazard assessment and mitigation planning, LLM-driven agentic systems are deployed to synthesize heterogeneous data sources—including hydrodynamic models, geospatial datasets, and policy documents—into actionable insights [34]. These multi-agent pipelines orchestrate model execution, retrieve relevant evidence, and generate interpretable risk assessments and communication materials for stakeholders. The workflow enhances decision robustness by providing context-aware, evidence-based recommendations for flood risk management or mitigation planning, as demonstrated in operational case studies. However, challenges related to reproducibility and prompt sensitivity are noted as critical considerations for reliable deployment.

In the broader domain of climate services, agentic systems are also designed to automate the generation of complex, multi-step analytical workflows. The Climate Service Recipes (HACID-CSR) system exemplifies this by using a multi-agent architecture, enhanced by a domain-specific knowledge graph, to produce transparent and traceable "recipes" for multi-hazard climate information [5]. This approach bridges the gap between raw climate data and sector-specific applications, improving the trustworthiness and contextual relevance of the generated advice for urban planning or agricultural extension services.

The integration of such agentic frameworks with participatory approaches and extension services further amplifies their impact. While participatory models emphasize farmer-led innovation and knowledge co-creation [35], agentic AI can augment these efforts by providing real-time, personalized advisories derived from synthesized evidence, thereby addressing capacity gaps identified in traditional extension systems [36]. Similarly, principles from integrated pest management (IPM), which advocate for ecological balance [37], can be operationalized at scale through agentic workflows that monitor pest dynamics and recommend sustainable interventions. Overall, the performance of these agentic AI frameworks in environmental domains is assessed not only through quantitative metrics like yield or resource efficiency but also through their ability to enhance adaptive capacity, ensure transparency, and support robust, long-term decision-making in the face of uncertainty.

### 3.5 User Experience, Trust, and Service Quality Evaluation

The evaluation of user experience (UX), trust, and service quality is paramount for the successful deployment of retrieval-evidence-writing workflows in intelligent information services. User-centric assessments move beyond traditional accuracy metrics to capture the holistic interaction between humans and AI agents, focusing on cognitive, affective, and behavioral outcomes. Empirical studies consistently show that LLM-based conversational agents can significantly enhance user satisfaction and reduce cognitive load compared to traditional interfaces [29]. This improvement stems from the agents' ability to provide immediate, coherent, and context-aware responses, streamlining complex information-seeking tasks. Key quantitative metrics for evaluating efficiency include task completion time and success rate, where AI agents demonstrate substantial gains by automating manual processes and accelerating service responses [27].

However, superior efficiency does not automatically translate into user trust or perceived service quality. Trust is a multifaceted construct heavily influenced by the agent's conversational skill and perceived humanness [38]. Agents exhibiting higher conversational competence, through natural turn-taking and contextual understanding, foster greater social presence and anthropomorphism, which in turn increases user engagement and trust. Furthermore, the concept of "relational personalization" is critical for service quality in digital customer service [39]. This framework emphasizes that personalizing conversation styles—such as tone, empathy, and formality—based on individual customer knowledge and context is essential for providing a "personal touch," moving beyond mere transactional efficiency to build rapport and improve the overall customer experience.

Despite these advantages, significant barriers to trust persist, primarily concerning transparency and data security [29]. Users often perceive the decision-making processes of multi-agent systems as opaque "black boxes," creating accountability gaps. The inability to trace how evidence was retrieved, synthesized, and formulated into a final written output can hinder adoption, especially in sensitive domains like healthcare or finance. Additionally, robust data governance is non-negotiable; users require assurance that their sensitive data is protected throughout the multi-stage workflow to mitigate risks of leakage [29]. Therefore, comprehensive evaluation must integrate technical performance with human factors. Perceived usefulness and ease of use, as highlighted in technology acceptance studies, remain strong predictors of behavioral intention to use these systems [40]. Ultimately, for retrieval-evidence-writing workflows to achieve widespread acceptance, they must not only be accurate and efficient but also transparent, secure, and capable of delivering personalized, trustworthy interactions that meet evolving user expectations.

## 4 Challenges and Ethical Considerations in Real-World Deployment

### 4.1 Technical and Operational Challenges

The deployment of multi-stage retrieval-evidence-writing workflows for intelligent information services confronts significant technical and operational hurdles that must be overcome to ensure robust, scalable, and cost-effective systems. A primary challenge lies in ensuring system scalability and computational efficiency. As these workflows often involve orchestrating multiple specialized agents, knowledge retrieval systems, and large language models (LLMs), the computational demand for real-time processing can be prohibitive. The coordination overhead in multi-agent systems, as discussed in the context of decision support [4], can lead to latency issues, especially when agents must collaborate on complex queries. Furthermore, the integration of real-time data streams and the execution of computational models, such as hydrodynamic simulations for hazard assessment [34], exacerbate resource consumption. Research on computation offloading in networked systems highlights the ongoing struggle to balance performance with energy and resource constraints [41; 42]. Maintaining reproducibility and consistency across workflow executions presents another major obstacle. The probabilistic nature of LLM outputs and the dynamic, stateful interactions between agents can lead to non-deterministic results. This is particularly problematic in high-stakes domains like healthcare or public health, where consistent, evidence-based outputs are critical. The challenge of prompt sensitivity further undermines reproducibility; minor variations in instruction prompts to LLM agents can yield significantly different reasoning paths and final outputs, as noted in hazard assessment applications [34]. Formal verification of coordination mechanisms remains complex [16]. Finally, the high operational costs associated with real-time, multi-agent coordination pose a substantial barrier to widespread adoption. These costs are not only monetary, stemming from the use of proprietary LLM APIs and cloud computing resources for parallel agent execution, but also relate to the infrastructure and expertise required for system maintenance and monitoring. The promise of efficiency gains from AI agents [27] must be weighed against these significant technical investments and ongoing operational expenses.

### 4.2 Validation, Reliability, and Safety Concerns

The deployment of retrieval-evidence-writing workflows in high-stakes domains such as healthcare and finance necessitates rigorous validation, unwavering reliability, and robust safety guarantees. A primary concern is the critical need for domain-specific validation that goes beyond generic performance metrics. For instance, in clinical settings, agentic systems must be validated against established medical guidelines and real-world patient outcomes to ensure clinical utility and safety [6]. The framework proposed for assessing LLM applications in healthcare underscores this by categorizing applications based on data source and output recipient, highlighting that solutions impacting clinical decisions require stringent oversight to address fundamental limitations like lack of understanding and predictability [32]. Without such validation, these systems risk providing unsound recommendations that could lead to patient harm.

A core technical challenge undermining reliability is the propensity of LLMs to generate hallucinations—outputs that are nonsensical or unfaithful to the retrieved evidence. This risk is exacerbated in complex, multi-agent workflows where errors can propagate. One proposed mitigation is the use of multi-agent architectures designed specifically to cross-verify information and reduce hallucination risks [43]. However, guaranteeing the complete absence of such unpredictable errors remains a formidable challenge, as LLMs inherently lack real-world understanding and can produce logically flawed reasoning [32]. This unpredictability is particularly dangerous in autonomous decision-making scenarios, such as real-time credit risk assessment, where model drift or inconsistent interpretations of high-dimensional data can lead to significant financial or operational risks [22].

Ensuring long-term robustness and safety involves continuous monitoring and adaptation. Systems must be designed to handle dynamic environments and maintain performance as data distributions shift. The integration of learning mechanisms, such as reinforcement learning for policy optimization, is a step toward adaptability [22]. However, this introduces new validation complexities, as the system's behavior evolves over time. Furthermore, the "black box" nature of complex multi-agent coordination complicates the traceability of decisions, making it difficult to audit failures and ensure accountability. Therefore, developing standardized evaluation protocols that assess not only accuracy but also consistency, failure modes, and recovery mechanisms under edge cases is paramount for the safe deployment of these cognitive workflows in critical applications.

### 4.3 Data Governance, Privacy, and Security Risks

The deployment of multi-stage retrieval-evidence-writing workflows in sensitive domains inherently amplifies data governance, privacy, and security risks. These agentic systems operate across distributed architectures, accessing and synthesizing information from heterogeneous sources, including proprietary databases, real-time APIs, and user-specific data. This creates a complex attack surface where sensitive data—such as personal health records, financial information, or confidential policy documents—traverses multiple agents and tools, significantly increasing the risk of inadvertent leakage or malicious exfiltration [44]. A primary challenge is maintaining end-to-end data provenance and enforcing strict access controls within dynamic, multi-agent pipelines where the boundaries of data usage can become blurred.

Implementing robust authentication, authorization, and audit (AAA) mechanisms for AI agents is therefore paramount. As highlighted by research on secure delegation frameworks, there is an urgent need to extend established protocols like OAuth 2.0 to manage agent-specific credentials, ensuring clear chains of accountability and restricting permissions based on the principle of least privilege [44]. Without such measures, unauthorized agent actions or compromised components could lead to significant data breaches. Furthermore, the "black box" nature of agent coordination complicates the implementation of secure audit trails, making it difficult to trace which agent accessed what data and for which reasoning step, a critical requirement for compliance with regulations like GDPR or HIPAA.

The risk is particularly acute in high-stakes applications. For instance, in outbreak analytics or clinical decision support, workflows process highly sensitive population health data [3]. Similarly, agentic frameworks for financial risk assessment or credit decisioning handle confidential borrower information [22]. A data leak in these contexts carries severe consequences. Moreover, the integration of external tools and services for real-time data retrieval introduces additional vulnerabilities at each API call point, requiring stringent validation of data inputs and outputs to prevent injection attacks or the accidental exposure of intermediate results containing sensitive inferences.

Ultimately, securing these cognitive workflows demands a holistic governance framework that embeds privacy-by-design principles. This includes techniques for data minimization, in-transit and at-rest encryption for all intermediate evidence, and the development of secure agent communication protocols. As the field progresses, creating standardized mechanisms for agent visibility and activity logging will be crucial for security auditing and forensic analysis [45]. Failure to address these intertwined governance and security challenges will severely hinder the trustworthy and compliant deployment of intelligent information services in real-world environments.

### 4.4 Algorithmic Bias, Fairness, and Equity Issues

The design and deployment of multi-stage retrieval-evidence-writing workflows for intelligent information services are fraught with significant challenges concerning algorithmic bias, fairness, and equity. These systems, which rely heavily on large language models (LLMs) and curated knowledge sources, risk perpetuating and even amplifying existing societal inequalities if not carefully governed. A primary source of bias stems from the training data and model design of the underlying LLMs. As noted in a comprehensive review, models like ChatGPT can inherit and propagate biases present in their vast, web-scraped training corpora, which often overrepresent certain demographics, languages, and cultural perspectives [13]. When these models form the reasoning core of an agentic workflow, their biased outputs can systematically disadvantage marginalized groups during critical stages such as evidence retrieval, synthesis, and final report generation. For instance, in a healthcare diagnostic support system, a model trained on data from predominantly high-income populations may provide less accurate or relevant evidence for patients from underrepresented backgrounds, leading to inequitable service quality.

Furthermore, the implementation of these advanced AI systems risks exacerbating the digital divide. While AI agents can potentially democratize access to expert-level analysis, as suggested in the context of outbreak analytics, their successful deployment requires robust digital infrastructure, technical literacy, and financial resources [3]. This creates a paradox where the very technology meant to bridge gaps could instead widen them, leaving rural or under-resourced communities behind. Research on AI service agents in rural India highlights that dimensions like efficiency and security are critical for user adoption and perceived service quality [28]. If these prerequisites are not met equitably across populations, the benefits of intelligent information services will be unevenly distributed, reinforcing existing socioeconomic disparities.

Ensuring equitable service delivery across diverse populations thus presents a profound challenge. It requires moving beyond technical accuracy to consider contextual fairness, which involves tailoring the workflow's outputs to be culturally relevant, linguistically appropriate, and accessible. The "black box" nature of complex multi-agent systems complicates this, as it is difficult to audit and rectify biased decision pathways. Moreover, as agents learn and adapt from user interactions, there is a risk of feedback loops that further entrench discriminatory patterns. The challenge is not only to mitigate bias at the model level but to architect the entire workflow—from data ingestion and tool selection to final communication—with equity as a core design principle. This necessitates diverse and representative development teams, continuous bias auditing frameworks, and proactive measures to ensure that the transformative potential of retrieval-evidence-writing systems serves all segments of society justly.

### 4.5 Transparency, Interpretability, and Accountability Gaps

The inherent complexity of multi-stage retrieval-evidence-writing workflows, particularly those orchestrated by multi-agent systems, creates significant challenges for transparency, interpretability, and accountability. The "black box" nature of these systems, where numerous specialized agents interact dynamically with knowledge bases and external tools, makes it exceedingly difficult to trace the provenance of information and the rationale behind final outputs [4]. This opacity is a critical barrier to trust and reliable deployment, especially in high-stakes domains like healthcare or finance where understanding the "why" behind a recommendation is as important as the recommendation itself. The difficulty in auditing the chain of reasoning from initial query through evidence retrieval to synthesized writing undermines accountability, making it hard to assign responsibility for errors or biases that may emerge from the collaborative process.

Efforts to inject explainability often focus on individual components, such as highlighting retrieved document passages in a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) step, but fall short of explaining the holistic, multi-agent decision-making process. For instance, an agentic framework for credit risk assessment may produce a final score and report, but the internal negotiations, weightings, and potential conflicts between specialized agents analyzing different risk factors remain obscured [22]. This lack of end-to-end interpretability is a major concern, as studies on conversational agents highlight that perceived opacity directly hinders user trust, even when the agents offer superior performance [29]. Furthermore, the integration of tools and real-time data streams adds layers of complexity, where an agent's decision to invoke one API over another can significantly alter the evidence base and subsequent reasoning, yet this tool-selection logic is rarely exposed.

Addressing these gaps requires mechanisms that go beyond traditional model explainability. There is a pressing need for structured audit trails that log agent interactions, tool calls, and intermediate evidence evaluations. Developing explainable AI (XAI) modules that can generate coherent narratives of the workflow's execution path is crucial, as proposed in some agentic frameworks [22]. Moreover, maintaining clear human oversight is paramount. This necessitates designing strategic handoff points and interpretable interfaces that allow human operators to comprehend the system's state and intervene meaningfully, aligning with the "Human-in-Command" governance principle [46]. Without such measures, the accountability for decisions generated by these powerful cognitive workflows remains dangerously diffused, posing risks to safety, fairness, and ethical compliance.

### 4.6 Ethical, Societal, and Regulatory Implications

Beyond technical and operational hurdles, the deployment of multi-stage retrieval-evidence-writing workflows raises profound ethical, societal, and regulatory implications that demand careful consideration. A primary concern is the potential for adverse psychological impacts, particularly for vulnerable users. As AI systems become more agentic and embedded in daily life, they risk mirroring, validating, or amplifying harmful content, which could contribute to epistemic instability or exacerbate mental health conditions [47]. For instance, an AI designed for proactive support might inadvertently reinforce delusional thinking in a user predisposed to psychosis by engaging with and affirming grandiose narratives, a phenomenon noted in emerging reports on so-called 'AI psychosis' [47]. This underscores the erosion of clear boundaries between supportive interaction and harmful reinforcement, necessitating frameworks for AI-integrated care that prioritize epistemic security.

Societally, the widespread adoption of these intelligent services risks exacerbating existing inequalities and creating new forms of digital dependency. While these systems promise to democratize access to complex information and decision support, their deployment is contingent on digital infrastructure and literacy, potentially widening the digital divide [13]. Furthermore, the automation of cognitive labor through agentic workflows could lead to the devaluation of human expertise and critical thinking skills, raising questions about the long-term impact on human agency and employment in knowledge-intensive sectors.

From a regulatory perspective, the dynamic, multi-agent nature of these workflows poses significant challenges for existing legal and compliance frameworks. Concepts of liability become blurred when a decision is the product of a chain of retrievals, syntheses, and generations orchestrated across multiple semi-autonomous agents. The "Human-in-Command" (HIC) framework emerges as a critical governance model, arguing that meaningful human oversight must be a systemic property to maintain accountability and ethical alignment, especially as mandated by regulations like the EU AI Act [46]. However, implementing such oversight in complex, real-time workflows is non-trivial. Regulators must grapple with how to audit these systems, ensure transparency in their reasoning traces, and establish standards for safety and fairness that keep pace with the technology's rapid evolution. Ultimately, navigating these implications requires a proactive, multidisciplinary approach that embeds ethical foresight and robust governance into the very architecture of intelligent information services.
