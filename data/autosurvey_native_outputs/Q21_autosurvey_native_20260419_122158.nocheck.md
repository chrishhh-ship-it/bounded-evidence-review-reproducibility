# The Impact of Writer, Reviewer, and Reviser Role Division on the Quality of Intelligence Reports: A Systematic Survey from Human-AI Collaboration to Multi-Agent Systems

## 1 Introduction and Conceptual Foundations

### 1.1 Introduction to the Tripartite Role Paradigm in Intelligence Production

The production of high-quality intelligence reports is a critical, high-stakes endeavor where errors or ambiguities can have profound consequences. To mitigate risks and enhance output, a structured division of labor has emerged as a foundational paradigm, most commonly articulated through the specialized roles of the writer, the reviewer, and the reviser. This tripartite role division represents a systematic approach to knowledge work, designed to leverage specialization, introduce checks and balances, and foster iterative refinement. The core research question this survey addresses is: How does this specific division of labor among writers, reviewers, and revisers impact the final quality of intelligence reports? This question is increasingly pertinent as the paradigm transitions from purely human teams to hybrid human-AI collaborations and fully autonomous multi-agent systems [1]. The writer is primarily responsible for the initial synthesis and drafting, transforming raw data and disparate information into a coherent narrative. The reviewer assumes a critical evaluative function, assessing the draft for factual accuracy, logical consistency, argumentative soundness, and alignment with analytical requirements. The reviser, acting on the reviewer's feedback, executes the necessary edits to correct errors, improve clarity, and enhance the overall persuasiveness and actionability of the report. This specialization is not merely procedural; it is a cognitive strategy to combat individual biases, reduce the likelihood of oversight, and systematically elevate the work product through structured critique and revision. The significance of this paradigm is particularly acute in complex domains like outbreak analytics, where timely, accurate, and actionable intelligence is vital for saving lives [2]. The formalization of these roles and their interactions creates a workflow that enhances consistency and reproducibility, a principle now being directly translated into computational systems using specialized large language model (LLM)-based agents [2]. Understanding the mechanics and efficacy of this human-originated paradigm provides the essential baseline for evaluating its implementation and optimization within modern AI-driven frameworks, including multi-agent decision support systems [3] and agentic knowledge management architectures [4].

### 1.2 Defining Intelligence Report Quality: A Multi-Dimensional Construct

A foundational step in assessing the impact of role division is to rigorously define and operationalize the concept of "intelligence report quality." This construct is inherently multi-dimensional, encompassing a spectrum of attributes that determine a report's utility, reliability, and ultimate value for decision-making. Synthesizing criteria from intelligence studies, knowledge management, and the evaluation of AI-generated outputs, quality can be deconstructed into several interdependent dimensions that serve as the dependent variables for evaluating writer-reviewer-reviser collaboration systems.

**Accuracy and Factual Consistency** form the bedrock of report quality, referring to the correctness of information, the fidelity to source data, and the absence of errors or hallucinations. In AI-agentic systems, this dimension is paramount, as models can generate plausible but incorrect content [2]. **Timeliness** is critical in dynamic intelligence contexts, measuring the speed from data acquisition to report dissemination. Automated agent workflows are often pursued specifically to accelerate this cycle [5]. **Clarity and Coherence** assess the logical flow, structural organization, and unambiguous presentation of complex information, ensuring the narrative is easily comprehensible.

Furthermore, quality extends to **Actionability**, which evaluates whether the report provides clear, context-relevant insights that directly support specific decisions or operations, a key goal of decision-support systems [3]. **Credibility and Trustworthiness** encompass the perceived reliability of the source and the methodological transparency of the analysis, which are essential for user adoption [6]. Finally, **Consistency** ensures the report is free from internal contradictions and aligns with established facts across its entirety, a dimension where iterative review processes are particularly impactful [2].

Operationalizing these dimensions allows for a structured assessment of how the specialized functions of writing (generation), reviewing (validation), and revising (refinement) collectively influence the final output. For instance, a reviewer agent may directly target accuracy and consistency, while a reviser agent may enhance clarity and actionability. This multi-faceted definition provides the necessary framework to analyze the efficacy of both human and AI-based tripartite role divisions in producing high-stakes intelligence deliverables.

### 1.3 Theoretical Foundations: From Human Teaming to Human-AI Collaboration

The theoretical foundations for understanding the tripartite role division in intelligence production span from established theories of human teaming to emerging models of human-AI collaboration. At its core, the specialization of writer, reviewer, and reviser roles is an instantiation of team cognition principles, where distributed expertise and coordinated processes are designed to enhance collective performance and mitigate individual cognitive limitations. This paradigm aligns with theories that posit effective teams develop shared mental models and engage in cross-monitoring, which are critical for complex, high-stakes tasks like intelligence analysis. The transition to incorporating artificial intelligence agents into these workflows necessitates an extension of these theories into the domain of human-AI teaming (HMT). HMT frameworks investigate how intelligent systems can be designed as collaborative team members rather than mere tools, focusing on dynamics such as trust calibration, communication, and adaptive autonomy. A pivotal model for governing this interaction in operational settings is the Human-in-Command (HIC) framework [7]. This framework positions human oversight as a systemic, strategic function essential for maintaining accountability, ensuring ethical alignment, and managing risks like algorithmic bias, especially as systems evolve from simple automation to semi-autonomous "agentic" AI [7]. The effectiveness of such collaborative systems, however, is ultimately contingent upon user acceptance. Here, the extensively validated Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and its extensions provide a critical lens. Systematic reviews confirm that perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use are strong predictors of behavioral intention to use AI technologies [8]. Furthermore, factors like trust, performance expectancy, and attitudes have been significantly and positively linked to the acceptance of AI agents across various service domains [8; 9]. These acceptance factors mediate how seamlessly human analysts integrate AI agents performing specialized roles into their workflows. Together, these theoretical strands—team cognition, HMT, HIC, and TAM—form a multidisciplinary foundation for analyzing how role-based collaboration, whether purely human or hybrid, influences the mechanisms that drive the quality of the final intelligence product.

To systematically analyze the impact of the writer, reviewer, and reviser role division on intelligence report quality, this survey adopts the Theories, Contexts, Characteristics, and Methods (TCCM) framework as a foundational analytical lens. The TCCM framework's utility in structuring reviews of complex agent-based phenomena is well-documented, as seen in its application to understand consumer responses to anthropomorphic AI service agents [10]. By applying this framework, we can dissect the tripartite role paradigm into its constituent analytical dimensions.

First, the **Theories** dimension allows for the consolidation of the diverse theoretical underpinnings that explain why role specialization functions. This includes theories of team cognition, human-AI teaming, and principal-agent theory, which provide the "why" behind the efficacy of dividing labor for quality control. Second, the **Contexts** dimension categorizes the environments in which these roles operate. This is crucial, as the effectiveness of role division is highly contingent on the operational setting, whether it be in traditional intelligence analysis, financial investment management [11], or real-time outbreak analytics pipelines [2]. Third, the **Characteristics** dimension focuses on the attributes of the agents—human or artificial—fulfilling each role. This involves examining agent capabilities, such as the reasoning power of LLM-based writers, the critical evaluation skills of reviewers, and the integrative synthesis abilities of revisers. Finally, the **Methods** dimension encompasses the methodological approaches used to study, evaluate, and optimize these role-based systems. This includes techniques for benchmarking performance, such as those proposed for financial LLM applications [11], as well as simulation-based approaches for composing agent policies [12] and evaluation methodologies drawn from operations research [1].

By employing the TCCM framework, this survey moves beyond a simple description of roles. It enables a systematic examination of how specific theories manifest in different contexts, how the characteristics of role-playing agents mediate outcomes, and what methods best capture the impact on the multi-dimensional construct of intelligence report quality. This structured lens is essential for generating actionable insights and identifying coherent future research trajectories for enhancing role-based collaborative systems.

## 2 Historical and Contemporary Models of Role Specialization

### 2.1 Historical Foundations in Human-Centric Processes

The tripartite division of labor among writers, reviewers, and revisers is not a novel construct of the AI era but is deeply rooted in centuries of human-centric knowledge production and quality assurance processes. Its historical foundations lie in the fundamental recognition that complex, high-stakes informational outputs benefit from specialized roles, iterative refinement, and systematic checks to mitigate individual error and bias. In the domain of academic publishing, this paradigm is institutionalized through the peer-review system, where an author (writer) submits work to be critically evaluated by domain experts (reviewers) whose feedback guides subsequent revisions by the author (reviser). This process, formalized in the 17th and 18th centuries with the advent of scientific journals, established a gold standard for validating knowledge, ensuring methodological rigor, and enhancing clarity before public dissemination. Similarly, within intelligence analysis—a core context for this survey—structured analytic techniques have long emphasized role separation to combat cognitive pitfalls. Historical frameworks, such as the use of "devil's advocates" or structured "red teaming," institutionalize the reviewer function to challenge assumptions and test hypotheses, while the primary analyst (writer) and a separate editing or quality control unit (reviser) work to synthesize findings and polish the final report for decision-makers [1]. Editorial workflows in journalism and publishing further exemplify this model, where reporters (writers), fact-checkers and editors (reviewers), and copy-editors or production staff (revisers) collaborate in a pipeline designed to uphold accuracy, consistency, and stylistic standards. The theoretical underpinning of this specialization draws from organizational theory and the division of labor, which posits that separating tasks according to skill sets increases efficiency and output quality. Furthermore, it aligns with principles of cognitive psychology, where the separation of generative (writing) and critical (reviewing) functions helps overcome confirmation bias and promotes deeper processing. The evolution of these human-centric processes provides the essential baseline and justification for its computational adaptation, demonstrating that the writer-reviewer-reviser structure is a proven mechanism for enhancing the credibility and actionability of complex reports [7].

### 2.2 Comparative Analysis of Role Implementation Across Domains

The tripartite role specialization of writer, reviewer, and reviser is not a novel construct but a well-established organizational principle across diverse professional domains. A comparative analysis reveals both common underlying principles and significant domain-specific adaptations in its operationalization. In traditional intelligence analysis and academic publishing, the roles are often formally institutionalized and sequential. A writer (analyst or researcher) produces a draft, a reviewer (peer or senior analyst) provides a critical assessment for accuracy and logic, and a reviser (often the original writer or an editor) incorporates feedback into a final, polished product. This linear, hierarchical model emphasizes error reduction, quality control, and the establishment of institutional credibility through rigorous, documented checks.

However, implementation varies considerably with context. In customer service management, for instance, roles can be more fluid and integrated. As discussed in [15], sophisticated multi-agent systems decompose service tasks among specialized agents that may simultaneously embody writing (generating responses), reviewing (checking policy compliance), and revising (adjusting tone based on sentiment) functions in rapid, iterative loops. This adaptation prioritizes timeliness and contextual adaptability over deep, deliberative review. Similarly, the concept of "professional fluidity" described in [16] highlights how self-employed consultants often cyclically assume all three roles themselves, blending them in a market-driven, relational process where validation comes from client feedback rather than institutional hierarchy. This contrasts sharply with the structured, internal validation of traditional models.

The domain of outbreak analytics presents a hybrid model, blending structured pipelines with agile adaptation. The workflow described in [2] suggests that while the core tripartite functions are preserved—data synthesis (writing), analytical validation (reviewing), and report refinement (revising)—their execution may be distributed across both human analysts and specialized LLM-based agents operating in parallel to meet extreme demands for speed and scale. Meanwhile, in agricultural extension services, as studied in [17], role specialization is linked to professionalization (accreditation, certification), aiming to standardize quality and accountability in service delivery, though knowledge gaps between public and private agents can lead to inconsistent implementation.

Common principles across domains include the separation of cognitive functions to mitigate bias, the establishment of feedback loops for refinement, and the pursuit of enhanced output credibility. Key adaptations arise from differing primary constraints: academic publishing prioritizes meticulous accuracy and novelty, intelligence analysis emphasizes actionable timeliness and security, customer service focuses on user satisfaction and efficiency, and outbreak response demands rapid scalability under uncertainty. These comparative insights establish a crucial baseline for understanding how the writer-reviewer-reviser paradigm must be thoughtfully adapted, rather than merely transplanted, when implemented in computational multi-agent systems or hybrid human-AI workflows.

### 2.3 Evolution Towards Hybrid Human-AI Collaborative Workflows

The evolution from purely human-driven role division to hybrid human-AI collaborative workflows represents a paradigm shift in intelligence production, fundamentally altering how the writer, reviewer, and reviser functions are allocated and executed. This transition is driven by the need to augment human cognitive capabilities, scale analytical processes, and enhance the overall quality and timeliness of reports. Hybrid systems strategically integrate AI agents to assume or assist with specific roles within the established tripartite framework, moving beyond simple automation to create synergistic partnerships. This shift is conceptually grounded in frameworks like Human-Machine Teaming (HMT) and the Human-in-Command (HIC) model, which provide principles for effective collaboration [7]. The HIC framework, in particular, emphasizes that meaningful human oversight is a systemic requirement for maintaining accountability and ethical alignment as AI assumes more autonomous functions, transitioning oversight from a technical practice to a critical governance mandate [7].

In practice, this evolution manifests in various configurations. AI may augment the *writer* role by drafting initial reports from structured data, assist the *reviewer* by performing automated fact-checking and consistency analysis, or support the *reviser* by suggesting clarifications and structural improvements. This delegation allows human analysts to focus on higher-order tasks such as strategic interpretation, nuanced judgment, and managing complex stakeholder contexts. The implementation often takes the form of specialized LLM-based agents working within a structured pipeline [2]. As noted in the context of outbreak analytics, using multiple agents formalizes team interactions, enables workflow optimization, and incorporates more review steps before human consumption, thereby enhancing consistency and reproducibility compared to direct LLM prompting [2].

However, this integration is not merely technical; it requires careful design of handoff mechanisms, governance structures, and interaction protocols. The operational triggers for strategic handoffs between human and AI agents are critical, ensuring that complex, ambiguous, or high-stakes decisions remain under human purview [7]. Furthermore, this evolution acknowledges the current state of AI as semi-autonomous, with limitations that necessitate a human-centric approach to ethics and system design [18]. The goal is not to replace human roles but to create an environment where skilled staff can dedicate more time to nuanced interpretation and decision-making, ultimately enhancing the overall efficacy of the intelligence production cycle [2].

### 2.4 Establishing a Baseline for Computational Adaptation

This subsection synthesizes the historical and contemporary human-centric models of the writer-reviewer-reviser paradigm to establish a foundational baseline framework. This baseline is essential for evaluating and guiding the computational adaptation of this tripartite role division into AI-agent systems. The core of this framework comprises two interdependent pillars: formalized coordination mechanisms and structured feedback loops. Drawing from the analysis of traditional domains, effective coordination is not merely sequential task handoff but involves sophisticated protocols for role interaction, conflict resolution, and information synchronization. This aligns with the principles highlighted in studies of multi-agent systems, where formal methods and coordination mechanisms are critical for validating system interactions and ensuring coherent outcomes [19]. In human workflows, these mechanisms are often implicit in organizational culture and standard operating procedures, but for computational adaptation, they must be explicitly defined as rules, communication protocols, and state management systems to prevent agent misalignment and ensure the workflow's integrity.

The second pillar, structured feedback loops, is the engine for iterative refinement and quality control. The baseline model demonstrates that quality emerges not from isolated role performance but from the cyclical exchange of critique and revision between the reviewer and reviser, informed by the writer's initial output. This creates a system of checks and balances that mitigates individual error and bias. The effectiveness of these loops depends on the clarity, specificity, and actionability of the feedback exchanged. Furthermore, the concept of leveraging stored information for system stability under disruptive conditions, as discussed in the context of resilient multi-agent control [20], finds a parallel in the human baseline where historical context, past reports, and precedent decisions inform current review and revision judgments. This historical awareness prevents context collapse and maintains consistency. Ultimately, this synthesized baseline framework provides the critical lens against which AI-agent implementations are measured. It asks whether a given multi-agent architecture has robust, formalized equivalents for human coordination protocols and whether its feedback loops are sufficiently rich and iterative to drive meaningful quality enhancement, or if they risk merely propagating errors efficiently.

## 3 Implementation in AI-Agent and Human-AI Collaborative Systems

### 3.1 Architectures for Multi-Agent Role Specialization

The instantiation of the writer, reviewer, and reviser paradigm within computational systems necessitates specific architectural patterns that enable role specialization, coordination, and effective task execution. Core architectural paradigms for this tripartite division are drawn from established fields such as Multi-Agent Systems (MAS), the emerging domain of Agentic Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), and frameworks built upon Large Language Models (LLMs). In a classic MAS architecture, autonomous software agents are designed with specific roles and capabilities, interacting through defined communication protocols and coordination mechanisms to achieve a collective goal [3]. This architecture naturally maps to the tripartite model, where distinct agents can be instantiated as a writer agent (responsible for content generation), a reviewer agent (tasked with critical evaluation), and a reviser agent (executing edits and refinements). The coordination among these agents—whether hierarchical, heterarchical, or through market-based mechanisms—is crucial for workflow integrity. For instance, a heterarchical architecture without a central orchestrator, as seen in some agent-based manufacturing systems, emphasizes peer-to-peer negotiation and can enhance robustness and adaptability in dynamic environments [21].

The advent of sophisticated LLMs has given rise to more flexible agent frameworks where the core "brain" of each specialized agent is a foundation model. These LLM-based agents can be prompted or fine-tuned to excel in specific sub-tasks of the intelligence production pipeline. A key advantage of this approach is the formalization of team interactions, which optimizes workflows and embeds quality control checkpoints, leading to more consistent and reproducible outputs than direct, monolithic LLM prompting [2]. Architectures often incorporate shared memory or blackboard systems to facilitate information exchange between the writer, reviewer, and reviser agents, ensuring that feedback and contextual knowledge are preserved across iterative cycles. Furthermore, the Agentic RAG paradigm introduces a specialized architectural layer where the writer agent's generation is grounded in dynamically retrieved evidence from external knowledge sources, while the reviewer and reviser agents validate the integration and relevance of this evidence [14]. This creates a more reliable and auditable knowledge foundation for the final report. Ultimately, the chosen architectural pattern—be it a tightly-coupled MAS, a flexible ensemble of LLM-based agents, or an evidence-anchored Agentic RAG system—determines the system's capacity for scalable role specialization, the efficiency of inter-agent communication, and the foundational reliability of the collaborative output.

### 3.2 Operationalization in Autonomous AI-Agent Workflows

The operationalization of the writer, reviewer, and reviser roles within fully autonomous AI-agent workflows represents a critical frontier in automating high-quality intelligence production. In these pipelines, the dynamic execution and coordination of specialized agents are governed by sophisticated collaboration protocols and task sequencing logic, which directly influence the efficacy of the final output. The core architectural principle involves decomposing the monolithic task of report generation into a sequential or iterative process where distinct agent instances, each embodying one of the tripartite roles, operate on a shared task context [22]. For instance, a writer agent may first draft content based on retrieved evidence, a reviewer agent then evaluates this draft against criteria like factual accuracy and logical coherence, and finally, a reviser agent incorporates the feedback to produce a refined version. This formalized division enables systematic quality control, as noted in the context of outbreak analytics, where multiple review steps before presenting an answer can yield higher-quality outputs than direct prompting [2].

Effective coordination between these autonomous agents relies on predefined communication protocols and interaction mechanisms. Research on multi-agent systems emphasizes the importance of coordination mechanisms to manage distributed, cross-platform communications and ensure accurate responding [19]. In autonomous workflows, this often translates to structured message-passing frameworks where agents exchange not only content (e.g., a draft report) but also meta-information such as confidence scores, identified issues, or revision directives. Task sequencing can be linear or involve complex loops; for example, a reviser's output may be sent back to the reviewer for a secondary evaluation, creating an iterative refinement cycle that enhances consistency. The design of these protocols must account for potential failures, such as communication breakdowns or goal misalignment, which are systemic risks in multi-agent coordination.

Quality control is embedded directly into the workflow through the reviewer's evaluative function and the reviser's corrective action. The reviewer agent acts as an automated validator, employing checks for hallucinations, internal contradictions, and adherence to formatting guidelines. The operationalization of this role can leverage techniques from decision support systems, where agents analyze data and evaluate alternatives [3]. Furthermore, to optimize performance, some systems implement learning mechanisms where agents adapt their behaviors based on historical interaction outcomes, thereby improving collaboration efficiency over time [2]. However, a key challenge in fully automated pipelines is ensuring that these internal quality control mechanisms are robust enough to catch subtle errors without human oversight, as the propagation of unchecked biases or errors from one agent to another remains a significant risk. The operational success of such autonomous tripartite systems thus hinges on the careful design of interaction protocols, fault-tolerant sequencing, and embedded validation checks that collectively aim to replicate and scale the rigorous human editorial process.

### 3.3 Implementation in Hybrid Human-AI Collaborative Workflows

The implementation of the writer-reviewer-reviser paradigm within hybrid human-AI collaborative workflows represents a critical evolution from purely automated systems, focusing on the strategic integration and handoff mechanisms between human actors and AI agents. This hybrid model seeks to leverage the scalability and computational power of AI while retaining essential human judgment, oversight, and ethical accountability. A central framework for governing this integration is the "Human-in-Command" (HIC) model, which positions human operators with ultimate authority and responsibility, ensuring that AI augmentation does not lead to automation complacency or a diffusion of accountability [7]. The HIC framework mandates clear operational triggers for strategic handoffs between agents and human overseers, particularly for high-stakes decisions or when the AI system encounters ambiguity or operates near its confidence boundaries.

Integration mechanisms vary based on the assigned role. For instance, an AI writer agent may generate a draft report from structured data, which is then passed to a human reviewer for substantive assessment of analytical soundness and strategic relevance. Conversely, a human writer might produce an initial assessment, which an AI reviewer agent then scans for logical inconsistencies, factual inaccuracies, or missed data patterns, flagging issues for human attention. The reviser role often becomes a collaborative space, where AI suggests edits for clarity or coherence, and a human integrator makes the final synthesis. Effective handoffs require robust interfaces that provide context, transparency into the AI's reasoning, and clear presentation of uncertainties [23]. This visibility is crucial for maintaining human situational awareness and trust, which are mediators of workflow efficacy.

The governance and oversight structures in these workflows are paramount. As noted in discussions on AI agent deployment, establishing necessary governance structures is essential to prevent misuse or unintended consequences, as misinterpretations could lead to erroneous conclusions with serious impacts [2]. Synergistic interaction is achieved when the system manages cognitive load effectively, allowing human experts to focus on tasks requiring nuanced interpretation, creativity, or ethical reasoning—areas where AI currently falls short. This aligns with the observation that in outbreak analytics, human skill and responsibility remain key for sufficiently nuanced interpretation, and AI agents should create an environment where skilled staff can focus more on these complex problems [2].

Ultimately, successful implementation hinges on designing workflows that are more than merely sequential. They should be interactive and iterative, fostering a dialogue where human feedback continuously refines agent performance and where agents, in turn, augment human cognitive capabilities. This collaborative loop enhances the final intelligence report's quality across dimensions of accuracy, credibility, and actionability, embodying a move towards a holistic "Humanity-in-the-Loop" approach that preserves human decisional ownership [7].

### 3.4 Optimization and Performance of Role-Based Agentic Systems

The optimization and performance evaluation of role-based agentic systems are critical for ensuring that the theoretical benefits of the writer-reviewer-reviser paradigm translate into practical gains in intelligence report quality, efficiency, and scalability. This involves methodologies for both enhancing the collaborative process and rigorously assessing its outcomes. A primary optimization challenge lies in dynamically coordinating the specialized agents to balance competing objectives such as report accuracy, generation speed, and computational resource consumption. Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) has emerged as a powerful framework for addressing this challenge. In MARL, each agent (e.g., writer, reviewer) learns optimal policies through interaction with the environment and other agents, aiming to maximize a collective reward signal. For instance, research applying a deep-Q-network model in a multi-agent reinforcement learning setting to guide scheduling demonstrates its capability to seek correlated equilibrium between conflicting objectives like completion time and cost without relying on prior expert knowledge [24]. This approach is directly applicable to orchestrating agentic workflows, where the "cost" could represent the cognitive or computational load of iterative revisions, and "completion time" equates to the timeliness of the final intelligence product.

Workflow scheduling algorithms constitute another core optimization methodology, particularly for managing task sequencing and resource allocation among the specialized agents. The efficiency of the entire tripartite process depends on intelligent scheduling that minimizes bottlenecks. Agent-based decision support systems often employ optimization algorithms to enhance this coordination [3]. For example, studies on cross-enterprise collaborative production scheduling utilize genetic algorithms within an agent structure to significantly reduce scheduling time and improve load balance, principles that can be adapted to sequence analysis, review, and revision tasks in a pipeline [25]. The performance of these optimized systems is measured against multi-dimensional quality metrics defined earlier (e.g., accuracy, consistency, actionability). Evaluation must also consider systemic efficiency, such as the reduction in human analyst cognitive load and the speed of generating actionable reports. The use of specialized LLM-based agents formalizes team interactions, enabling optimization of workflows and quality control to enhance consistency and reproducibility, which is a key performance advantage over direct LLM prompting [2].

Furthermore, optimization extends to improving the individual capabilities of agents within their specialized roles through continuous learning. Techniques like federated or collaborative machine learning can be employed to allow agents to improve their strategies (e.g., a reviewer agent getting better at detecting factual inconsistencies) in a decentralized manner, as seen in architectures designed for energy-efficient edge networks [26]. Similarly, cooperative multi-agent deep reinforcement learning methods have been shown to achieve optimal resource allocation strategies with low complexity in complex systems like satellite networks, a principle applicable to allocating "attention" or "verification resources" within an agent team [27]. Ultimately, the performance of a role-based agentic system is not solely the sum of its parts but a product of the optimized integration and adaptive coordination between the writer, reviewer, and reviser agents, governed by intelligent scheduling and learning mechanisms.

### 3.5 Challenges and Enablers in Practical Deployment

Deploying robust writer-reviewer-reviser agent systems in real-world intelligence production environments presents a suite of intertwined technical and operational challenges, alongside key enabling solutions. A primary technical hurdle is the inherent brittleness and unpredictable failure modes of LLM agents when interacting with complex, dynamic environments, which can severely degrade report quality. As highlighted in [28], agent-environment interaction failures—such as issues with observability or action execution—are a major source of low success rates. This is particularly critical in intelligence contexts where data sources are volatile and tasks are ill-defined. Furthermore, achieving seamless coordination and information exchange among specialized agents without central oversight remains difficult, risking communication breakdowns and goal misalignment that can propagate errors through the writing-reviewing-revising chain [3]. Operationally, integrating these autonomous workflows into existing human-centric analytical processes poses significant adoption barriers, including managing the cognitive load for human overseers and establishing appropriate governance structures for hybrid collaboration [1].

Key enablers are emerging to address these deployment challenges. To mitigate agent-environment failures, environment optimizations such as enhancing system observability and offloading common computations can significantly improve agent success rates without modifying the core LLM, as demonstrated by [28]. For coordination, implementing robust multi-agent architectures with clear communication protocols and collaborative learning mechanisms is essential. Frameworks like [14] show how structured agentic workflows can enhance consistency and reproducibility, which are directly transferable to intelligence reporting. To facilitate human-AI integration, the Human-in-Command (HIC) model and similar governance frameworks provide a pathway for maintaining human oversight while leveraging agent autonomy. The cautious, pilot-based approach advocated for integrating LLM-based agents into outbreak analytics pipelines, as discussed in [2], underscores the need for iterative validation, staff training, and the development of trust through transparency. Finally, leveraging digital twin environments for simulation and testing, as seen in industrial automation contexts [22], offers a powerful enabler for stress-testing agentic report-generation workflows before live deployment, ensuring robustness against real-world complexity.

## 4 Mechanisms and Factors Influencing Report Quality

### 4.1 Role Specialization as a Mechanism for Error Reduction and Accuracy Enhancement

Role specialization within the writer-reviewer-reviser paradigm serves as a foundational mechanism for enhancing the accuracy and reliability of intelligence reports by instituting systematic, multi-stage checks that mitigate individual errors, cognitive biases, and model hallucinations. The core principle is that separating the creative generation of content (writing) from its critical evaluation (reviewing) and subsequent correction (revising) creates a workflow where each role acts as a filter for the errors introduced by the previous one. In human-centric processes, this division is a long-standing quality control practice. When transposed to AI-agentic and hybrid systems, this structured separation becomes crucial for managing the inherent limitations of large language models (LLMs), such as their propensity to generate plausible but incorrect or biased information—a phenomenon widely documented as hallucination [29].

In autonomous multi-agent systems, this specialization is explicitly engineered to reduce hallucination risks. For instance, one paper presents a system where dedicated LLM-based agents, each with a defined role, work in concert to handle customer requests, integrating fuzzy logic to cross-verify outputs [30]. The reviewer agent in such a setup is tasked with fact-checking and consistency validation against source data or knowledge bases, directly targeting inaccuracies produced by the writer agent. Similarly, the application of specialized LLM-based agents in outbreak analytics pipelines is advocated precisely because formalized role interactions allow for more review steps before a final output is presented, thereby enhancing consistency and quality over direct, single-prompt interactions with an LLM [2]. This iterative, role-based review process is a direct mechanism for error reduction.

Furthermore, role specialization helps counteract decision-making biases that can infiltrate analytical reports. Research on LLM agents in operational contexts, such as inventory management, reveals that these agents can exhibit biases analogous to human ones, like the pull-to-centre effect [31]. A reviewer or reviser role, potentially operating with a different prompt profile, access to counterfactual data, or a specific debiasing instruction, can identify and correct for such skewed judgments in a draft report. The separation of functions prevents a single agent's latent biases from becoming the unchallenged foundation of the final product. In hybrid human-AI workflows, the human reviewer or reviser brings critical oversight, applying domain expertise and contextual understanding to catch subtle errors or misalignments that purely automated checks might miss, thereby adding a crucial layer of accuracy enhancement [9].

Ultimately, the efficacy of this mechanism hinges on the independence and distinct operational parameters of each role. If the agents are too similar or share the same underlying biases, the review process may fail to catch systemic errors. However, when properly configured, the tripartite division creates a robust system of checks and balances. It transforms error correction from a passive hope into an active, structured process, making accuracy enhancement a built-in feature of the intelligence production workflow rather than an external afterthought.

### 4.2 The Role of Feedback Loops and Iterative Refinement in Ensuring Consistency

The structured interaction between the writer, reviewer, and reviser roles creates a dynamic system of feedback loops and iterative refinement that is fundamental for ensuring the logical coherence and factual alignment of intelligence reports. This cyclical process transforms a linear production pipeline into a recursive quality assurance mechanism. In human-centric workflows, this manifests as formal review rounds and editorial revisions. In AI-agentic and hybrid systems, this principle is computationally instantiated through protocols that govern inter-agent communication and human-AI handoffs, establishing systematic cycles of generation, evaluation, and correction. The formalization of these interactions is a key advantage of using specialized LLM-based agents, as it enables the optimization of workflows and quality control to enhance consistency and reproducibility [2]. By having the output of one agent (e.g., the writer) serve as the input for another (e.g., the reviewer), and subsequently for a third (the reviser), the system embeds multiple checkpoints that progressively refine the report.

These iterative cycles directly target core dimensions of consistency. Logical coherence is enhanced as the reviewer agent analyzes the narrative flow, argument structure, and internal validity of claims, providing feedback that the reviser agent uses to reorganize content and clarify reasoning. Factual alignment is strengthened through a similar process, where the reviewer cross-references assertions against source data or knowledge bases, flagging inconsistencies or hallucinations for the reviser to correct. This multi-step review process before presenting a final output yields a higher-quality result than single-step generation [2]. In complex domains like healthcare or finance, where accuracy is paramount, such iterative refinement allows agentic systems to integrate diverse data sources and contextually refine outputs [32]. The reviser role acts as the crucial integrator of feedback, synthesizing disparate critiques into a unified, polished document. In hybrid Human-AI collaborative workflows, these loops extend to include human oversight, where a human-in-command provides strategic feedback at critical junctures, guiding the AI agents' refinement process toward alignment with broader organizational goals and nuanced contextual understanding [7]. Ultimately, the efficacy of these feedback loops depends on the clarity of the communication protocols between roles and the defined criteria for what constitutes an acceptable iteration, ensuring that the refinement cycle converges on a consistent and high-quality final report.

### 4.3 Mediating Factors: Trust, Transparency, and Cognitive Load

The effectiveness of the writer-reviewer-reviser paradigm in intelligence production is not solely determined by its structural design but is critically mediated by three interdependent human-centric factors: trust in the AI agents, the transparency of their operations, and the cognitive load imposed on human overseers. These factors act as crucial moderators, determining whether the theoretical benefits of role specialization translate into tangible improvements in report quality.

Trust is the foundational enabler for effective human-AI collaboration. In a tripartite system, human analysts must trust that the AI writer accurately synthesizes information, that the AI reviewer reliably identifies errors, and that the AI reviser appropriately incorporates feedback. Without this trust, human overseers are likely to engage in excessive verification, undermining workflow efficiency, or conversely, exhibit dangerous automation complacency. Research on conversational agents highlights that user satisfaction and effective use are closely tied to trust, which can be eroded by perceived opacity in decision-making processes [33]. This underscores the need for systems that actively build and maintain trust to mediate successful collaboration.

Transparency directly feeds into trust and is essential for managing cognitive load. When the internal reasoning, data sources, and decision pathways of each AI agent are opaque, human reviewers and commanders struggle to perform meaningful oversight, leading to high cognitive strain as they attempt to infer an agent's logic. Conversely, explainable operations allow humans to quickly verify outputs and understand discrepancies. Studies in clinical settings demonstrate that multi-agent systems designed with transparency modules can achieve high prediction accuracy while maintaining explainability scores, facilitating human validation [34]. In the context of the tripartite model, transparency regarding why a reviewer flagged an issue or how a reviser resolved a conflict is vital for efficient human-in-the-loop governance.

Finally, cognitive load must be strategically managed. The Human-in-Command (HIC) framework emphasizes that oversight is only meaningful if the human's cognitive capacity is not overwhelmed by the volume or complexity of agent interactions [7]. A well-designed role-based system should reduce cognitive load by delegating routine verification and editing to agents, freeing human intelligence for high-level synthesis and judgment. However, poor interface design or a lack of synthesized agent outputs can scatter human attention, increasing load and causing oversight failures. Therefore, the mediation of report quality hinges on designing interactions that present information from the writer, reviewer, and reviser in a coherent, integrated manner that supports, rather than burdens, human cognition.

### 4.4 The Impact of Task Complexity and Governance Structures on Workflow Efficacy

The efficacy of a writer-reviewer-reviser workflow in intelligence production is not static; it is profoundly shaped by two interdependent variables: the inherent complexity of the analytical task and the governance structure that orchestrates the roles. Task complexity, ranging from routine data summarization to forecasting under deep uncertainty, dictates the requisite depth of specialization, iteration, and oversight within the tripartite system. For simpler, well-structured tasks, a linear or lightly iterative workflow with clear, rule-based role boundaries may suffice for efficiency. However, as tasks grow more complex—characterized by ambiguous data, conflicting sources, and high-stakes implications—the workflow must evolve. Complex tasks demand more intensive, non-linear collaboration and iterative refinement cycles between roles to mitigate cognitive biases and integrate disparate perspectives [11]. This necessity for dynamic interaction is a core principle in Multi-Agent Based Decision Support Systems (MADSS), where agent coordination mechanisms must adapt to problem complexity to enable informed decisions by analyzing vast amounts of data and evaluating alternatives [3].

The chosen governance model provides the structural framework that enables or constrains this adaptation, directly determining the authority balance and interaction protocols among writers, reviewers, and revisers. In human-AI collaborative systems, governance structures like the Human-in-Command (HIC) model define the locus of final authority and oversight. For highly complex and consequential tasks, a governance model that places a human in a strong supervisory or final revision role may be essential to manage risk and ensure accountability, as highlighted in discussions on the cautious integration of LLM-based agents in high-stakes domains like outbreak analytics [2]. Conversely, for lower-complexity, high-volume tasks, a more autonomous multi-agent system with decentralized coordination might be optimal for scalability and speed. The design of such decentralized systems, where agents must cooperatively select actions to optimize global and local objectives, is exemplified in research on load-balancing for IoT services [35]. Furthermore, in industrial settings, agent-based reconfiguration mechanisms demonstrate how governance—defined through interaction protocols and negotiation mechanisms—can enhance system adaptability and robustness in dynamic production environments [36].

Ultimately, the optimal configuration emerges from aligning the governance structure with task demands. A mismatch, such as applying a rigid, hierarchical model to a rapidly evolving crisis scenario, can stifle the iterative refinement needed for quality. Conversely, excessive agent autonomy in a complex, ethically sensitive task without clear human oversight can lead to accountability diffusion and error propagation. Therefore, workflow efficacy is maximized when governance structures are explicitly designed to be reconfigurable, scaling the degree of role autonomy, the tightness of feedback loops, and the level of human integration in direct response to the dimensions of task complexity.

### 4.5 Systemic Risks and Quality Trade-offs in Automated Systems

While the tripartite role division in automated systems offers significant quality benefits, it also introduces systemic risks and inherent trade-offs that can negatively impact the final intelligence report. A primary concern is the **propagation and amplification of systemic bias**. When a writer agent generates content based on biased training data or flawed reasoning, a reviewer or reviser agent operating on similar foundational models or data may fail to detect and correct these errors, instead reinforcing them through iterative cycles. This creates a closed-loop system where biases are validated rather than challenged, leading to reports that are consistently skewed. The challenge of achieving meaningful oversight to break this cycle is highlighted in discussions of governance frameworks like Human-in-Command (HIC) [7]. Furthermore, the delegation of complex tasks to autonomous agents necessitates robust visibility into their operations to monitor for such bias propagation [23].

A second critical risk is **accountability diffusion**. The clear chain of responsibility present in human teams can become blurred in multi-agent systems. When a report contains an error, it can be challenging to attribute fault to a specific agent (writer, reviewer, or reviser) or to the human overseer, especially in complex, cascading workflows. This "responsibility gap" can undermine the credibility of the intelligence product and complicate remediation efforts. Frameworks for authenticated delegation aim to address this by maintaining clear chains of accountability [37], but practical implementation in dynamic agentic systems remains a challenge.

Finally, **over-reliance on automated workflows** presents a significant trade-off between efficiency and critical scrutiny. The seamless operation of a specialized multi-agent system can foster automation complacency, where human analysts may unduly trust the output, reducing their vigilance and depth of engagement. This is particularly dangerous in high-stakes domains like outbreak analytics or natural hazard assessment, where nuanced interpretation is essential [2; 38]. The system's efficiency gains can be offset by a degradation in the human's situational awareness and ability to catch subtle, context-dependent errors that agents may miss. Therefore, the design of these systems must carefully balance automation with mechanisms that force meaningful human engagement and critical evaluation at strategic points to mitigate these systemic risks.

## 5 Challenges, Ethical Considerations, and Limitations

### 5.1 Technical and Operational Limitations

The implementation of writer, reviewer, and reviser roles within AI-agent systems introduces a suite of inherent technical and operational limitations that can directly constrain the quality and reliability of the final intelligence product. A primary and pervasive challenge is the risk of model hallucination, where AI agents generate plausible but incorrect or fabricated information. While multi-agent architectures are often proposed to mitigate this risk through cross-verification [30], the fundamental lack of real-world understanding in LLMs means hallucinations can propagate through the collaborative chain if multiple agents share a common flawed inference or training bias. This issue is critically highlighted in high-stakes domains like healthcare, where unpredictable errors pose significant patient risks [39]. Furthermore, role-based AI systems exhibit brittleness, where their performance degrades significantly when faced with inputs or task sequences outside their training distribution or predefined workflow logic. This fragility is exacerbated in complex, dynamic environments like outbreak analytics or logistics, where novel situations require adaptive reasoning beyond rigid protocols [2; 40].

Operationally, the coordination between specialized agents can lead to unpredictable error patterns that are difficult to diagnose and rectify. Communication breakdowns, goal misalignment, or inconsistent internal states among writer, reviewer, and reviser agents can produce cascading failures, undermining the very quality checks the division of labor is meant to ensure. The challenge of agent coordination and handling uncertainty is a recognized hurdle in Multi-Agent-Based Decision Support Systems (MADSS) [3]. Additionally, the "black-box" nature of many advanced models creates an opacity that conflicts with the need for transparency and auditability in intelligence production. Even with explainable AI modules, as suggested in frameworks for credit risk [6], providing coherent, end-to-end explanations for decisions shaped by multiple interacting agents remains a significant technical hurdle. Finally, these systems face substantial operational burdens related to computational resource demands, latency in multi-step iterative loops, and the complexity of maintaining consistency across distributed agent memories or knowledge bases, which can impede timeliness—a key dimension of report quality.

### 5.2 Systemic and Coordination Failures

The delegation of intelligence production tasks to a multi-agent system based on the writer-reviewer-reviser paradigm introduces significant risks of systemic and coordination failures. These failures stem from the inherent complexities of orchestrating interactions between autonomous or semi-autonomous agents, potentially undermining the very quality gains the role division seeks to achieve. A primary risk is communication breakdown, where agents fail to exchange information accurately or completely. In complex workflows, the output of one agent (e.g., the writer's draft) serves as the critical input for the next (e.g., the reviewer). Ambiguities, loss of contextual nuance, or format mismatches in this handoff can propagate errors. Formal coordination mechanisms are essential to mitigate this, as highlighted in research on [19], which emphasizes the need for validated interaction protocols to ensure accurate cross-entity communication. Without robust, mathematically verifiable coordination, the system's coherence degrades.

Goal misalignment presents another critical failure mode. While each agent may be optimized for its specific sub-task (writing, reviewing, revising), their local objectives may not perfectly align with the global goal of producing a high-quality, actionable intelligence report. For instance, a reviewer agent overly focused on grammatical correctness might neglect substantive logical flaws, or agents may develop conflicting interpretations of evaluation criteria. This misalignment can lead to unproductive cycles of revision or the acceptance of suboptimal outputs. The architecture of multi-agent systems must therefore incorporate mechanisms for ensuring goal congruence, a challenge noted in discussions of [3], where agent coordination and trust are identified as key hurdles.

Perhaps the most severe risk is that of cascading errors, where a small initial mistake or bias is amplified through the iterative review and revision loop. If a writer agent introduces a factual hallucination based on a flawed data retrieval step, and the reviewer agent fails to detect it due to a complementary blind spot, the reviser may then entrench the error by refining the prose around it. This creates a systemic fragility where the collaborative process, designed to catch errors, instead legitimizes them. The potential for such cascades underscores the warnings in [2], which cautions that misinterpretation by automated systems can lead to erroneous conclusions with serious consequences. Furthermore, in highly autonomous workflows, coordination failures can become opaque, making root-cause analysis difficult and diffusing accountability. Effective governance and human oversight frameworks, such as those discussed in [7], are therefore not merely ethical imperatives but technical necessities to monitor for and interrupt these systemic coordination failures before they compromise report integrity.

### 5.3 Bias Propagation and Amplification

The division of labor among writer, reviewer, and reviser agents, while designed to enhance quality, creates structured pathways for the systematic propagation and potential amplification of biases. These biases, originating from training data, model architecture, or prompt design, can be reinforced rather than corrected through multi-agent interactions, posing a significant threat to the objectivity and credibility of intelligence reports. A primary risk is that biases embedded in a foundational LLM can become endemic across all specialized roles. For instance, if the "writer" agent generates content based on skewed data representations, a "reviewer" agent operating with similar underlying assumptions may fail to flag these issues, and a "reviser" agent might further entrench them during refinement [29]. This creates a closed-loop system where biases are validated and normalized through simulated consensus.

The problem is exacerbated in decision-making contexts where agents must interpret uncertain information. Research on LLM agents in operational settings shows they can exhibit human-like decision-making biases, such as the framing effect or pull-to-centre bias [31]. In a tripartite workflow, such cognitive biases could be compounded. For example, a writer's initial framing of an intelligence scenario might influence the reviewer's evaluation criteria, leading to a narrow revision that amplifies the initial skewed perspective. This mirrors challenges observed in Multi-Agent Based Decision Support Systems (MADSS), where achieving coherent agent coordination is paramount but does not inherently guarantee unbiased outcomes [3]. The iterative feedback loops intended for quality control can, under certain conditions, simply reinforce a shared biased worldview.

Furthermore, bias propagation is closely linked to issues of trust and transparency within the agentic system. If human overseers develop excessive trust in an apparently rigorous multi-stage process, they may lower their critical guard, allowing amplified biases to reach the final report unchallenged. This is particularly dangerous when agents interact with complex, real-world data. Case studies in geoscientific domains highlight that LLM-driven agents, while powerful for synthesis, can introduce interpretation biases based on data provenance or prompt sensitivity, which may cascade through an analytical pipeline [38]. Similarly, in domains reliant on networked information and social dynamics, the mechanisms of trust propagation and opinion dynamics can be modeled within agent systems, potentially leading to the amplification of specific viewpoints at the expense of others [41].

Mitigating this risk requires moving beyond architectural role separation to incorporate explicit debiasing mechanisms. Potential strategies include employing heterogeneous agent models trained on distinct datasets to break homogeneity, implementing adversarial review protocols where agents are specifically prompted to challenge assumptions, and designing meta-supervision mechanisms to audit the workflow for bias drift. Without such safeguards, the very structure meant to ensure report quality risks institutionalizing bias at a systemic level.
