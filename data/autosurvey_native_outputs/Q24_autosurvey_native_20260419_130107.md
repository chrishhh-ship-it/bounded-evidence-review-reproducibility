# The Role of Evidence Agents in Multi-Agent AI Systems: A Survey on Architectural Independence versus Integration with Writer Agents

## 1 Introduction: Defining Evidence Agents and Writer Agents in AI Systems

### 1.1 The Rise of Agentic AI and Specialized Roles in Multi-Agent Systems

The paradigm shift towards agentic AI represents a fundamental evolution in artificial intelligence, moving from static models to dynamic systems where autonomous agents perform complex, goal-oriented tasks with a degree of independence. This shift is driven by the limitations of monolithic AI systems, which often struggle with adaptability, scalability, and multifaceted problem-solving. In contrast, agentic frameworks decompose complex objectives into subtasks managed by specialized, collaborative entities. This approach is exemplified by the growing adoption of Multi-Agent Systems (MAS), where multiple intelligent agents interact within a shared environment to achieve common or individual goals, offering significant advantages in adaptability, flexibility, and scalability [1]. The rise of such systems is evident across diverse domains, from automated customer service, where multiple agents cooperate to manage complex, multi-turn dialogues [2], to industrial automation, where large language model (LLM)-based agents enable intelligent planning and control of flexible production processes [3].

Within these sophisticated MAS architectures, the principle of **specialization** has become paramount. Just as human teams divide labor based on expertise, agentic systems increasingly delegate specific functions to dedicated agent roles to optimize overall performance, reliability, and trustworthiness. This specialization mitigates the cognitive overload and error risks associated with a single agent attempting to master all aspects of a complex workflow. For instance, in high-stakes domains like outbreak analytics, the synthesis of real-time, policy-relevant evidence requires the coordination of multiple distinct analytical techniques and data sources. Here, the use of specialized LLM-based agents formalizes team interactions, optimizes workflows, and enhances consistency and reproducibility, allowing for simultaneous task execution and more rigorous review steps [4]. Similarly, in networking, the vision for agentic AI in 6G involves creating an ecosystem of diverse autonomous agents that collaborate to improve performance and enable new services, moving beyond passive learning frameworks [5].

It is within this context of increasing complexity and a demand for robust, trustworthy AI that specialized roles like the **evidence agent** and **writer agent** have distinctly emerged. The evidence agent role crystallizes around the critical, epistemologically rigorous functions of information acquisition, validation, and synthesis—tasks demanding strict attention to source credibility, traceability, and logical reasoning. Conversely, the writer agent specializes in the rhetorically focused tasks of narrative construction, content generation, and audience-aware communication. Their emergence is a direct response to the need for systems that can not only generate outputs but also ground those outputs in verifiable information and communicate them effectively. This specialization reflects a broader architectural trend towards modularity, where agents with defined responsibilities interact through clear protocols, as seen in frameworks supporting context-aware policy composition [6] and those addressing authorization and accountability for autonomous agents [7]. The subsequent debate on whether these two roles should remain architecturally independent or be functionally merged is thus fundamentally rooted in this foundational shift towards agentic AI and the consequent drive for role-based specialization within collaborative multi-agent ecosystems.

### 1.2 Core Definitions: Evidence Agents vs. Writer Agents

At the heart of the architectural debate lies the precise functional distinction between evidence agents and writer agents within multi-agent AI systems. This distinction directly operationalizes the principle of specialization introduced earlier, crystallizing into two distinct roles with complementary yet fundamentally different mandates.

An **evidence agent** is a specialized autonomous entity whose primary mandate is the rigorous, epistemologically-focused management of information to support fact-based reasoning. Its core functions are centered on the acquisition, validation, synthesis, and curation of data from diverse sources. This involves targeted information retrieval from structured databases or unstructured corpora, critical assessment of source credibility and relevance, and the synthesis of multi-modal evidence into a coherent, traceable knowledge base. The agent's output is not final narrative content but rather validated, structured evidence intended to inform downstream decision-making or content creation processes. This role is crucial in domains requiring high-stakes, auditable decisions, such as in agentic systems for medical decision support [8] or in generating robust workflows for climate services [9]. The design of such agents often incorporates mechanisms for maintaining provenance and enabling evidence-based reasoning, positioning them as foundational modules for trust and accuracy within a collaborative ecosystem.

In contrast, a **writer agent** is a specialized entity tasked with the rhetorically-focused generation, structuring, and communication of content. Its core responsibilities involve transforming inputs—which may include raw data, synthesized evidence from an evidence agent, or high-level instructions—into coherent, stylistically appropriate narratives. This encompasses tasks such as summarization, report generation, narrative structuring, and the adaptation of tone, style, and complexity for specific audiences. The writer agent's strength lies in linguistic fluency, rhetorical organization, and the ability to craft compelling communications from provided information. Its role is prominently featured in systems designed to automate analytical reporting, as seen in pipelines for outbreak analytics where generating situational updates is a key bottleneck [4], or in creating developer-aligned test scenarios in software engineering [10].

The fundamental divergence is thus between *evidence handling* and *content generation*. While an evidence agent is concerned with the epistemological rigor of the information substrate—asking "what is known and how do we know it?"—a writer agent focuses on the rhetorical effectiveness of the derived narrative—asking "how is this knowledge best communicated?" This clear functional dichotomy, establishing separate loci for factual integrity and communicative efficacy, directly sets the stage for the subsequent debate on architectural separation versus integration. The distinct cognitive processes, required competencies, and failure modes for ensuring factual fidelity versus narrative coherence form the substantive core of that design decision.

### 1.3 The Architectural Debate: Independence versus Integration

Building upon the clear functional distinction between evidence handling and content generation established in the previous section, the architectural design of multi-agent AI systems presents a fundamental and consequential debate: should evidence agents exist as architecturally independent modules, or should their functions be merged into writer agents? This question is central to the design of robust, trustworthy, and efficient agentic systems. Proponents of architectural independence argue that a dedicated evidence agent, as a specialized entity focused solely on information retrieval, validation, and synthesis, is essential for ensuring the integrity and traceability of the knowledge base upon which decisions and narratives are built. This separation of concerns aligns with foundational principles of modularity and specialization in Multi-Agent Systems, as discussed in literature on [1]. Such a design enhances system transparency, allows for rigorous validation processes, and is particularly critical in high-stakes domains where evidence quality directly impacts outcomes, such as in [4]. An independent evidence agent can act as a verifiable source of truth, mitigating risks associated with hallucinations or uncorroborated data being passed to the writer agent.

Conversely, the integrationist perspective advocates for merging evidence-handling capabilities directly into the writer agent to create a more unified and streamlined architecture. This approach is often motivated by desires for operational efficiency, reduced latency in task execution, and simplified agent coordination. In applications where speed and workflow optimization are paramount, such as in dynamic [2], a single, more capable agent might be preferable to manage the end-to-end process from information gathering to response formulation. Integration can minimize communication overhead and potentially allow for more nuanced, context-aware generation where evidence curation and narrative construction are deeply intertwined.

The implications of this architectural choice are profound, extending beyond mere software design to impact system performance, scalability, security, and trust—key themes that will be systematically evaluated in the subsequent methodological analysis of this survey. An independent model may offer superior auditability and facilitate compliance with regulatory standards by clearly delineating the evidence pipeline, a concern highlighted in studies on user trust like [11]. However, it may introduce complexity and coordination challenges. A merged architecture, while potentially more efficient, risks creating opaque, monolithic agents where evidence validation steps are obscured, compromising transparency and making error diagnosis difficult. This debate is therefore not merely technical but touches on core requirements of the application domain, whether it demands the rigorous, traceable evidence handling of a clinical decision support system or the rapid, adaptive response of a network orchestration agent as envisioned in [12].

## 2 Theoretical and Architectural Foundations

### 2.1 Foundational Theories of Specialization in Multi-Agent Systems

The theoretical foundations of multi-agent systems (MAS) provide a compelling rationale for the architectural independence of evidence agents from writer agents, rooted in the core principles of specialization, modularity, and autonomy. MAS theory posits that complex problems are best addressed by a collective of autonomous, specialized entities rather than a monolithic intelligence, leveraging advantages in adaptability, flexibility, and scalability [1]. This specialization enables heterogeneous agents to focus on distinct sub-tasks within a collaborative workflow. The principle is operationalized through modularity, which decomposes a system into well-defined, interchangeable components—each encapsulating specific knowledge and functions. This separation of concerns simplifies design, enhances maintainability, and allows individual agents to be updated or replaced without overhauling the entire architecture. Autonomy further empowers each specialized agent to control its own actions and internal state, enabling proactive operation toward designated goals. For an evidence agent, this autonomy is critical, allowing it to independently navigate information landscapes, validate sources, and synthesize findings without requiring continuous direction from a writer agent.

These theoretical constructs are embodied in role-based design, a prevalent MAS paradigm where agents are assigned specific roles defining their responsibilities, permissions, and interaction protocols. For example, a Multi-Agent-Based Decision Support System (MADSS) typically comprises distinct user, decision, and knowledge agents, each fulfilling a specialized function [1]. Such role-based specialization addresses cognitive and practical limits: it reduces the cognitive load on any single agent, minimizes error propagation across disparate functions, and permits deeper optimization within a narrow domain. The justification extends to decision support systems where integrating specialized knowledge sources is paramount. In clinical domains, for instance, multi-agent ontologies-based systems employ agents specialized in distinct fields—such as semiology or pharmacology—each managing its own knowledge base and ontological framework [16]. This modular, specialized approach is deemed essential for realizing robust MADSS, underscoring that evidence-intensive tasks benefit from dedicated agents focused solely on information acquisition, validation, and reasoning. Thus, MAS theory solidly supports the conception of evidence agents as architecturally independent entities, a foundation that seamlessly aligns with the recurring design patterns observed in practical implementations, as detailed in the following subsection.

### 2.2 Architectural Patterns for Evidence-Centric Agent Design

Building directly upon the theoretical principles of specialization and modularity in Multi-Agent Systems (MAS), the architectural independence of evidence agents is substantiated by several recurring design patterns in practical implementations. These patterns systematically position evidence agents as distinct, specialized entities within larger workflows, operationalizing their unique role in information acquisition, validation, and synthesis. A prominent pattern emerges from agentic Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems, particularly in high-stakes domains. For instance, in medical decision support, architectures often delineate a clear separation between agents responsible for evidence retrieval and validation and those handling report generation and communication [8]. This separation is crucial for ensuring clinical guidelines are adhered to and that decision-making frameworks remain interpretable and auditable. The evidence agent in such a design operates as a dedicated module that queries knowledge graphs, assesses source credibility, and synthesizes multi-modal data before passing curated evidence to a writer agent for narrative construction.

Another foundational pattern is derived from knowledge management frameworks for multi-agent systems. Here, the architecture explicitly defines specialized knowledge or information agents whose sole function is to manage, retrieve, and serve validated knowledge to other agents within the enterprise [17]. This design promotes scalability, reusability, and system adaptability by allowing the evidence-handling logic to be updated independently of the content generation modules, a direct application of MAS modularity. The automatic inter-agent communication protocols in such systems facilitate efficient knowledge dissemination, where an evidence agent acts as a centralized or federated source of truth.

Furthermore, the MAPE-K (Monitor-Analyze-Plan-Execute over a shared Knowledge base) control loop, a staple in autonomic computing, provides a robust architectural template that inherently supports evidence-centric agents. In complex network management scenarios, such as in Space-Air-Ground Integrated Networks (SAGIN), specific agents are designed as semantic resource perceivers and analyzers [18]. These agents continuously monitor the environment, analyze data, and populate a shared knowledge base with evidence about network states and intents. This pattern enforces a clear separation of concerns: evidence agents handle the "Monitor" and "Analyze" phases, focusing on data fidelity and situational awareness, while other agents manage planning and execution. Similarly, in proposed future Internet architectures like FlexNGIA 2.0, LLM-based agents are designed to autonomously perceive and reason about network conditions, effectively functioning as evidence agents that feed into decision-making processes for protocol design and traffic engineering [12].

Collectively, these recurring patterns demonstrate that architecturally distinct evidence agents are a practical and recurrent solution for ensuring rigor, traceability, and adaptability in complex, dynamic AI systems. This established architectural precedent directly informs and justifies the domain-specific functional specializations discussed next, where the unique demands of fields like healthcare and network management compel this separation.

### 2.3 Domain-Specific Motivations for Architectural Independence

The architectural independence of evidence agents is not merely a design preference but a critical requirement driven by the unique demands of various high-stakes domains. These domains necessitate specialized agents dedicated to evidence handling due to stringent needs for rigorous validation, comprehensive traceability, and the management of complex, often multimodal, data streams. This functional specialization directly builds upon the recurring architectural patterns discussed previously, which systematically position evidence agents as distinct entities. In healthcare, for instance, the consequences of erroneous information are profound. Agentic AI systems designed for diagnostics and clinical decision support must integrate and validate evidence from diverse sources such as medical records, lab results, and research literature [8]. A dedicated evidence agent ensures this validation process is systematic, traceable, and adheres to clinical guidelines, thereby mitigating risks like model "hallucinations" that could lead to patient harm [19]. The need for architectural separation is further underscored in outbreak analytics, where rapid, evidence-based responses are vital. Specialized agents can formalize workflows for synthesizing information from multiple distinct analytical techniques and sources, enhancing consistency, reproducibility, and quality control before narrative reporting [4].

Similarly, in technical domains like network management and root cause analysis (RCA) of software incidents, the complexity of diagnostic data necessitates an independent evidence-gathering module. RCA requires dynamically collecting and correlating diagnostic information from logs, metrics, and databases, a task ill-suited to a unified writer agent focused on narrative generation [20]. An independent evidence agent can systematically retrieve and validate this technical evidence, ensuring factual accuracy for subsequent analysis. This pattern extends to business and customer service operations, where governance frameworks like "Human-in-Command" (HIC) emphasize accountability and auditability [21]. A separate evidence agent creates a clear audit trail for data sourcing and decision logic, which is essential for compliance and ethical oversight. Furthermore, the principles of Multi-Agent Based Decision Support Systems (MADSS) highlight how specialized knowledge agents improve decision-making in complex environments by analyzing vast amounts of data under various criteria [1]. This functional specialization, where evidence agents act as dedicated knowledge curators, is crucial for maintaining system robustness, trust, and safety across healthcare, legal, financial, and operational domains, compelling their architectural independence from writer agents. The domain-specific demands for rigor and traceability thus provide a compelling rationale for this separation, a rationale that is technically enabled by the sophisticated knowledge representation and integration mechanisms discussed in the following section.

### 2.4 Knowledge Representation and Integration Mechanisms

Building directly upon the domain-specific imperatives for separation, the architectural independence of evidence agents is fundamentally enabled by sophisticated knowledge representation and integration mechanisms. These technical components provide the necessary infrastructure for evidence agents to operate as specialized modules, systematically retrieving, synthesizing, and validating evidence before it is passed to writer agents for narrative construction. A cornerstone of this capability is the use of structured knowledge representations, such as knowledge graphs and ontologies, which provide a semantic framework for organizing and querying complex information. For instance, systems like Microsoft Academic Services (MAS) leverage knowledge graphs assembled through natural language understanding and knowledge-assisted inference to capture scholarly communications at scale [22]. This structured representation allows an independent evidence agent to perform precise, context-aware retrieval and trace the provenance of information, a critical function for validation. Similarly, in clinical decision support, multi-agent systems utilize medical ontologies to enable cooperation between specialized agents operating on heterogeneous knowledge bases, ensuring semantic alignment and rigorous reasoning [16]. The Climate Service Recipes (HACID-CSR) system further exemplifies this, employing a domain knowledge graph for climate services (CS-DKG) to guide specialized agents in retrieving relevant subgraphs, thereby filling knowledge gaps and enhancing the traceability of generated workflows [9].

Beyond static knowledge structures, advanced integration mechanisms empower evidence agents to synthesize insights from distributed and privacy-sensitive data environments. Techniques like federated learning support evidence agents in curating evidence from disparate, secure repositories without centralizing raw data, a vital capability in domains like healthcare and network management. While not explicitly detailed in all provided papers, the principle aligns with the collaborative, decentralized learning approaches discussed for intelligent edge networks, where multi-agent systems coordinate to improve strategies without sharing raw data [23]. For an evidence agent, such mechanisms facilitate evidence synthesis from multiple federated nodes, preserving data confidentiality while building a comprehensive evidence base. Furthermore, the use of Graph Retrieval-Augmented Generation (GraphRAG), as seen in HACID-CSR, represents a key integration technique where an evidence agent combines retrieved subgraph context with an LLM's reasoning to generate enriched, evidence-backed content for subsequent stages [9]. Collectively, these representation and integration mechanisms provide the technical foundation that empowers evidence agents as independent entities. They ensure evidence quality, maintain rigorous audit trails, and handle complex, multi-modal data streams, thereby operationally justifying their architectural separation from writer agents, which are optimized for narrative fluency and stylistic coherence.

## 3 Functional Analysis: Core Responsibilities of Evidence and Writer Agents

### 3.1 Core Functions of Evidence Agents: Information Acquisition and Curation

Evidence agents constitute a specialized functional pillar within multi-agent AI systems, dedicated to the rigorous acquisition, validation, and curation of information to establish a reliable foundation for evidence-based reasoning. Their core mandate is distinct from content generation, focusing instead on ensuring the integrity and relevance of the knowledge upon which subsequent actions and communications are built. This specialized focus justifies their consideration as an independent architectural component.

The first critical function is **targeted information retrieval**. This extends beyond simple search to involve a nuanced understanding of the task context and specific evidentiary requirements. Evidence agents proactively query diverse, often domain-specific databases, knowledge graphs, and real-time data streams to gather pertinent information. In complex, dynamic domains such as outbreak analytics, this capability is paramount for synthesizing intelligence from disparate sources to generate timely, policy-relevant evidence [4]. Architectures like Agentic Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems formalize this role, employing dedicated agents to fetch and integrate relevant clinical or research data [8].

Following retrieval, the **validation of source credibility** represents a non-negotiable safeguard. Evidence agents must systematically assess the authority, timeliness, and potential biases of information sources. This is especially vital in high-stakes domains like healthcare and legal analysis, where erroneous data can lead to severe consequences. The agent cross-references facts, checks for consensus among reputable sources, and flags inconsistencies. This rigorous, methodical process is a core argument for architectural independence, as it requires a focused, objective approach insulated from the narrative and creative pressures inherent to content generation. The imperative for such validation is underscored in discussions on the cautious integration of LLMs, where preventing data misinterpretation is critical [4].

A third essential function is the **synthesis of multi-modal evidence**. Information is rarely homogeneous; it may encompass structured data (e.g., databases), unstructured text (e.g., research papers), and images or sensor data. Evidence agents are tasked with integrating these disparate data types into a coherent, contextualized evidence package. This synthesis often involves reasoning across modalities, such as linking textual findings with numerical trends or visual indicators. Frameworks like Multi-Agent Based Decision Support Systems (MADSS) utilize specialized knowledge agents for this purpose, analyzing vast amounts of heterogeneous data to inform decisions [1].

Finally, **maintaining traceability and provenance** is a foundational responsibility that underpins system trustworthiness. For outputs to be auditable and credible, it must be possible to trace any claim or conclusion back to its source evidence. Evidence agents meticulously log the provenance of all retrieved information, the steps taken in validation and synthesis, and any transformations applied. This creates a vital audit trail for debugging, performance improvement, and meeting regulatory or ethical standards in fields like finance and healthcare. This function directly supports transparency and accountability—objectives that are more challenging to achieve if evidence handling is deeply entangled with narrative construction within a single, monolithic writer agent.

In summary, through targeted retrieval, rigorous validation, multi-modal synthesis, and diligent provenance tracking, evidence agents establish the evidentiary bedrock. This clear functional separation sets the stage for the subsequent work of the writer agent, which transforms this curated evidence into coherent communication.

### 3.2 Core Functions of Writer Agents: Content Generation and Communication

Writer agents serve as the communicative and narrative engine within multi-agent AI systems, with their core responsibilities centered on transforming the curated evidence foundation—established by the preceding evidence agent—into coherent, structured, and audience-appropriate content. Their primary duty is **content generation and synthesis**, which encompasses a wide range of outputs from concise summaries and detailed analytical reports to comprehensive narratives and explanatory text. This involves not merely transcribing data but interpreting and synthesizing validated insights into a logical flow, structuring arguments, and ensuring the final output is both informative and accessible. For instance, in contexts like outbreak analytics, writer agents are tasked with generating regular situational updates and policy briefs from complex, evolving data, a process that requires distilling technical findings into actionable intelligence for decision-makers [4]. The ability to autonomously produce such written material is a key driver for operational efficiency, as highlighted in studies showing AI agents can accelerate service response times significantly [14].

Building upon this generative capacity, a critical function is **narrative structuring and communication adaptation**. Writer agents must organize information according to established rhetorical patterns—such as problem-solution frameworks or executive summaries—and tailor the tone, style, and complexity of the content for specific audiences. This requires sophisticated natural language capabilities to shift from technical jargon suitable for domain experts to plain-language explanations for the general public or stakeholders. The effectiveness of this adaptation directly impacts user trust and the practical utility of the system's output. Furthermore, writer agents often engage in **iterative refinement and collaborative integration**, a process that inherently links to the functional overlap discussed in subsequent analysis. They may polish drafts, ensure consistency, and align the message with overarching goals, frequently in a feedback loop where narrative needs guide further evidence queries and evidence synthesis informs narrative adjustments. In agentic systems designed for domains like network management or scientific research, the writer agent's role in clearly communicating designed protocols, analysis results, or system states is fundamental for human-in-the-loop oversight and actionable intelligence [12]. Ultimately, the writer agent bridges the gap between the evidence-based reasoning facilitated by its specialized counterpart and human comprehension, making it an indispensable component for knowledge dissemination and decision support within collaborative AI architectures.

### 3.3 Analysis of Functional Overlap and Synergy

While evidence agents and writer agents are defined by distinct primary functions—information acquisition and curation versus content generation and communication—their operational boundaries are not rigidly siloed. A critical analysis reveals significant functional overlap and interdependencies that necessitate tight coupling for effective task completion, particularly in complex, multi-step workflows. This overlap is most pronounced in tasks requiring iterative refinement, where evidence retrieval informs narrative structure, and narrative goals, in turn, guide subsequent evidence searches. For instance, in generating a comprehensive outbreak analytics report, the writer agent's initial draft may identify gaps in temporal data, prompting the evidence agent to perform targeted retrieval of time-series datasets [4]. Conversely, the evidence agent's synthesis of conflicting study results may require the writer agent to adapt its communication strategy to present nuanced conclusions clearly. This cyclical interaction exemplifies a synergistic feedback loop, where separation of roles does not imply isolation but rather enables a more disciplined and traceable collaboration.

The synergistic benefits of this collaboration are manifold. First, it enhances output quality and reliability. By having a dedicated evidence agent rigorously validate and synthesize information before passing it to the writer agent, the risk of the writer agent generating content based on unverified or hallucinated data is substantially reduced. This is crucial in domains like finance and legal analysis, where accuracy is paramount [13]. The multi-agent review process inherent in this separation adds a layer of quality control, as noted in the context of LLM-based agents improving consistency and reproducibility over direct prompting [4]. Second, this collaboration enables the handling of more complex tasks by decomposing them into specialized subtasks. A writer agent can focus on structuring a persuasive argument or tailoring language for a specific audience, while concurrently, an evidence agent mines relevant case law or financial precedents. This parallel processing, facilitated by clear interfaces between agents, can improve overall system efficiency and scalability for intricate problems.

However, this synergy relies on effective communication protocols and shared context. The overlap necessitates interfaces where evidence agents must not only pass raw data but also metadata concerning source credibility and uncertainty—information critical for the writer agent to appropriately qualify statements. Similarly, writer agents must communicate narrative constraints and audience needs to guide the evidence retrieval process. Architectures that formalize this interaction, such as those using shared knowledge bases or structured communication languages, are essential to realize the benefits. For example, in conversational agent systems, delegating intent recognition and context management to specialized agents allows for more robust dialogue [2]. The Manager-Customer-Service Dual-Agent paradigm further illustrates how shared, consistent knowledge bases enable reliable collaboration between agents with overlapping concerns in task execution [24]. Thus, the functional overlap does not argue for merging the agents into a monolithic entity but rather for designing sophisticated coordination mechanisms that leverage their specialized strengths while managing their necessary interdependencies.

### 3.4 Task Complexity and the Case for Specialization

Building upon the functional synergies and necessary interdependencies outlined previously, the argument for maintaining evidence agents as architecturally independent entities from writer agents is strongly reinforced by an analysis of task complexity, stakes, and domain-specific requirements. Complex tasks, particularly those in high-stakes domains, impose significant cognitive demands that are best managed through deep specialization, thereby reducing error risks and system failure. The core premise is that the cognitive processes for rigorous evidence handling—involving retrieval, validation, and synthesis from heterogeneous sources—are fundamentally distinct from those required for effective narrative construction and communication. Merging these disparate functions into a single agent risks overwhelming its operational capacity, leading to cognitive overload, which can manifest as oversimplification, overlooked data inconsistencies, or logical fallacies in the final output. This separation is crucial because the failure modes differ: an evidence agent's error might involve sourcing incorrect or biased data, while a writer agent's error might involve misrepresenting valid evidence through poor structuring or ambiguous language. Independent specialization allows each agent type to develop and optimize its unique cognitive stack—evidence agents for critical evaluation and traceability, writer agents for rhetorical coherence and audience adaptation—without cross-contaminating their respective failure probabilities.

The necessity for this architectural separation is vividly illustrated in domains like outbreak analytics and healthcare. In outbreak response, generating timely, policy-relevant evidence requires synthesizing multimodal data streams and applying diverse analytical techniques under extreme time pressure [4]. A unified agent attempting to both curate this complex evidence base and simultaneously draft a situational report would likely compromise on the depth of validation or the clarity of communication, with potentially severe public health consequences. Similarly, the application of domain-specific Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) necessitates agents that can meticulously follow complex, branching decision graphs for evidence-based actions, a function distinct from generating explanatory narratives [25]. Furthermore, in customer service and network management, the real-time, dynamic nature of tasks requires agents to coordinate interference or infer user intent from contextual behavior, processes that benefit from focused specialization rather than amalgamated responsibilities [2] [26].

Ultimately, architectural separation based on specialization directly addresses the trade-off between efficiency and robustness. While a merged agent might offer streamlined workflows for simple tasks, the cognitive load and error risks escalate non-linearly with task complexity. Maintaining independent evidence agents ensures a dedicated focus on the fidelity of the information foundation, which is a prerequisite for trustworthy content generation by writer agents. This division of labor not only enhances system reliability but also aligns with governance frameworks that require clear audit trails and validation checkpoints, particularly as emphasized in discussions on human oversight and trust in sensitive applications [21] [11]. This foundation of robust, specialized agents is essential for enabling the sophisticated coordination mechanisms discussed earlier, ensuring that collaboration is built upon a base of verifiable expertise and minimized systemic risk.

## 4 Arguments for and against Merging Evidence Agents with Writer Agents

### 4.1 Efficiency and Workflow Optimization: The Case for Integration

A primary argument for architecturally merging evidence agents with writer agents centers on achieving significant gains in operational efficiency and streamlined workflows. Proponents of integration contend that combining the information retrieval, validation, and synthesis functions of an evidence agent with the narrative construction and communication functions of a writer agent into a single, unified agent can reduce systemic latency and automate task coordination. This leads to faster end-to-end task completion, which is particularly compelling in domains where rapid response is critical. For instance, in outbreak analytics, the need for real-time evidence generation to inform public health policy creates bottlenecks when workflows involve multiple manual, time-intensive steps [4]. A unified agent could seamlessly transition from gathering and validating the latest epidemiological data to drafting a situational update report, thereby eliminating the overhead of inter-agent communication and data serialization/deserialization. This direct integration mirrors the efficiency gains observed in broader AI Agent implementations, where automating service processes can substantially accelerate response times [14].

Furthermore, integration facilitates automated task coordination within a single cognitive architecture. Instead of requiring explicit orchestration protocols to manage handoffs between separate agents, a merged agent can internally manage the sequence and dependencies of subtasks. This reduces overall system design complexity and can minimize failures due to miscommunication or synchronization errors between autonomous entities. The workflow becomes a continuous, internalized process within the agent's reasoning loop, a concept aligned with architectures for autonomous systems where a single intelligent agent orchestrates action sequences by directly interpreting interfaces and knowledge [3]. In such a model, the distinction between "gathering evidence" and "reporting on it" blurs into a singular goal-directed process. The result is a potentially leaner system with fewer moving parts, where computational and memory resources can be dynamically optimized for the monolithic task at hand rather than being statically partitioned between specialized modules. This architectural simplicity supports integration as a path to more efficient and responsive agentic systems, especially for well-defined, sequential tasks where the evidence-to-narrative pipeline is linear and tightly coupled.

### 4.2 Scalability and System Performance: Benefits of a Unified Architecture

Building upon the argument for operational efficiency, a unified architecture for evidence and writer agents also offers significant advantages for enhancing system scalability and overall performance. A primary benefit is the dynamic allocation of computational and memory resources. In a merged architecture, a single agent entity can internally prioritize and shift resources between evidence-gathering subtasks—such as data retrieval and validation—and content-generation subtasks—like narrative structuring and summarization—based on real-time demand. This fluid internal management occurs without the overhead of inter-agent communication protocols, leading to more efficient utilization of system capacity, particularly under variable workloads. For instance, during periods requiring intensive evidence synthesis, the agent can allocate more processing cycles to validation, subsequently reallocating focus to narrative generation once the evidence base is solidified. This intrinsic adaptability is crucial for scaling operations to handle large volumes of concurrent tasks, as observed in domains like automated customer service or large-scale data reporting [14].

Furthermore, a unified design inherently facilitates more streamlined parallel task execution. While specialized independent agents can operate in parallel, their collaboration necessitates explicit coordination, message passing, and synchronization, which introduces latency and potential bottlenecks. In contrast, a merged agent, operating with a shared internal state and objective, can orchestrate evidence collection and content drafting phases in a tightly coupled, pipelined manner. This reduces coordination overhead and can significantly decrease end-to-end task completion time. The efficiency gains from such an integrated workflow are highlighted in research on multi-agent systems for complex, dynamic environments, where minimizing communication latency is key to performance [1]. The potential for scalable, round-the-clock operation is also critical in contexts like outbreak analytics, where rapid, large-scale evidence synthesis and reporting are paramount [4].

From a system performance perspective, integration simplifies optimization. Developers can fine-tune a single, more complex model or a cohesive set of sub-modules for the combined task, rather than optimizing two separate agents for interaction. This holistic approach can lead to improvements in inference speed and reduce the system's aggregate memory footprint by eliminating redundant components. The concept of centralized learning for coordinated action, as discussed in multi-agent deep reinforcement learning frameworks for resource allocation, supports the performance benefits of a unified control strategy [27]. Ultimately, by reducing architectural complexity and inter-agent dependencies, a unified evidence-writer agent presents a more scalable and performant solution for applications where operational throughput and low latency are primary concerns. However, as the following discussion will reveal, these performance benefits must be carefully weighed against the significant security, privacy, and governance risks that such integration introduces.

### 4.3 Security, Privacy, and Governance Risks of Integration

While the previous section argues that a unified architecture enhances scalability and performance, such integration introduces significant security, privacy, and governance risks that can undermine system integrity and trust. A primary concern is the heightened vulnerability to privilege escalation attacks. In a merged agent, the boundaries between evidence retrieval—which may require access to sensitive or high-privilege data sources—and content generation become blurred. This creates a larger attack surface where malicious inputs or prompt injections could trick the single agent into performing unauthorized actions or leaking confidential information retrieved during its evidence phase. Research on securing LLM agents highlights that their runtime behavior, determined by natural language prompts, is inherently non-deterministic and vulnerable to such exploitation [28]. A merged architecture complicates the implementation of robust guardrails, as opposed to an independent evidence agent which can be isolated and subjected to stricter input/output validation and access controls.

Furthermore, data confidentiality and privacy breaches become more likely. An independent evidence agent can be designed with a principle of least privilege, accessing only the necessary data for validation and synthesis before passing curated evidence to the writer. Merging the roles risks exposing all internal data flows and retrieved raw materials—which may contain personal or proprietary information—to the content generation module, increasing the chance of inadvertent disclosure in the final output. This is particularly critical in domains like healthcare or legal analysis. The need for clear authorization and audit trails is emphasized in frameworks for accountable AI agent deployment [7]. A unified agent obscures the provenance of information and the decision trail, reducing auditability. It becomes difficult to discern whether a factual claim in the generated text originated from a validated source or an unverified assumption made during the writing process. This lack of transparency directly hinders accountability and complicates compliance with regulatory standards requiring explainability and data governance.

Therefore, architectural separation acts as a critical security and governance control. It enables better oversight, minimizes the risk of privilege escalation and data leaks, and preserves a verifiable chain of custody for evidence. This foundational separation, as the following section will elaborate, is essential for fostering user trust and enabling rigorous validation in high-stakes domains where transparency and auditability are paramount.

### 4.4 Trust, Transparency, and Validation in Critical Domains

The architectural independence of evidence agents is a critical enabler of user trust, system transparency, and rigorous validation, especially in high-stakes domains where errors can have severe consequences. As established in the preceding discussion on security and governance, this separation provides a foundational control for auditability and error tracing. In fields like healthcare and legal analysis, the credibility of an AI system's output is paramount. An independent evidence agent, dedicated to information retrieval, validation, and synthesis, creates a clear separation of concerns that facilitates explicit logging of evidence sources, the methods used for credibility assessment, and the reasoning chain from raw data to synthesized evidence. Such traceability is foundational for transparency, providing users and regulators with a verifiable path to understand how conclusions were reached and directly addressing concerns about the opacity of AI decision-making processes [11]. When evidence handling is merged into a monolithic writer agent, this chain of custody can become obfuscated, making it difficult to distinguish between source-derived facts and generative embellishments, thereby increasing the risk of undetected hallucinations.

Empirical studies in clinical settings underscore the value of this modular separation for both performance and trust. Research comparing multi-agent systems (MAS) to single-agent systems (SAS) in clinical decision support found that a MAS with specialized agents, including those for lab analysis and validation, improved prediction accuracy for critical outcomes like mortality [29]. While the transparency scores were comparable, the architectural pattern of specialized agents inherently supports a more interpretable framework by design. This aligns with the broader vision for agentic AI in healthcare, where systems must integrate and validate multimodal data with high precision to support patient-centric care [30]. Surveys on Agentic Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems for medical decision support further identify architectural patterns that leverage knowledge graphs and dedicated reasoning agents to create interpretable decision-making frameworks, which are essential for clinical validation and provider collaboration [8].

In legal analysis or outbreak analytics, the need for rigorous, reproducible validation is equally acute. The synthesis of policy-relevant evidence during public health emergencies requires the coordination of multiple analytical tasks and data sources [4]. Specialized LLM-based agents formalize workflows and introduce review steps, enhancing consistency and reproducibility—a quality control mechanism that is more robust when evidence curation is an independent, accountable process rather than a subsumed function. Ultimately, maintaining evidence agents as distinct entities institutionalizes validation checkpoints and fosters stakeholder confidence through demonstrable rigor. This structural transparency is not only necessary for responsible AI deployment in critical domains but also provides the clear functional boundaries that, as the following section will argue, are essential for enabling system flexibility and seamless interoperability within larger, collaborative ecosystems.

### 4.5 Architectural Flexibility and Interoperability Considerations

Building upon the need for transparency and validation in critical domains, the architectural choice between integrating evidence agents into writer agents or maintaining them as independent modules also has profound implications for a system's flexibility and its ability to interoperate within broader ecosystems. A modular, multi-agent architecture, where evidence and writer agents are distinct, inherently promotes greater flexibility. This separation allows each agent type to be developed, updated, or replaced independently according to its specialized function. For instance, an evidence agent's retrieval and validation algorithms can be refined without necessitating changes to the writer agent's narrative generation models, and vice versa. This modularity is a core principle in frameworks like the CACA Agent (Capability Collaboration based AI Agent) [31], which advocates for an open architecture where collaborative capabilities are integrated to enhance extensibility and reduce dependence on any single monolithic model. Such a design aligns with the need for scalable and adaptable systems, as seen in complex simulation platforms like SimMobility [32], which integrates diverse agent roles within a flexible, event-based architecture to model intricate real-world interactions.

From an interoperability perspective, independent agent roles are crucial for seamless integration into heterogeneous multi-agent systems and standardized communication protocols. The vision for universal interoperability, as outlined in work on Conversational AI multi-agent frameworks [33], relies on agents with well-defined, discrete functions that can be discovered and composed dynamically via open APIs. A dedicated evidence agent with a clear service manifest for "information validation and synthesis" is far more interoperable than a monolithic writer agent attempting to expose a convoluted set of mixed capabilities. This separation facilitates the agent discovery and composition processes that are foundational to scalable agentic ecosystems. Conversely, merging the roles creates a more monolithic architecture, which can streamline internal workflows for specific, closed-loop tasks but at the cost of reduced external interoperability and increased system brittleness. A unified agent becomes a single point of failure and is harder to integrate with other specialized agents that may offer superior evidence curation or content generation in specific domains.

The trade-off, therefore, lies between the internal efficiency of a tightly integrated, potentially simpler monolithic design and the long-term adaptability and collaborative potential of a modular, multi-agent collaborative design. For systems requiring integration into larger, evolving digital ecosystems—such as industrial automation [3] or autonomous cloud operations [34]—the flexibility and clear service boundaries afforded by independent evidence agents are paramount. These domains demand systems that can adapt to new tools, data sources, and partner agents. A monolithic writer agent that subsumes evidence handling may become a bottleneck, unable to easily plug into new evidence sources or delegate specialized validation tasks to a more competent external agent. Thus, while integration might offer short-term development simplicity, architectural separation is a strategic enabler for sustainable flexibility, robust interoperability, and the collaborative intelligence envisioned in advanced Multi-Agent Based Decision Support Systems (MADSS) [1].

## 5 Case Studies and Domain-Specific Implementations

### 5.1 Scientific Research and Outbreak Analytics

Building upon the general rationale for specialized agent roles, the domain of scientific research, particularly outbreak analytics, provides a compelling case study for the architectural separation of evidence agents from writer agents. Outbreaks such as mpox and COVID-19 necessitate swift, evidence-based responses, which rely on the synthesis of diverse information sources and multiple analytical techniques [4]. The traditional workflow involves numerous time-intensive manual tasks, creating bottlenecks that hinder the delivery of analysis at scale. The integration of Large Language Model (LLM)-based agents into these pipelines presents a significant opportunity to enhance efficiency. Crucially, the literature advocates for the use of *specialized* LLM-based agents, which formalize team interactions, optimize workflows, and enable quality control to improve consistency and reproducibility [4]. This architectural choice directly supports the argument for independent evidence agents.

In this context, an independent evidence agent would be responsible for the core functions of information acquisition and curation. It would perform targeted retrieval and validation of data from epidemiological reports, genomic databases, and scholarly literature. Systems like Microsoft Academic Services (MAS) demonstrate the power of AI in comprehensively collecting and analyzing scholarly communications at scale, using natural language understanding to extract factoids and assemble them into a knowledge graph [22]. A dedicated evidence agent could leverage such technologies to synthesize multi-modal evidence, maintain traceability of sources, and ensure the credibility of the information foundation. This separation allows the evidence agent to focus on rigorous validation, a critical need given that misinterpretation of data could lead to erroneous conclusions with serious consequences [4].

Conversely, a writer agent would utilize the curated, high-quality evidence provided by the evidence agent to generate situational reports, policy briefs, and public communications. The impact of this architectural separation is threefold. First, it enhances **response speed** by allowing evidence retrieval and synthesis to occur in parallel with the drafting of initial narrative structures, and through the simultaneous deployment of multiple specialized agents [4]. Second, it improves **evidence quality** by instituting formal review steps where the evidence agent's output is scrutinized before being passed to the writer agent, reducing the risk of hallucinations and errors propagating into final outputs. This creates a system of checks analogous to human peer review. Third, it bolsters **reproducibility**. A clear separation of concerns means the evidence trail—the sources, validation steps, and synthesis logic—is maintained independently of the narrative construction. This makes the analytical pipeline more transparent and auditable, allowing other researchers to verify the evidence base even if the final report's phrasing is altered. Therefore, in high-stakes, fast-moving domains like outbreak analytics, the case for architecturally independent evidence agents is strong, as it directly addresses the core requirements for speed, quality, and rigorous scientific reproducibility. This emphasis on verifiable processes and trust, established here in a scientific context, is similarly paramount in the following examination of the legal domain.

### 5.2 Legal Analysis and Judicial Assistance

Building upon the scientific rationale for role specialization, the legal domain presents a quintessential high-stakes environment where the architectural separation of evidence agents from writer agents is critically examined and validated. In applications such as legal analysis and judicial assistance, the imperatives of accuracy, the reduction of hallucinations, and the cultivation of user trust are paramount, mirroring the needs for verifiable processes established in outbreak analytics. Empirical case studies from this field demonstrate that a multi-agent architecture with distinct evidence-handling roles significantly enhances system reliability and trustworthiness. For instance, the Chatlaw system exemplifies this approach by employing a multi-agent collaborative framework specifically designed to mitigate the hallucination problem, which poses substantial legal risks [35]. In this architecture, specialized agents are responsible for evidence retrieval and validation against structured legal knowledge graphs, while separate writer agents generate the final legal consultation text. This separation ensures that content generation is grounded in verified legal precedents and statutes, directly improving response accuracy. The system's use of Standardized Operating Procedures (SOP), modeled on real law firm workflows, further institutionalizes the distinct roles of evidence gathering and content drafting, leading to a documented outperformance of models like GPT-4 in legal benchmarks.

Within such a setup, an independent evidence agent acts as a dedicated validator, cross-referencing claims against a curated legal database before information is passed to the writer agent. This process directly addresses user trust concerns highlighted in broader studies on LLM-based conversational agents, where transparency in decision-making is a key factor for adoption, especially in sensitive fields [11]. By making evidence retrieval and validation an explicit, auditable module, the system provides clearer provenance for its conclusions, which is essential for legal professionals who must assess the reliability of the AI's output. The alternative—a merged agent performing both evidence synthesis and narrative generation—risks conflating these processes, potentially allowing unverified information or speculative reasoning to taint the final output without a clear internal checkpoint. Furthermore, principles of trust and reputation management, as discussed in the context of networked systems, underscore the importance of verifiable processes for building credibility [36]. An independent evidence agent contributes to a "trust-based" system architecture where the quality of the foundational information can be assessed separately from its presentation.

Therefore, for high-stakes legal applications, the evidence strongly supports an architectural paradigm where evidence agents operate as independent roles to enforce rigorous validation, provide traceability, and foster the necessary trust for practical deployment. This emphasis on auditability and a clear chain of evidence, established as critical in law, similarly forms the foundational requirement for the next domain of examination: financial services, where regulatory compliance and decision integrity are equally non-negotiable.

### 5.3 Financial Services and Investment Management

The domain of financial services and investment management presents a compelling case study for examining the architectural separation of evidence agents from writer agents, as it demands a delicate balance between rigorous auditability, decision autonomy, and operational efficiency. Mirroring the high-stakes imperatives seen in legal and healthcare domains, financial applications require robust mechanisms to ensure data integrity, regulatory compliance, and user trust. Here, independent evidence agents are tasked with the critical function of sourcing, validating, and synthesizing complex, multi-modal data—from real-time market feeds and regulatory filings to economic indicators and news sentiment [13]. This dedicated focus ensures a clear, inspectable chain of evidence, which is paramount for compliance and audit trails. For instance, in credit risk assessment, an autonomous evidence agent can continuously ingest and evaluate borrower data, applying consistent validation rules independent of the narrative construction process, thereby reducing the risk of biased or unsubstantiated conclusions being generated [37]. This separation directly supports the explainability mandates of modern financial governance, as the evidence retrieval and reasoning chain remains a distinct and auditable module.

Conversely, arguments for a merged agent architecture highlight potential gains in workflow optimization and reduced latency. A unified agent that both gathers evidence and drafts investment summaries or client reports could enable faster responses to market events, which is particularly relevant for high-frequency trading insights or personalized customer communications where speed is a competitive advantage [14]. However, this integration introduces significant trade-offs, analogous to the risks identified in other sensitive fields. Merging roles can obscure the provenance of information, complicating regulatory audits and eroding stakeholder trust. In complex cognitive decision-making tasks like investment planning, the risk of the agent conflating evidence curation with persuasive narrative generation increases, potentially leading to "hallucinated" justifications for decisions [38]. The multi-agent decision support system (MADSS) paradigm, which often employs specialized knowledge agents, suggests that separation enhances system robustness and allows for more effective coordination among specialized entities, each accountable for a specific facet of the decision-making pipeline [1].

Therefore, empirical observations from agentic AI implementations in finance indicate a context-dependent preference. Architectural independence is strongly favored in scenarios demanding high accountability and stringent auditability, such as regulatory reporting and fiduciary advice—paralleling the needs of legal and clinical settings. In contrast, more integrated designs may be pragmatically deployed for internal analytics or customer-facing chatbots where operational efficiency and personalization are prioritized over the need for granular evidence traceability. Ultimately, the choice hinges on the specific task's risk profile and the required strength of the human-in-the-loop oversight mechanisms [21], reinforcing the principle that the stakes of the application dictate the necessity for role separation.

### 5.4 Healthcare Diagnostics and Clinical Decision Support

The domain of healthcare diagnostics and clinical decision support represents a critical testbed for evaluating the architectural separation of evidence agents from writer agents, where the imperatives of safety, trust, and rigorous protocol adherence are paramount. This context strongly advocates for maintaining evidence agents as independent, specialized modules, a design choice that directly addresses the high-stakes nature of medical applications. As surveyed in [8], agentic RAG systems in healthcare often employ distinct evidence agents responsible for rigorous information retrieval, validation against trusted medical knowledge bases (e.g., clinical guidelines, drug databases), and synthesis of multi-modal patient data. This separation ensures that the evidence-gathering and validation process is insulated from the narrative generation performed by writer agents, thereby minimizing the risk of the writer agent "hallucinating" or fabricating medical facts—a fundamental limitation of LLMs noted in [19].

The independence of the evidence agent is crucial for building clinician trust, a significant barrier to AI adoption. By functioning as a dedicated module, it can provide clear provenance for its outputs, citing specific guidelines, research papers, or patient data points. This traceability allows clinicians to audit the AI's reasoning, moving from a "black box" to a more transparent, evidence-based tool. The framework for assessing LLMs highlights the necessity of control mechanisms and referencing external sources to ensure appropriateness of output, a function naturally served by a specialized evidence agent [19]. Furthermore, specialized architectures like the multi-agent intuitionistic fuzzy logic system described in [39] demonstrate how dedicated agents for data processing and uncertainty quantification (acting as evidence agents) contribute to accurate health status detection, reinforcing the value of role specialization for reliability.

Regarding safety protocols, an independent evidence agent enforces a critical "checkpoint" in the clinical workflow. It can be designed to strictly adhere to validation rules, cross-reference contraindications, and flag inconsistencies before evidence is passed to the writer agent for summarization or report drafting. This creates a systemic guardrail, ensuring that generated content is anchored in vetted information. The vision of next-generation agentic AI for healthcare, as outlined in [30], emphasizes autonomy, precision, and reduced error rates—goals that are bolstered by an architecture where evidence handling is a dedicated, accountable function. Conversely, merging the roles risks conflating evidence retrieval with persuasive narrative generation, potentially obscuring source credibility and compromising the rigorous validation processes essential in clinical settings. This architectural choice thus prioritizes safety, auditability, and trust over the potential workflow efficiencies of a merged model, establishing a precedent for domains where accountability is non-negotiable.

### 5.5 Business Intelligence and Customer Service Operations

The domain of business intelligence (BI) and automated customer service presents a nuanced perspective on the architectural separation of evidence agents, where the dual imperatives of personalized engagement and operational scalability must be balanced. This context reveals that a dedicated evidence agent can strengthen the foundation for personalization while enhancing system robustness. For instance, in customer service applications, a specialized evidence agent retrieves and validates customer history, product details, and policy rules from heterogeneous sources such as knowledge graphs and databases [40]. This curated, verified evidence is then supplied to a writer agent, which synthesizes it into a coherent, relationally personalized response adapted to the customer’s context and communication style [41]. This division of labor ensures that personalization is built upon accurate, traceable information, mitigating the risk of hallucinations or inconsistent service that could undermine trust. The writer agent is thereby freed to focus on the nuanced task of relational communication, leveraging psychological and contextual insights, a process critically dependent on the reliable data stream provided by its evidence-oriented counterpart [41].

From an operational standpoint, the independence of the evidence agent supports scalability and efficiency. The evidence retrieval and validation module can be optimized and scaled separately from the narrative generation layer. As research on AI agent-based service innovation indicates, such specialization can accelerate response times and reduce operational workload by streamlining complex information processing pipelines [14]. A unified agent handling both evidence curation and personalized writing may become a bottleneck under high load, as the competing cognitive and computational demands of each task interfere. In contrast, a modular architecture enables parallel execution; evidence agents can continuously pre-fetch and update knowledge bases, while writer agents generate responses from the latest validated evidence pool. This design aligns with the demand for "agility and user-oriented features" in dynamic business environments [40]. However, this separation introduces challenges, including the need for sophisticated integration mechanisms and potential latency in agent handoffs. Effective governance also becomes paramount, as underscored by the "Human-in-Command" framework, which stresses human oversight to ensure accountability and ethical alignment in these multi-stage automated workflows [21].

Thus, in business intelligence and customer service, the architectural independence of evidence agents appears to fortify the reliability underpinning personalized interactions while providing a more resilient and scalable foundation for large-scale operations—echoing the specialization arguments seen in healthcare and prefiguring the trade-offs between integration and separation observed in network management.

### 5.6 Network Management and Telecommunications

The domain of network management and telecommunications presents a compelling case study for examining the architectural independence of evidence agents, as the inherent complexity, dynamism, and criticality of modern networks demand systems capable of robust, evidence-based decision-making and rapid adaptation. This context builds upon the specialization arguments observed in business intelligence, while prefiguring the high-stakes, data-intensive demands seen in agricultural management. Implementations in this field, particularly within next-generation architectures like space-air-ground integrated networks (SAGIN) and software-defined infrastructures, highlight how specialized evidence agents enhance system adaptability, reduce failure response times, and manage operational complexity. For instance, in the context of SAGIN resource management, an agentic AI framework embeds specialized agents within a MAPE-K control plane to autonomously interpret operator intents and optimize network execution [18]. Here, distinct agents such as semantic resource perceivers function akin to evidence agents by continuously monitoring and validating heterogeneous, time-varying network conditions—a task requiring dedicated focus on information acquisition and credibility assessment. This separation allows the orchestrator agent (analogous to a writer agent responsible for planning and generating configuration commands) to base its decisions on a curated, validated evidence stream, thereby improving the system's adaptability to dynamic topologies and reducing latency in service orchestration.

The benefits of this architectural separation become particularly evident when considering failure response and management complexity, echoing concerns about cognitive overload and scalability raised in customer service systems. A unified agent attempting to both gather multi-domain evidence and execute reconfiguration plans would face significant cognitive load, potentially delaying critical responses to network anomalies or security threats. The specialized evidence agent model, by providing a dedicated module for observability and validation, directly contributes to faster and more reliable failure diagnosis. This is supported by research into optimizing agent-environment interactions, where enhancing environment observability—a core function of an evidence agent—is identified as a key method for improving agent success rates without modifying the core reasoning agent [42]. In more ambitious visions like FlexNGIA 2.0, where AI agents autonomously design and deploy network protocols, the implicit need for evidence gathering and validation at each stage of the design-test-deploy cycle suggests that embedding this function within a distinct agent role would enhance traceability and auditability, crucial for managing the complexity of self-evolving networks [12].

Conversely, arguments for integration surface in scenarios prioritizing ultra-low latency and streamlined workflows for specific, well-defined orchestration tasks. Some resource orchestration mechanisms employ a more integrated agent model where a single agent handles both perception and action based on a learned policy, as seen in certain deep reinforcement learning approaches for multi-domain virtual network embedding [43]. This can reduce coordination overhead. However, such integrated models often struggle with explainability and may lack the rigorous, evidence-traceable validation required for high-stakes network decisions or when integrating semantic, intent-based instructions from human operators—a limitation that foreshadows the critical need for credible, validated information in agricultural advisories. Therefore, the choice between independent evidence agents and integrated writer agents in telecommunications hinges on the specific trade-off between raw operational speed and the need for adaptable, transparent, and verifiable decision-making in increasingly autonomous and complex network environments, a tension that persists across domains.

### 5.7 Agricultural and Environmental Management

The domain of agricultural and environmental management presents a compelling case study for examining the architectural separation of evidence and writer agents, particularly in the context of climate-responsive adaptation. This domain is characterized by high-stakes decisions under uncertainty, involving complex interactions between climatic data, soil conditions, crop models, and socio-economic factors. The implementation of agentic AI systems here demonstrates how independent evidence agents enhance decision robustness and improve communication with diverse stakeholders, from smallholder farmers to policy-makers. For instance, frameworks like the "AGENTIC AI–DRIVEN DECISION-SUPPORT FRAMEWORK FOR CLIMATE-RESPONSIVE AGRICULTURAL ADAPTATION USING REINFORCEMENT LEARNING" leverage multi-agent reasoning, where specialized agents handle distinct functions. In such a system, an independent evidence agent is responsible for the continuous retrieval, validation, and synthesis of multi-modal data—including real-time climate forecasts, soil sensor readings, and historical yield patterns [44]. This separation ensures the evidence base is rigorously curated and traceable before being passed to a writer agent tasked with generating actionable advisories or reports for farmers.

The need for this architectural independence is underscored by the critical importance of credible, validated information for building resilient systems, as highlighted in agricultural extension studies [45]. When evidence gathering is a distinct, auditable process, it increases the trustworthiness of the final recommendations—a factor crucial for farmer adoption. Furthermore, participatory approaches that emphasize the co-creation of knowledge require systems capable of separating raw data and evidence synthesis from the narrative tailoring needed for different audiences [46]. An independent evidence agent can maintain a consistent, validated knowledge graph, while a separate writer agent adapts the communication style—whether as a simple SMS reminder, an interactive voice response, or a detailed plan for a cooperative—based on stakeholder needs and literacy levels, as explored in studies on ICT-mediated advice [47].

This separation also mitigates risks in dynamic environments, paralleling concerns in network management about cognitive overload and failure response. Climate adaptation decisions must integrate complex variables, and bundling evidence retrieval with content generation in a single agent could lead to cognitive overload, increasing the risk of generating unsupported recommendations during narrative construction. The conceptual design of smart farming solutions, often built as multi-agent systems with ontology-driven knowledge bases, inherently supports this modularity [48]. By maintaining an architecturally independent evidence agent, the system ensures that the foundational data and reasoning processes remain transparent and verifiable. This is vital not only for operational decisions but also for stakeholder communication, enabling clear explanations of *why* a specific adaptation strategy (e.g., adjusting planting dates or conserving soil moisture) is recommended, thereby enhancing both the robustness of the decision and the efficacy of the agricultural extension process. This emphasis on traceability and auditability for high-stakes decisions further aligns with the rationale seen in supply chain logistics, where separating evidence handling from action planning is deemed essential for robustness and transparent human-AI collaboration.

### 5.8 Supply Chain and Logistics Optimization

Building upon the case for architectural separation in agricultural management, the domain of supply chain and logistics optimization further underscores the necessity of maintaining evidence agents as distinct entities. This high-stakes, dynamic environment demands decisions on inventory, routing, forecasting, and disruption response that synthesize vast, real-time data streams from IoT sensors, market feeds, ERP systems, and external partners. The core argument for independence rests on the need for rigorous, auditable, and specialized data validation and synthesis before any prescriptive or communicative action is taken. An integrated writer-evidence agent might prioritize narrative coherence or action planning, potentially at the expense of thoroughly vetting underlying data for anomalies, credibility, or contextual relevance. In contrast, a dedicated evidence agent focuses on continuous information acquisition, curating evidence from disparate sources to construct a validated, traceable knowledge base. This separation ensures that a subsequent writer or orchestrator agent, responsible for generating reports, alerts, or executable plans, operates on a foundation of high-fidelity evidence, thereby mitigating the risk of cascading errors from flawed data.

This architectural division aligns with principles of multi-agent coordination, akin to those discussed in agricultural systems and explicitly supported by research on service composition [49]. In a complex supply network, various functional agents (e.g., for procurement, transportation) must cooperate. An independent evidence agent can serve as a neutral arbiter, providing a shared, validated situational awareness critical for achieving global optimization over local sub-optimal decisions. This pattern enhances system resilience; when a disruption like a port closure occurs, the evidence agent can independently gather and correlate multimodal evidence (e.g., satellite imagery, news reports, carrier updates) to confirm the event's scope and impact. The writer agent then utilizes this synthesized evidence to communicate contingency plans with clear provenance, fostering trust and enabling rapid, informed human oversight.

Moreover, stringent auditability and compliance requirements in global logistics necessitate transparent decision trails. An independent evidence agent maintains logs of its retrieval sources, validation checks, and synthesis logic, which is crucial for regulatory compliance, performance analysis, and post-mortem reviews. Merging this function into a writer agent could obfuscate the lineage from raw data to final recommendation, making it difficult to pinpoint whether a failure originated from poor evidence or flawed narrative construction. Therefore, in supply chain and logistics optimization—where decisions carry direct financial, operational, and reputational consequences—the architectural separation of evidence handling from content generation is not merely beneficial but often essential for robustness, transparency, and effective human-AI collaboration, extending the rationale established in climate-responsive agriculture to another critical, data-intensive domain.
