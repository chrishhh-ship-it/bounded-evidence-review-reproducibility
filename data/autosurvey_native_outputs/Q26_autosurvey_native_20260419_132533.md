# Does Scaling Agent Quantity Always Improve Performance? A Comprehensive Survey on Multi-Agent System Efficacy and Limitations

## 1 Introduction: The Promise and Complexity of Agent Scaling

### 1.1 The Rise of LLM-Powered AI Agents and Their Ubiquitous Applications

The advent of Large Language Models (LLMs) has catalyzed a paradigm shift in artificial intelligence, enabling the creation of autonomous, intelligent agents capable of reasoning, planning, and executing complex workflows. These LLM-powered agents represent a fundamental evolution, imbuing systems with advanced autonomy, adaptability, and scalability [1]. This new generation of "agentic AI" is characterized by its ability to integrate diverse data sources, iteratively refine outputs, and leverage vast knowledge bases for context-aware decision-making, thereby unlocking transformative potential across a broad spectrum of high-stakes domains. The rapid adoption of these agents underscores a move towards more dynamic, collaborative, and intelligent systems that promise to redefine operational efficiency and service delivery.

This transformative potential is evident across numerous sectors. In healthcare, agentic AI systems are deployed to enhance diagnostics, clinical decision support, and personalized treatment planning, aiming for more precise, patient-centric care while optimizing administrative workflows [1]. In customer service and digital business, AI agents utilize natural language processing and real-time data analysis to automate interactions, provide personalized recommendations, and significantly improve response times and user satisfaction [2]. The finance and investment sector is also undergoing a transformation, where LLMs are applied to linguistic tasks, sentiment analysis, and complex financial reasoning, driving innovation in traditional practices [3].

Furthermore, the reach of these agents extends to critical public service domains such as outbreak analytics. Specialized LLM-based agents can be integrated into analytical pipelines to synthesize information, perform multiple tasks simultaneously, and enhance the efficiency and scalability of real-time epidemic response [4]. In networking, the convergence of AI and communication systems is paving the way for "agentic AI networking," where autonomous agents interact within an ecosystem to enable dynamic adaptation and collaborative problem-solving in complex environments like 6G networks [5].

This ubiquitous application across healthcare, finance, customer experience, public health, and networking highlights the core promise of agentic AI: to deliver unprecedented efficiency, scalability, and personalized service. The theoretical advantages of deploying multiple such agents—including specialization, parallel processing, and robust problem-solving—are a primary driver for their scaling within these systems. However, this widespread integration and the intuitive appeal of scaling also set the stage for the central inquiry of this survey, as increasing the number of agents within these diverse and demanding environments introduces new layers of complexity regarding their collective efficacy and potential limitations.

### 1.2 The Intuitive Appeal and Theoretical Promise of Agent Scaling

Building upon the transformative potential of agentic AI systems across diverse domains, a foundational and intuitively appealing hypothesis emerges: that increasing the number of intelligent agents within a system inherently improves its overall performance. This scaling promise, central to multi-agent systems (MAS) research, is rooted in compelling theoretical advantages drawn from distributed computing, organizational theory, and complex systems science.

The primary expected benefit is the enhancement of task accuracy and quality through specialized division of labor. By deploying multiple agents, each can be designed or trained for a specific, narrow function, fostering deeper expertise and higher precision in subtasks. This specialization is a core motivation behind frameworks employing multiple LLM-based agents, where different models assume dedicated roles to perform complex analytical workflows—such as in outbreak analytics—with the goal of yielding higher-quality, more consistent outputs than a single, general-purpose model could achieve [4]. Similarly, in service innovation, specialized AI agents handling distinct aspects of customer interaction can lead to more accurate information retrieval and personalized recommendations [2].

Beyond specialization, scaling promises significant gains in operational efficiency via parallel processing. Multiple agents can execute different tasks or process various data streams simultaneously, dramatically accelerating system throughput and reducing latency. Empirical evidence suggests that multi-agent implementations can substantially reduce operational workload and improve service response times [2]. This parallelism is crucial for time-sensitive applications, from real-time customer service management [6] to dynamic decision-making in complex environments like agriculture or finance [7].

Furthermore, increasing agent quantity theoretically bolsters system robustness and reliability through redundancy. If one agent fails or is compromised, others can potentially compensate or assume its functions, thereby enhancing fault tolerance and ensuring continuous service availability. This redundancy, coupled with the capacity for collaborative problem-solving, also empowers systems to tackle more complex, multi-faceted challenges that exceed the capability of any single agent. By decomposing a large, intricate problem into interconnected subtasks, a multi-agent system can integrate diverse perspectives and specialized capabilities. This collaborative approach is essential for domains such as personalized product-service system design, where multiple agents search, evaluate, and synthesize solutions [8], and for climate-responsive agricultural adaptation, where different agents reason about various environmental factors [9].

Collectively, this theoretical promise paints a compelling vision of scalable systems that are more accurate, efficient, resilient, and capable of navigating unprecedented complexity. It is this very promise that underpins the widespread drive to scale agent deployments, as highlighted in the preceding overview of sectoral applications. However, this optimistic framework sets the stage for the critical inquiry that follows, as the practical realization of these benefits is not guaranteed and is often mediated by significant emergent complexities.

### 1.3 Formulating the Central Research Question: The Non-Linear Relationship Between Quantity and Quality

While the intuitive appeal of scaling agent quantity is strong, the preceding discussion of its theoretical promise necessitates a critical examination of its practical limits. This survey therefore explicitly challenges the linear assumption that more agents invariably equate to better performance. The central research question we formulate is: **Does scaling the number of agents in a multi-agent system (MAS) always lead to a proportional or even positive improvement in overall system quality and efficacy?** This inquiry moves beyond a simplistic "more is better" paradigm to investigate the nuanced, and often non-linear, relationship between quantity and quality.

The promise of scaling, as seen in domains like outbreak analytics where multiple specialized agents can enhance workflow efficiency and output quality through formalized team interactions [4], is counterbalanced by emergent complexities that can degrade returns. The core hypothesis we examine is that increasing agent count introduces diminishing returns, performance plateaus, or outright degradation due to escalating coordination overhead, compounded security vulnerabilities, and heightened sensitivity to environmental and operational dependencies.

Our investigation is framed by recognizing that systemic quality is a multi-dimensional construct encompassing task accuracy, decision reliability, resource efficiency, security, and ethical alignment. Simply adding agents, without sophisticated architectural support, can overwhelm these dimensions. For instance, in complex network environments like Cloud-Edge-Terminal Collaborative Networks (CETCN), while multi-agent deep reinforcement learning (MADRL) is proposed to manage high-dimensional tasks through collaboration [10], scaling agent numbers indiscriminately can exacerbate challenges in coordination and resource allocation, leading to suboptimal or unstable decision-making. Similarly, in business applications such as automated customer service or inventory management, scaling agent teams might improve task specialization but also amplify inherent decision-making biases or coordination failures, as highlighted by concerns regarding LLM agent biases in operational contexts [11; 6].

Therefore, this survey posits that the relationship between agent quantity and system quality is inherently non-linear and contingent. It is mediated by critical factors including the sophistication of collaborative frameworks, the nature of the task environment, the robustness of governance mechanisms, and the inherent limitations of the underlying AI models, such as predictability and understanding issues in LLMs [12]. To systematically dissect this relationship, the subsequent sections will first explore the architectural foundations necessary for effective scaling, and then empirically analyze the performance trade-offs and failure modes that define its practical boundaries.

## 2 Architectural and Collaborative Frameworks for Scalable Multi-Agent Systems

### 2.1 Foundational Architectures for Scalable Multi-Agent Collaboration

Foundational architectures for scalable multi-agent collaboration are pivotal in structuring how autonomous agents interact, share knowledge, and coordinate to achieve complex objectives. These architectures are designed to prevent the inefficiencies of monolithic designs and to harness the potential of increased agent numbers. A core paradigm is the service-oriented architecture, which decouples agent capabilities into modular, discoverable services. This approach enhances extensibility by allowing new agents or functions to be integrated seamlessly without overhauling the entire system. For instance, the CACA Agent (Capability Collaboration based AI Agent) framework explicitly adopts an open architecture inspired by service computing to integrate a set of collaborative capabilities [19]. By reducing dependence on a single Large Language Model (LLM), it allows for the flexible expansion of both planning abilities and available tools, demonstrating how decoupling facilitates scalable application scenario extension. Similarly, initiatives like the Open Voice Network (OVON) propose universal open APIs to establish interoperable interactions among diverse conversational AI agents, including chatbots and voicebots [20]. This framework includes a service discovery specification, enabling efficient lookup of agents providing specific services via a standard manifest, thereby directly supporting scalable, platform-agnostic collaboration.

Beyond service-oriented models, ecosystem-focused frameworks create interactive knowledge bases that agents can collectively access and contribute to, promoting shared intelligence and reducing redundant computations. The AgentNet framework, designed for agentic AI networking in 6G, introduces a generative foundation model (GFM)-based implementation where multiple GFM-as-agents form an interactive knowledge-base [5]. This architecture bootstraps the development of embodied AI agents according to task requirements and environmental features, effectively decoupling knowledge storage and generation from individual agent reasoning. This separation is crucial for scalability, as it prevents knowledge silos and allows the system to leverage collective learning. Complementing this, architectural reviews on multi-agent knowledge management propose systems where agents automatically communicate to disseminate knowledge, enhancing service quality through fast processing and knowledge reusability [21]. Furthermore, frameworks like M-ASK (Multi-Agent Search and Knowledge) explicitly decompose agentic search into specialized roles: Search Behavior Agents for planning actions and Knowledge Management Agents for aggregating and maintaining a compact internal context [22]. This decoupling of search behavior from knowledge management reduces interference, mitigates structural bottlenecks of monolithic agents, and provides a stable foundation for scaling collaboration.

Collectively, these foundational architectures—through service orientation, ecosystem knowledge bases, and role specialization—aim to create scalable, extensible multi-agent systems. They establish the structural prerequisites necessary to harness increased agent quantity productively. As the following discussion will elaborate, these architectural enablers directly support the subsequent need for effective coordination, service discovery, and knowledge management mechanisms to mitigate the overhead that can otherwise accompany scale.

### 2.2 Mechanisms for Coordination, Service Discovery, and Knowledge Management

Building upon the foundational architectures that establish scalable, extensible structures for multi-agent collaboration, this subsection delves into the critical operational mechanisms required to manage the interactions within such systems. **Effective coordination, service discovery, and knowledge management** are the essential pillars that translate architectural potential into functional reality, directly mitigating the inefficiencies and redundancies that can erode performance gains as agent numbers increase.

Coordination mechanisms, such as planning and negotiation, orchestrate agent activities to achieve coherent system-level goals. Their formalization is vital, particularly in distributed, service-oriented architectures where intelligent coordination with minimal interruption is paramount [23]. These protocols enable complex workflows, such as integrating diverse evidence streams to support clinical decision-making in healthcare multi-agent systems [13].

Parallel to coordination, robust **service discovery** is indispensable for enabling agents in a large, heterogeneous ecosystem to dynamically locate and utilize capabilities. The historical lack of standardized cross-vendor discovery has been a significant bottleneck. Proposals like AgentDNS directly address this by creating a root domain naming system, allowing LLM agents to autonomously discover and securely invoke third-party services, thereby enhancing interoperability and reducing the overhead of manual integration as systems scale [24].

For **knowledge management**, advanced architectures operationalize the decoupling principles introduced earlier by separating distinct cognitive functions. A key innovation is the explicit decomposition of search behavior from knowledge aggregation. Frameworks like M-ASK (Multi-Agent Search and Knowledge) exemplify this by employing specialized Search Behavior Agents for planning and execution, while dedicated Knowledge Management Agents filter and maintain a compact internal context [22]. This separation reduces interference, curbs redundant processing, and enables granular supervision, directly countering scaling inefficiencies. Similarly, in enterprise settings, multi-agent knowledge management systems automate inter-agent communication to facilitate rapid knowledge retrieval and dissemination, enhancing overall service quality and adaptability [21]. The complexity of this integration escalates with more agents, making effective methods crucial for synthesizing heterogeneous knowledge into unified decisions, as seen in financial e-services [25].

Collectively, these coordinated mechanisms for discovery and knowledge handling are not merely supportive features but essential operational components. They determine whether scaling agent quantity leverages architectural foundations to yield improved performance or devolves into systemic fragmentation and overhead. The necessity for these mechanisms sets the stage for the next critical consideration: as systems grow and operate in dynamic environments, static coordination becomes insufficient, necessitating the advanced frameworks for **dynamic and adaptive coordination** discussed in the following subsection.

### 2.3 Dynamic and Adaptive Coordination in Complex Environments

Building upon the foundational coordination and knowledge management mechanisms discussed earlier, which are essential for mitigating inefficiencies at scale, this subsection examines the advanced architectural frameworks required for **dynamic and adaptive coordination** in complex, real-time environments. As agent populations grow within heterogeneous and time-sensitive domains—such as wireless networks and the Industrial Internet of Things (IoT)—static coordination protocols become insufficient. Instead, systems must enable agents to adapt their collaborative strategies in real-time based on fluctuating conditions and resource availability. These frameworks employ adaptive control loops, collective learning, and semantic reasoning to achieve robust, self-optimizing orchestration, directly addressing the performance risks associated with scaling agent numbers in dynamic settings.

A prominent architectural pattern for such environments is the integration of agents within a Monitor-Analyze-Plan-Execute-Knowledge (MAPE-K) control plane. This is exemplified in Space-Air-Ground Integrated Networks (SAGIN), where an agentic AI framework embeds Large Language Model (LLM)-based agents to autonomously manage resources [26]. Specialized agents—semantic resource perceivers, intent-driven orchestrators, and adaptive learners—collaborate through natural language reasoning. A key innovation is a hierarchical collaboration where LLM-based orchestrators dynamically shape reward functions for Reinforcement Learning (RL) agents based on semantic interpretations of the network state, enabling adaptive resource allocation that responds to both operator intents and environmental dynamics.

Similarly, in wireless sensor and vehicular networks, multi-agent deep reinforcement learning (MADRL) frameworks are designed for decentralized, adaptive coordination. For instance, a multi-agent deep deterministic policy gradient algorithm with centralized learning and decentralized execution jointly optimizes channel allocation and power control in heterogeneous vehicular networks [27]. This approach allows individual agent-vehicles to learn and execute policies locally while benefiting from centralized training, enabling the system to adapt to rapid changes in topology and service demands. In Wireless Sensor Networks (WSNs), an Adaptive Distributed Artificial Intelligence (ADAI) technique with a hierarchical strategy addresses inter-cluster and intra-cluster resource allocation [28]. By combining multi-agent coordination with adaptive optimization algorithms like Adaptive Particle Swarm Optimization (APSO), the system dynamically manages energy and response time based on real-time node conditions.

Furthermore, the vision for future networks explicitly calls for agentic systems capable of runtime adaptation. Proposals like FlexNGIA 2.0 envision LLM-based AI agents that autonomously orchestrate, configure, and evolve network protocols and resource allocation strategies in response to evolving conditions [29]. This represents a shift towards systems where coordination is not pre-programmed but emerges from the agents' collective reasoning and learning.

These frameworks collectively demonstrate that dynamic and adaptive coordination is a critical architectural response to scaling. It is not merely about increasing the number of decision points but about structuring agent interactions—through hierarchical control, shared learning, and semantic reasoning—to transform a larger agent population into a responsive, self-optimizing collective capable of managing complexity. This capability to adaptively coordinate is a prerequisite for ensuring that increased agent quantity translates into enhanced system performance and resilience, rather than chaos, especially as we consider the economic and incentive structures necessary for sustainable scaling, which will be discussed next.

### 2.4 Economic and Incentive-Aware Architectural Considerations

The economic viability and sustainability of multi-agent systems are paramount considerations when scaling agent quantity, as unchecked proliferation can lead to prohibitive costs, misaligned incentives, and systemic inefficiencies. **Building upon the need for adaptive coordination in complex environments, architectural designs must therefore incorporate explicit economic and incentive-aware components** to balance computational expenditures, quality of service (QoS), and agent participation. A primary challenge is the high infrastructure and computation cost associated with agentic AI services, coupled with multi-dimensional and task-dependent QoS requirements [30]. To address this, contract-theoretic frameworks have been proposed to model service offerings under information asymmetry. For instance, PACT designs incentive-compatible contracts that align user needs with provider costs—including potential liability—along both objective (e.g., response time) and subjective (e.g., user satisfaction) QoS dimensions [30]. This architectural approach ensures that scaling services remains economically sustainable for providers while offering users tailored, liable service options.

Dynamic incentive models are crucial for fostering reliable participation in distributed, federated settings where agent willingness may fluctuate. In edge-cloud ecosystems for mobile metaverses, for example, edge servers contribute to federated AI agent construction. A two-period dynamic contract model is designed to continuously incentivize participation by addressing dynamic information asymmetry between a cloud server and edge servers [31]. This architectural component uses enhanced diffusion model-based algorithms to generate optimal contracts, ensuring that scaling the number of contributing agents does not collapse due to a lack of economic motivation. Furthermore, the economic equilibria within multi-agent ecosystems themselves dictate the utility of scaling. Research into economies comprising multiple AI and human agents reveals that simply adding more powerful agents does not guarantee mutual benefit; in some equilibria, more capable agents may contribute zero utility to less capable ones [32]. This underscores the need for architectures that model long-term utility exchanges and market dynamics to prevent scenarios where increased agent quantity leads to parasitic or zero-sum outcomes.

Architectural considerations must also integrate service level agreements (SLAs) to formalize QoS expectations and manage the economic risks of scaling. The shift from Model-as-a-Service to Agent-as-a-Service introduces new challenges in defining and assuring quality for autonomous AI components [33]. Proposing a quality model and a domain-specific language for SLAs helps architect systems where scaling agents is coupled with clear, enforceable performance metrics, thereby protecting both provider investments and user expectations. Ultimately, these economic and incentive-aware architectural components—spanning dynamic contracts, equilibrium analyses, and SLA frameworks—are essential to ensure that scaling agent quantity translates into a sustainable, cooperative, and economically viable multi-agent ecosystem rather than a costly, misaligned, or unstable one. **This focus on economic sustainability and incentive alignment provides a crucial foundation for the subsequent discussion on the broader systemic implications of scaling.**

## 3 Empirical Analysis of Performance Trade-offs and Failure Modes

### 3.1 Observed Performance Benefits in Complex Task Domains

Empirical studies across diverse, knowledge-intensive domains consistently demonstrate that scaling the number of agents within a system can yield significant performance benefits, particularly for complex, multifaceted tasks. The core advantage lies in the strategic division of labor, where specialized agents, each with distinct roles and expertise, collaborate within structured workflows to achieve outcomes that would be challenging for a single, monolithic agent. This collaborative scaling enhances accuracy, efficiency, and the ability to manage intricate processes.

In healthcare, a prime example of a high-stakes, complex domain, multi-agent systems show marked improvements in diagnostic reasoning and clinical decision support. Frameworks like Agentic Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems leverage multiple agents to autonomously coordinate clinical workflows, integrate evidence from disparate sources, and provide interpretable decision-making support [13]. By employing specialized agents for tasks such as medical question answering, report generation, and guideline adherence, these systems can synthesize vast amounts of multimodal patient data and medical literature more effectively than a single model. Similarly, the proposed openCHA framework utilizes an orchestrator agent to plan and execute multi-step actions, gathering information from external knowledge bases and analysis models to generate personalized healthcare responses, thereby overcoming the limitations of simpler conversational agents [34].

The benefits of scaling extend to public health and epidemic response, where timely analysis requires synthesizing information from numerous sources. In outbreak analytics, employing a team of specialized LLM-based agents formalizes workflows, allows simultaneous task execution, and introduces multiple review steps, leading to higher-quality, more reproducible outputs than direct LLM prompting [4]. This scalability is crucial for providing round-the-clock analytical support during crises. Furthermore, in strategic public health initiatives like HIV epidemic control, Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) frameworks enable jurisdiction-specific decision-making while accounting for cross-jurisdictional interactions. Experimental results show that MARL-driven policies for resource allocation outperform single-agent approaches by better reducing new infections under budget constraints, highlighting how multi-agent systems can optimize large-scale, interconnected problems [35].

Beyond healthcare, in domains like agriculture and networked computing, scaling agent quantity facilitates tackling environmental and logistical complexity. Participatory agricultural extension models, enhanced by digital technologies, rely on multi-stakeholder collaboration—effectively a multi-agent system involving farmers, researchers, and AI advisors—to co-create context-specific, sustainable farming practices [36]. In cloud-edge-terminal networks, Multi-Agent Deep Reinforcement Learning (MADRL) allows devices from different vendors to collaborate on complex tasks like offloading and caching, responding effectively to high-dimensional, dynamic environments without requiring an accurate, pre-defined system model [10].

These cases collectively underscore that the performance gains from agent scaling are most pronounced when agents are designed with specialization and integrated through architectures that enable effective coordination, knowledge sharing, and parallel processing. However, as the following discussion will illustrate, these benefits are not guaranteed; they critically depend on overcoming inherent challenges in system design, including transparency, coordination overhead, and robustness to input quality.

### 3.2 Critical Failure Modes and Performance Degradation

While the strategic scaling of agents can unlock significant performance gains, as detailed in the preceding empirical examples, this approach is not without substantial risks. Empirical evidence reveals several critical failure modes that can paradoxically lead to significant performance degradation as agent populations grow. A primary and immediate concern is the reduction in system transparency and interpretability. The intricate web of interactions and decision-making processes among multiple agents becomes increasingly opaque, making it difficult to audit or understand the rationale behind collective outputs. This challenge is highlighted in clinical settings, where a multi-agent system (MAS) for decision support was found to have a slightly lower transparency score compared to a single-agent counterpart, despite its superior predictive accuracy [37]. Similarly, user studies on conversational agents indicate that the perceived opacity of how LLMs process information hinders trust, a problem that is amplified when multiple specialized agents collaborate in a complex pipeline [16].

Furthermore, scaling introduces substantial coordination overhead, which can precipitate inefficiency or even systemic deadlock, eroding the very efficiency gains it seeks to create. Without meticulously designed coordination mechanisms, the benefits of parallel processing and specialization are quickly undermined by communication latency, conflicting sub-goals, and resource contention. The need for formal coordination is explicitly addressed in service-oriented architectures, where mathematical modeling is used to validate and verify interactions to prevent failures in cross-platform communications [23]. In decentralized systems like edge networks, the challenge of coordinating independent nodes necessitates sophisticated collaborative learning schemes to manage signaling overhead and maintain efficiency [38].

Finally, scaled multi-agent systems exhibit a heightened sensitivity to input quality and environmental noise. Errors or biases in initial data can be propagated and amplified through successive agent interactions, leading to cascading failures and erroneous conclusions. This risk is particularly acute in high-stakes domains like outbreak analytics, where the misinterpretation of data by an agent in a collaborative pipeline could have serious consequences, underscoring the need for rigorous validation and human oversight [4]. In financial Agentic AI systems, inconsistencies in interpreting high-dimensional data present a practical limitation that can undermine the reliability of autonomous credit risk assessments [39].

These failure modes—diminishing transparency, escalating coordination costs, and amplified vulnerability to poor inputs—collectively demonstrate that increasing agent quantity, without corresponding advances in system design, often leads to diminishing returns or outright performance decline. Crucially, these operational challenges directly enable and exacerbate the broader security, privacy, and ethical risks that emerge at scale, as will be explored in the following section.

### 3.3 Security, Privacy, and Ethical Risks Amplified by Scaling

Building upon the identified failure modes related to transparency and coordination, scaling the number of agents within a multi-agent system (MAS) also compounds and introduces novel security, privacy, and ethical risks. The expanded attack surface and amplified data processing inherent to larger, more interconnected agent collectives create systemic vulnerabilities that can directly undermine performance and trust, mirroring the cascading failures observed in other scaling contexts. A primary concern is the heightened exposure to adversarial attacks. As agent interactions increase in frequency and complexity—paralleling the coordination overhead discussed earlier—the potential for malicious inputs, prompt injection, or manipulation of inter-agent communication channels grows, potentially leading to widespread misinformation or system compromise [15]. Furthermore, the challenge of **authenticated delegation** becomes critical at scale. Determining whom an agent acts on behalf of and ensuring it operates within strictly defined permissions is a foundational security requirement. Without robust frameworks, unauthorized actions or privilege escalation by a single agent can have widespread consequences, a risk amplified by the reduced system transparency in large collectives. Research into frameworks for authenticated, authorized, and auditable delegation highlights the urgency of this issue, proposing extensions to existing protocols like OAuth to manage agent credentials and scope permissions securely [40].

Privacy risks are similarly amplified by the scale of interaction. A scaled MAS inherently processes larger volumes of potentially sensitive data across more nodes, each representing a potential vector for data leakage. This proliferation of data touchpoints exacerbates the challenge of maintaining **visibility** into agent activities—understanding where, why, and how agents operate—which is paramount for accountability but inherently conflicts with privacy preservation. Measures such as agent identifiers, real-time monitoring, and activity logging, while necessary for governance, raise significant concerns about user privacy and the potential for concentrated surveillance power [41]. This tension between necessary transparency for security and the right to privacy is a key governance challenge that becomes more acute with scaling.

Ethical risks also evolve in scale and complexity, extending beyond performance degradation to societal impact. The difficulty of ensuring consistent ethical alignment and accountability across a vast, dynamic agent ecosystem is immense, as a system's overall behavior emerges from countless opaque micro-interactions. This opacity can erode user trust, a finding supported by studies on conversational agents where users expressed concerns over how decisions are made and data is processed [16]. Moreover, scaling can introduce novel societal harms that transcend the operational environment. For instance, the pervasive integration of AI agents into daily life raises concerns about their impact on mental health, with evidence suggesting they may inadvertently reinforce delusional thinking or epistemic instability in vulnerable users—a risk that becomes more widespread as agent deployment scales [42]. Ultimately, scaling agent quantity without parallel advancements in secure delegation protocols, privacy-preserving oversight mechanisms, and ethical governance frameworks does not enhance quality but instead introduces significant systemic risks. These risks, if unmitigated, can degrade not only technical performance but also societal trust, thereby nullifying any theoretical benefits of increased agent numbers—a theme that directly connects to the following discussion on how environmental factors further constrain scaling efficacy.

### 3.4 Environmental and Operational Dependencies

The efficacy of scaling agent quantity is fundamentally contingent upon the operational environment in which the multi-agent system (MAS) is deployed, a factor that critically mediates the transition from theoretical benefits to realized performance. A poorly optimized or complex environment can not only induce systemic failures but also amplify their severity as more agents are introduced, thereby creating a counterproductive scaling effect that mirrors the compounded security and ethical risks discussed previously. Empirical analyses confirm that environmental factors are a primary and independent source of performance degradation. A seminal study, [43], provides a crucial taxonomy of agent-environment interaction failures, identifying six key failure modes. Among these, limitations in environment observability—where agents lack sufficient or accurate perceptual data—are particularly exacerbated at scale. As the collective information need of a larger agent network grows, the environment's capacity to provide it may not, leading to cascading errors that degrade overall system quality.

Furthermore, the operational environment imposes significant configuration and integration burdens that directly constrain scaling efficacy. In complex, heterogeneous systems like the cloud-edge-terminal collaborative network (CETCN), the dynamic and high-dimensional state space presents a formidable challenge to agent decision-making [10]. Scaling agents within such an environment without corresponding advancements in orchestration can overwhelm the system's capacity, turning potential coordination into detrimental contention. The requirement for agents to seamlessly operate across diverse contexts, as highlighted in [44], underscores this environmental dependency. If the environment cannot support rapid context-switching or on-demand policy composition, adding more agents simply increases the coordination load and the probability of context-inappropriate actions. Similarly, in networking ecosystems designed for agentic AI, the underlying framework's ability to support fluid interaction and knowledge transfer is a prerequisite for beneficial scaling [5]. Without these environmental supports, scaled agents operate in ineffective isolation, failing to achieve the collaborative gains that justify their increased numbers.

These dependencies underscore a critical principle: scaling agent quantity within a suboptimal environment often compounds existing issues rather than mitigating them, thereby setting a hard boundary on potential quality improvements. The environmental optimizations proposed in [43]—such as enhancing observability and offloading common computations—demonstrate that improving the environment itself can yield significant performance boosts (6.7-12.5%) without modifying the agents. This evidence powerfully argues that the environment is not a passive backdrop but an active and limiting determinant of scaling efficacy. Consequently, the pursuit of performance through agent scaling must be accompanied by a co-design philosophy that equally prioritizes the optimization and resilience of the operational environment. This approach is essential to prevent systemic failures from nullifying theoretical benefits and to create a foundation where increased agent numbers can genuinely enhance quality—a consideration that leads directly into the subsequent analysis of the economic and computational trade-offs inherent in such scaling efforts.

### 3.5 Economic and Resource Trade-offs

While the preceding analysis establishes the environment as a critical determinant of scaling efficacy, the decision to increase agent quantity is further constrained by significant economic and computational trade-offs. These factors present a critical counterpoint to the intuitive promise of performance gains, as adding agents simultaneously introduces non-linear increases in infrastructure, operational, and liability costs. A primary concern is the high computational expenditure required to deploy and maintain numerous agent instances, particularly when powered by large language models (LLMs) [30]. These costs are compounded by potential liability in high-stakes applications, where erroneous outputs from a scaled system could lead to severe consequences, further complicating economic viability [30]. Consequently, developing sustainable pricing models for agentic services becomes a formidable challenge, as providers must account for multi-dimensional Quality of Service (QoS), heterogeneous user needs, and information asymmetry [30].

Beyond direct monetary costs, the energy consumption of scaled multi-agent systems raises substantial environmental and operational concerns, creating a resource footprint that often scales faster than utility. Research in mobile edge computing networks highlights the critical objective of minimizing overall energy consumption while meeting performance requirements [45]. Similarly, work on aerial edge networks formulates optimization problems specifically to reduce the weighted energy consumption of both mobile users and unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), indicating that scaling agent populations without efficient coordination can lead to prohibitive energy use [46].

Collectively, these factors lead to the law of diminishing returns, where incrementally adding more agents yields minimal improvements in output quality or task success at a disproportionately high cost. The marginal benefit of each additional agent diminishes as coordination overhead, communication latency, and resource contention increase. This is evident in complex domains like outbreak analytics, where while multi-agent systems can enhance efficiency and scale, they require substantial investment in development, testing, validation, and staff training—costs that must be justified by tangible, super-linear performance gains [4]. Therefore, scaling decisions must be evaluated not just on potential quality improvements but on a comprehensive cost-benefit analysis that includes computational resource drain, economic sustainability, and environmental impact, framing agent quantity as a variable to be optimized rather than maximized.

## 4 Emerging Challenges and Future Directions for Sustainable Scaling

### 4.1 Governance, Accountability, and Ethical Frameworks

As the preceding sections have established, scaling multi-agent systems (MAS) to manage complex tasks can yield significant performance gains, particularly in domains like dynamic resource orchestration. However, this expansion in agent quantity and operational complexity necessitates a parallel and critical focus on governance, accountability, and ethical frameworks to prevent systemic failures and mitigate amplified risks. The core challenge lies in ensuring that a growing population of autonomous agents acts within defined permissions, maintains clear chains of responsibility, and remains auditable.

A foundational requirement for this is authenticated delegation, which formally clarifies the authority relationship between human users and AI agents. The framework proposed in [40] addresses this by extending established protocols like OAuth 2.0 to create agent-specific credentials, enabling secure and scoped task delegation while preserving accountability. Without such mechanisms, scaling agent populations can lead to unauthorized actions and obscured liability, undermining the very systems they are designed to enhance.

Closely tied to delegation is the need for sophisticated authorization protocols and real-time oversight, or visibility. As noted in [41], understanding where, why, and how agents are used is critical for governance. Scaling exacerbates the difficulty of monitoring agent activities, necessitating technical measures ranging from unique agent identifiers to comprehensive activity logging. These visibility tools are essential for detecting malfeasance, auditing decisions, and ensuring compliance with ethical guidelines, especially in decentralized deployment contexts. However, implementing these measures requires balancing informativeness with privacy concerns and avoiding undue concentration of power among infrastructure providers.

Furthermore, the ethical integration of scaled agent systems into high-stakes domains demands frameworks that ensure reliability and prevent harm. For instance, in healthcare, where agentic AI promises transformative benefits in diagnostics and patient care [1], the consequences of erroneous or ungoverned agent actions are severe. Similarly, in outbreak response, where multi-agent systems can enhance analytical pipelines [4], robust governance structures are necessary to validate outputs and prevent the dissemination of flawed conclusions that could impact public health. This underscores that scaling without governance risks amplifying biases, privacy violations, and operational hazards, negating potential quality improvements.

Ultimately, sustainable scaling requires architectural and policy innovations that embed accountability throughout the agent lifecycle. This includes designing systems where authorization is dynamically enforceable, actions are transparently logged, and ethical alignment is continuously verified. As the following subsection will explore in the context of dynamic resource orchestration, the challenges of coordination and optimization at scale are formidable. Future research must therefore focus on standardizing these governance frameworks to be as scalable and adaptive as the multi-agent systems they aim to control, ensuring that increases in agent quantity are matched by proportional enhancements in oversight, trustworthiness, and ultimately, system quality.

### 4.2 Dynamic Resource Orchestration in Complex and Heterogeneous Environments

Building upon the need for robust governance to manage the risks of scaling, the technical challenge of coordinating a growing agent population becomes paramount. As multi-agent systems (MAS) scale to manage increasingly complex and heterogeneous environments—such as large-scale communication networks or distributed IoT ecosystems—dynamic resource orchestration emerges as a critical yet formidable challenge. The core difficulty lies in achieving optimal performance, energy efficiency, and Quality-of-Service (QoS) under stringent, time-varying constraints without succumbing to the coordination overhead that scaling can introduce. Traditional centralized or static orchestration methods fail to adapt to the rapid dynamics and scale of these environments. Consequently, AI-driven solutions, particularly those leveraging decentralized intelligence and semantic reasoning, are at the forefront of research to enable sustainable scaling.

A prominent solution paradigm employs Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL), where agents learn decentralized policies for resource management through interaction with the environment. This approach is effective for problems like computation offloading and channel allocation in dynamic networks. For instance, a decentralized and collaborative machine learning architecture uses multi-agent DRL for energy-efficient computation offloading in 6G edge networks [38]. Similarly, a framework for vehicular networks utilizes a multi-agent deep deterministic policy gradient algorithm to jointly optimize channel and power allocation for heterogeneous QoS guarantees [27]. These MARL-based systems demonstrate that scaling the number of learning agents can improve resource utilization, but they also highlight the need for efficient training frameworks, such as federated learning, to manage signaling overhead and complexity at scale [38].

Beyond reactive learning, a more advanced direction is intent-driven orchestration, which uses high-level semantic goals to dynamically guide resource allocation. This is exemplified in agentic AI frameworks for next-generation networks. The FlexNGIA 2.0 architecture proposes LLM-based agents that can autonomously design, deploy, and adapt protocols and resource allocation strategies at runtime [29]. A more specialized implementation for Space-Air-Ground Integrated Networks (SAGIN) embeds LLM-based agents within a MAPE-K control plane. Here, an "intent-driven orchestrator" agent interprets high-level operator goals and collaborates with reinforcement learning agents by dynamically shaping their reward functions based on semantic network conditions, leading to significant gains in energy efficiency and latency [26]. This hybrid approach marries the planning and reasoning capabilities of LLM agents with the optimization prowess of RL agents, creating a scalable, hierarchical collaboration mechanism.

However, scaling such agentic systems for resource orchestration introduces new challenges that complicate the path from increased quantity to improved quality. The operational environment itself becomes a critical dependency; failures can arise from partial observability, complex configuration requirements, and the need for real-time adaptation. Furthermore, optimizing for multiple, often competing objectives—such as minimizing energy consumption while guaranteeing ultra-low latency—requires sophisticated multi-criteria decision-making. A hybrid decision support system for urban traffic control illustrates this well, integrating MARL for signal control with a fuzzy logic layer for energy-aware IoT routing, thereby jointly optimizing traffic flow and network lifetime [47]. These complexities underscore that the benefits of scaling are not automatic but contingent on overcoming significant coordination and optimization hurdles. Future research must therefore focus on developing more robust and explainable orchestration agents that can reason under uncertainty, negotiate resource conflicts in truly decentralized manners, and maintain stability as the number of managed resources and coordinating agents grows exponentially. The vision of frameworks like AgentNet, which proposes a generative foundation model-as-agent interactive knowledge base to bootstrap embodied agents for task-driven collaboration, points toward creating more adaptable and knowledge-sharing ecosystems for this purpose [5].

The technical and coordination challenges inherent in dynamic resource orchestration at scale directly set the stage for a critical subsequent consideration: the economic and market dynamics that determine whether scaled multi-agent ecosystems are sustainable and beneficial for all participants.

### 4.3 Economic and Market Dynamics in Multi-Agent Ecosystems

The economic viability and sustainability of scaled multi-agent systems are fundamentally governed by complex market dynamics and incentive structures, a critical consideration that emerges directly from the technical challenges of dynamic resource orchestration. As ecosystems comprising both AI and human agents grow, understanding the resulting economic equilibria becomes paramount for ensuring that scaling translates into genuine system-wide benefits. A critical insight from economic modeling is that scaling does not inherently lead to mutually beneficial collaboration or Pareto improvements in utility distribution. Research using Markov chain stationary distribution models reveals that in economies with multiple agents, equilibria can emerge where "more powerful" AI agents contribute zero utility to "less capable" agents [32]. This finding directly challenges the intuitive appeal of scaling, demonstrating that simply increasing agent quantity can lead to skewed utility distributions and ineffective collaboration unless specific, stringent conditions are met. For instance, in a two-agent system, collaboration in the form of purchasing each other's services only becomes an equilibrium outcome if each agent can at least *double* its marginal utility from the transaction, a high bar that underscores the fragility of cooperative equilibria in scaled settings.

Furthermore, scaling intensifies challenges related to service pricing and market efficiency, which are essential for the practical deployment of orchestrated systems. The high infrastructure, computation, and potential liability costs associated with agentic AI services necessitate sophisticated pricing frameworks that account for multi-dimensional Quality of Service (QoS) [30]. In scaled ecosystems with information asymmetry, designing incentive-compatible pricing models that align user needs with provider costs is a non-trivial task. Market failures are particularly pronounced in environments with information asymmetries, such as credence goods markets. Simulations with LLM agents show that without carefully designed institutional frameworks (e.g., liability rules) or explicit alignment of agent social preferences (e.g., towards efficiency or equity), markets can break down due to rampant fraud and consumer exploitation [48]. Scaling the number of self-interested agents in such environments often amplifies these inefficiencies, leading to high market concentration, polarized fraud patterns, and surplus distribution that may not align with social welfare goals. These findings indicate that the conditions for successful collaboration in a scaled ecosystem are tightly coupled with institutional design and the programmed social preferences of the agents themselves, rather than emerging naturally from increased numbers.

Therefore, future research must focus on designing adaptive economic mechanisms that can sustain healthy market dynamics at scale, a prerequisite for the effective human oversight and governance discussed subsequently. This includes developing dynamic incentive models that evolve with the ecosystem, robust reputation systems effective for AI-agent interactions, and governance structures that can enforce contracts and ensure accountability. The goal is to move beyond simple scaling and towards the orchestration of economically sustainable multi-agent ecosystems where increased agent quantity is coupled with mechanisms that promote fair utility distribution, stable service pricing, and resilient collaborative equilibria, thereby laying the economic foundation for scalable and trustworthy human-agent collaboration.

### 4.4 Human-Agent Collaboration and Effective Oversight

The economic and institutional prerequisites for scaling, as established in the preceding analysis, set the stage for the critical role of human governance in ensuring that increased agent quantity translates into enhanced system quality and safety. As multi-agent systems (MAS) scale in complexity and autonomy, the role of human oversight must evolve from direct, tactical control to strategic governance. Effective human-agent collaboration becomes paramount, especially in high-stakes domains where algorithmic decisions have significant consequences. This necessitates frameworks that meticulously balance increasing agent autonomy with unwavering accountability, manage the cognitive load on human supervisors, and ensure the ethical alignment of the entire system.

The transition is from a simple "human-in-the-loop" model to more sophisticated paradigms like "Human-in-Command" (HIC), which positions humans as strategic overseers responsible for final accountability and ethical alignment, particularly in sensitive sectors like customer service and finance [17]. This framework addresses the risk of automation complacency by defining clear operational triggers for strategic handoffs, ensuring humans intervene at critical junctures. A core technical enabler for this oversight is establishing secure and auditable chains of authority. As systems scale, understanding which human or entity an AI agent represents and what actions it is authorized to perform is non-trivial. Research into authenticated delegation proposes extending established protocols like OAuth 2.0 to create agent-specific credentials, enabling humans to securely delegate and restrict permissions while maintaining clear accountability [40]. This is crucial for preventing unauthorized actions and providing a foundational layer for governance.

Furthermore, the ethical imperative shifts when dealing with semi-autonomous agents. Rather than imbuing AI with human-like moral reasoning, a pragmatic approach focuses on embedding principles and restrictions that protect human interests, acknowledging the current limitations of agent autonomy [49]. Managing the human cognitive load in scaled systems is another critical dimension. The goal is not to overwhelm human supervisors with minutiae but to architect workflows where AI agents handle routine cognitive decision-making tasks, freeing humans to focus on nuanced judgment, exception handling, and strategic oversight [50]. This is evident in domains like outbreak analytics, where specialized LLM-based agents can perform time-intensive data curation and preliminary analysis, allowing skilled epidemiologists to concentrate on complex interpretation and policy recommendations [4]. Effective collaboration also requires systems designed for interpretability, providing human operators with transparent reasoning traces from agent interactions within Multi-Agent Based Decision Support Systems (MADSS) to build trust and facilitate informed intervention [7].

Future directions must therefore focus on developing adaptive oversight interfaces and collaborative protocols that scale with the agent ecosystem. This includes dynamic trust calibration mechanisms, context-aware alerting systems that prioritize human attention, and frameworks for "Humanity-in-the-Loop" that preserve human dignity and decisional ownership [17]. In mission-critical networks, this may involve AI agents that can explain their orchestration decisions to human network administrators [29]. Ultimately, sustainable scaling hinges on moving beyond viewing humans as mere operators or fail-safes, and instead designing symbiotic ecosystems where human expertise and agentic scalability are cohesively integrated. This governance layer is essential to ensure that the economic and technical potential of scaling is realized in a manner that enhances overall system quality, safety, and ethical integrity, thereby directly addressing the core question of when and how increased agent quantity yields genuine benefits.
