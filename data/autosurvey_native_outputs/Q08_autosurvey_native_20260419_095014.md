# The Impact of Evidence Agents on Evidence Coverage in Intelligence Reports: A Comprehensive Survey

## 1 Introduction to Evidence Agents and Intelligence Reporting

### 1.1 Defining Evidence Agents and Intelligence Reports

Building upon the overarching imperative for robust evidence coverage in intelligence workflows, it is essential to first establish clear and precise definitions for the core concepts of "evidence agents" and "intelligence reports." This foundational step delineates the scope of this survey and clarifies the relationship between the two. In this context, an **evidence agent** is defined as an autonomous or semi-autonomous artificial intelligence (AI) system engineered to perform the end-to-end lifecycle of evidence handling within complex information environments. This encompasses the automated or assisted gathering of data from diverse sources, the subsequent processing and validation of this information for credibility and relevance, and its synthesis into an integrated, actionable form. These agents operate within defined parameters to support analytical and decision-making processes, often leveraging architectures such as multi-agent systems (MAS) for collaborative work [1] or large language model (LLM) backbones for semantic reasoning and task execution [2]. Their design ranges from fully autonomous systems that perceive and act independently [3] to semi-autonomous agents that require meaningful human oversight and collaboration [4; 5].

Conversely, an **intelligence report** is conceptualized as a structured document or analytical product whose primary function is to synthesize processed information—or evidence—into coherent insights to support strategic, operational, or tactical decision-making. These reports are not mere data dumps; they are the culmination of an analytical workflow that involves assessing source reliability, contextualizing findings, identifying patterns, and presenting conclusions or forecasts under uncertainty. The imperative for high-quality intelligence reports spans numerous domains, from informing public health policy during outbreaks [2] to guiding financial risk assessments [3] and optimizing agricultural production [6]. Crucially, the value of such a report is intrinsically tied to the **evidence coverage** it provides—the breadth, depth, timeliness, and reliability of the evidentiary foundation upon which its analysis is built. By defining these two interconnected terms, this survey establishes the conceptual framework for examining how the integration of AI-driven evidence agents transforms the processes of evidence acquisition and integration, thereby directly impacting the quality and coverage of the resulting intelligence products—a critical link that sets the stage for the detailed analysis of this impact in the following sections.

### 1.2 The Imperative for Evidence Coverage in Intelligence Workflows

The imperative for robust evidence coverage is foundational to intelligence analysis, as the efficacy of assessments and the quality of subsequent decisions are directly contingent upon the comprehensiveness, accuracy, and timeliness of the evidentiary base. Within intelligence workflows, **evidence coverage** is thus defined as the extent to which all available, relevant, and validated information is systematically identified, integrated, and synthesized to form a coherent and complete situational picture. Gaps in this coverage—whether arising from missed sources, analytical biases, or processing delays—can lead to flawed assessments with potentially severe operational consequences.

This critical imperative is vividly demonstrated across high-stakes domains. In public health, for instance, effective outbreak response requires the rapid synthesis of diverse information streams—from real-time epidemiological data to emerging scientific literature—to generate actionable intelligence for policy makers [2]. Traditional, manual analytical pipelines create bottlenecks that risk delayed or incomplete evidence, directly undermining containment efforts. Similarly, in climate-responsive agriculture, managing dynamic and uncertain environmental conditions demands evidence coverage that is both technically deep and contextually broad. Static decision-support systems often fail to provide the adaptive, evidence-based recommendations needed for sustainable productivity, highlighting the risks of inadequate coverage [7].

The security and financial sectors present analogous demands. Comprehensive risk assessment, investment management, and incident analysis necessitate the integration and correlation of vast, multi-modal data streams. Inaccuracies or omissions in processing this information can result in significant financial losses or security failures. Research confirms that leveraging artificial intelligence, including agent-based systems, is pivotal for achieving transformative enhancements in analytical capabilities and operational outcomes [8]. Furthermore, the broader drive for service innovation emphasizes that improved information accuracy and accelerated response times—core dimensions of evidence coverage—are directly linked to superior decision quality and user experience [9].

Ultimately, the imperative for robust evidence coverage transcends mere data collection; it is about constructing a reliable and actionable intelligence foundation. Failures in this foundational process compromise the entire decision-making chain, which underscores why enhancing coverage through advanced methodologies—such as the integration of evidence agents—is a paramount concern in contemporary intelligence practice and a central focus of the following analysis.

### 1.3 Research Scope, Core Question, and Significance

Building upon the foundational imperative for robust evidence coverage in intelligence, this survey delineates a specific scope centered on the transformative impact of AI-driven evidence agents on the quality and comprehensiveness of intelligence reporting. The scope is intentionally focused on the operational and analytical influence of these autonomous or semi-autonomous systems—encompassing architectures like multi-agent systems (MAS) and LLM-based agents—on the metrics of evidence coverage. The analysis spans critical domains where evidence coverage is paramount, including public health outbreak response [2], financial management [10], and medical decision support [11]. The survey does not aim to be an exhaustive technical manual for agent design but rather a comprehensive analysis of their functional role in enhancing or potentially compromising the evidence base for decision-making.

The core research question guiding this inquiry is: **How do evidence agents influence the breadth, depth, and reliability of evidence in intelligence reports?** This tripartite question probes the essential dimensions of coverage. "Breadth" refers to the diversity and volume of sources and data types integrated, a capability highlighted in systems designed for scholarly communication analysis [12]. "Depth" concerns the granularity of analysis, contextual understanding, and iterative reasoning applied to evidence, a strength of agentic systems that perform complex, multi-step tasks [11]. By systematically addressing the core question, this work aims to inform both the responsible development of evidence agents and their effective integration into high-stakes decision-making processes, ultimately contributing to more robust, timely, and actionable intelligence.

### 1.4 Historical Context and Evolution Towards Agentic AI

The historical trajectory of intelligence reporting and evidence collection has undergone a profound transformation, evolving from manual, labor-intensive processes to the sophisticated, AI-driven agentic systems that now address the core imperatives outlined in the survey's scope. This evolution provides the essential context for understanding the contemporary drivers and technical architectures of evidence agents. Traditionally, intelligence workflows relied heavily on human analysts to manually gather, collate, and synthesize evidence from disparate sources—a process often hampered by cognitive biases, limited bandwidth, and delays in processing vast information volumes. Early computational support emerged in the form of Decision Support Systems (DSS), which provided structured data analysis but lacked the autonomy and advanced reasoning capabilities needed for comprehensive evidence coverage.

A significant conceptual leap was the development of Multi-Agent-Based Decision Support Systems (MADSS) [1], which introduced frameworks where autonomous software agents could collaborate. This paradigm established the foundational principle of distributed, specialized agents working towards a common analytical goal, bringing newfound adaptability and scalability to complex decision-making environments. The subsequent advent of large language models (LLMs) marked another pivotal milestone, endowing systems with unprecedented natural language understanding and generation abilities. Initially integrated under a passive "Model-as-a-Service" paradigm, LLMs were soon harnessed within more dynamic architectures. The need for goal-oriented systems capable of planning, executing tools, and reasoning iteratively spurred the shift towards "Agent-as-a-Service" [13], moving from static model invocation to deploying autonomous or semi-autonomous agents that orchestrate complex workflows. This shift is critically documented in domains like medical AI, where Agentic Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems autonomously coordinate clinical workflows and integrate evidence [11].

Further architectural innovations solidified this agentic turn, enhancing the ability to manage complex information environments. The agent-service approach, exemplified in intelligent decision support for sectors like energy, treats agents as interoperable services within a larger ecosystem, enabling flexible and scalable integration [14]. Concurrently, multi-agent architectures were refined for complex interactive tasks, such as in automated customer service systems where multiple AI agents collaborate to manage multi-turn dialogues and infer user intent [15]. The frontier now envisions a fully agentic networking ecosystem, as proposed for 6G, where generative foundation models act as agents that interact and transfer knowledge, pushing towards generally intelligent systems [16].

This historical arc—from manual collection to collaborative multi-agent systems, through the LLM revolution, and culminating in the service-oriented agentic paradigm—frames the contemporary landscape. It directly sets the stage for examining the powerful drivers that now propel the integration of these evolved systems into intelligence workflows to enhance evidence coverage.

## 2 Architectures and Mechanisms of Evidence Agents in Intelligence Systems

### 2.1 Foundational Architectural Paradigms

The architectural design of evidence agents fundamentally determines their capacity to gather, process, and integrate evidence within intelligence workflows. Three core paradigms have emerged as foundational: Multi-Agent Systems (MAS), Large Language Model (LLM)-based agents, and the Agent-Service approach. Each paradigm embodies distinct design principles and operational roles tailored to enhance evidence coverage through collaboration, semantic reasoning, and scalable integration, respectively.

**Multi-Agent Systems (MAS)** represent a distributed architectural paradigm where multiple autonomous or semi-autonomous software agents interact to achieve complex goals beyond the capability of a single agent. As will be elaborated in the following section, MAS frameworks enable collaborative evidence processing by delegating specialized tasks—such as data collection from specific sources, validation, and synthesis—to different agent types (e.g., user, decision, and knowledge agents) [1]. This architecture is prized for its inherent adaptability, flexibility, and scalability, allowing the system to dynamically coordinate and exchange information to handle complex, multi-source intelligence environments [1]. The operational role of MAS within intelligence workflows is to decompose large-scale evidence synthesis tasks into manageable subtasks executed in parallel or sequence, thereby improving the breadth and timeliness of evidence coverage through coordinated, specialized effort.

In contrast, **LLM-based agent architectures** center on the powerful semantic understanding and generative capabilities of foundation models. These agents are designed to perceive, reason, plan, and act using natural language as a primary medium. Their fundamental design principle leverages the LLM's ability to understand context, retrieve relevant information from vast corpora, execute code for analysis, and synthesize coherent reports. This makes them exceptionally suited for tasks requiring deep semantic reasoning and knowledge synthesis from unstructured or diverse data sources, a critical function for ensuring the depth and contextual relevance of evidence in reports. The literature highlights their role in formalizing and optimizing complex analytical workflows, such as in outbreak analytics, where specialized LLM-based agents can perform simultaneous, review-intensive tasks to enhance the consistency and quality of evidence [2]. Their operational role is often that of an intelligent orchestrator or a specialized analytical unit capable of iterative refinement and tool use.

The **Agent-Service paradigm**, sometimes aligned with concepts like Agent-as-a-Service, treats agents as modular, interoperable services with well-defined capabilities and APIs. This architectural approach emphasizes scalability and seamless integration into existing enterprise or operational systems, such as network management or customer service platforms. Drawing from network management literature, this paradigm envisions agents that can be dynamically discovered, composed, and invoked to perform specific evidence-gathering functions on demand [23]. The design principle is one of modularity and standardization, facilitating the flexible assembly of evidence-processing pipelines. Within an intelligence workflow, agent-service components can be plugged into larger systems to provide specific capabilities—like real-time data monitoring or specialized analytics—thereby extending the system's evidence coverage in a scalable and maintainable manner. This approach is crucial for building large-scale, heterogeneous intelligence infrastructures that require the integration of diverse, evolving tools and data sources.

### 2.2 Multi-Agent System (MAS) Frameworks for Collaborative Evidence Processing

Building directly upon the architectural overview of Multi-Agent Systems (MAS) as a distributed paradigm for collaborative evidence processing, this section delves into the specific structural and operational mechanisms that enable MAS frameworks to enhance evidence coverage. The core strength of a MAS lies in its typology of specialized agents and the sophisticated coordination protocols that govern their interactions, which together facilitate a comprehensive, parallelized approach to evidence gathering and synthesis.

As established in the literature on [1], a typical MAS for intelligence workflows employs a division of labor among distinct agent types. These commonly include user agents that interface with human analysts, decision agents tasked with evaluating alternatives and criteria, and knowledge agents responsible for managing and retrieving evidentiary data from diverse sources. This specialization allows for the parallel execution of tasks that would be sequential in manual processes. For instance, while a knowledge agent continuously harvests data from open-source intelligence (OSINT) feeds, a decision agent can concurrently assess the credibility of incoming information, thereby simultaneously expanding the breadth and scrutinizing the depth of the evidence base.

The efficacy of this multi-agent collaboration is governed by critical coordination mechanisms. Interaction protocols—such as contract net protocols, blackboard systems, or auction-based mechanisms—facilitate dynamic communication, task allocation, and result sharing. Through these protocols, agents engage in collaborative planning to decompose a high-level intelligence query into subtasks and in collaborative execution to integrate intermediate findings. A practical illustration can be found in financial intelligence for risk assessment, where a MAS might deploy one agent to scrape news for market sentiment, another to analyze transactional time-series data, and a third to synthesize these inputs to evaluate a company's stability, as suggested by applications in [10]. This orchestrated, multi-threaded approach directly enhances evidence coverage by ensuring multiple evidentiary dimensions are addressed in a coordinated manner.

Furthermore, the iterative nature of agent interactions within a MAS inherently supports the validation and dynamic management of evidence. Agents can be designed to critique and verify each other's outputs, establishing a system of checks and balances that improves the reliability of the synthesized intelligence. In clinical decision support, for example, a multi-agent system might utilize one agent to retrieve patient data, another to cross-reference clinical guidelines from a knowledge graph, and a third to flag potential contradictions, a process aligned with the collaborative reasoning described in [11]. Similarly, in manufacturing intelligence, an agent-based reconfiguration mechanism, as discussed in [24], enables production edge agents to negotiate and adapt based on real-time sensor data, ensuring evidence about production status is continuously integrated and managed. This capacity for continuous, validated integration transforms evidence processing from a monolithic activity into a dynamic, collaborative ecosystem, significantly augmenting the coverage, timeliness, and robustness of evidence for final reporting.

This detailed examination of MAS operational mechanics sets the stage for understanding the next architectural paradigm. While MAS excels at orchestrating specialized agents, the advent of LLM-based agent architectures, discussed in the following section, introduces a transformative capacity for deep semantic reasoning and autonomous tool use within such collaborative frameworks.

### 2.3 LLM-Based Agent Architectures for Semantic Reasoning and Knowledge Synthesis

Building upon the architectural foundations of Multi-Agent Systems (MAS), LLM-based agent architectures represent a transformative paradigm for evidence processing in intelligence systems, leveraging the advanced natural language understanding and generative capabilities of foundation models to operationalize agent collaboration at a granular level. These agents are designed to autonomously perform complex, evidence-oriented tasks by orchestrating a sophisticated cycle of reasoning, action, and reflection. A core architectural pattern involves equipping an LLM with the ability to interact with external tools and data sources, often operationalized through frameworks like ReAct (Reasoning and Acting). In this iterative loop, the agent reasons about a task, decides on an action (such as querying a database or executing code), observes the result, and continues until completion, enabling dynamic, goal-directed evidence gathering beyond static text generation.

A critical capability that enhances this process is semantic retrieval and contextual reasoning, significantly augmented by Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG). In a standard RAG setup, an agent retrieves relevant documents from a knowledge base to inform its response. The agentic evolution, known as Agentic RAG, involves autonomous agents managing the entire retrieval, generation, and validation workflow [11]. This allows for sophisticated evidence synthesis from vast, unstructured corpora. For instance, in outbreak analytics, LLM-based agents can perform simultaneous tasks like literature review, data extraction, and code-based analysis, synthesizing information from diverse sources to generate situational reports [2].

Furthermore, these architectures excel at tool use and code execution, allowing them to interface directly with analytical environments to process non-linguistic data. An LLM agent can write and execute Python code to clean datasets, run statistical models, or visualize trends, thereby transforming raw data into actionable evidence. The integration with structured knowledge sources adds another layer of semantic depth. As demonstrated in systems like HACID-CSR, agents can query a knowledge graph to retrieve precise domain concepts and relationships, which are then used to augment the LLM's internal reasoning for generating detailed, traceable workflows [25]. This combination of neural semantic understanding and symbolic knowledge retrieval enables the automated synthesis of coherent, context-aware evidence from highly heterogeneous information landscapes.

This inherent capacity for autonomous, tool-augmented processing positions LLM-based agents as fundamental components within the broader agent-service paradigm. Their ability to function as specialized, intelligent services facilitates the modular and interoperable architecture described next, where discrete capabilities can be dynamically composed to achieve comprehensive evidence coverage.

### 2.4 Agent-Service and Modular Approaches for Scalable Integration

The agent-service paradigm represents a pivotal architectural shift for deploying evidence agents within intelligence systems, moving away from monolithic, single-model implementations towards modular, service-oriented frameworks. This approach treats autonomous or semi-autonomous evidence-gathering agents as interoperable services, akin to microservices in modern software engineering. The core principle is to decompose the complex functionalities of an evidence agent—such as data retrieval, source validation, semantic analysis, and report synthesis—into discrete, collaborative capabilities that can be independently developed, scaled, and integrated. This modularity directly addresses the challenge of how to conveniently expand the application scenario of AI agents [26]. By decoupling capabilities from a single large language model (LLM), this paradigm reduces dependency on any one model's limitations, enhances system robustness, and improves the extensibility of both planning abilities and available tools [26].

Standardized application programming interfaces (APIs) are the linchpin of this architecture, enabling seamless communication and data exchange between heterogeneous agent services and legacy intelligence platforms. The push for Universal Open APIs for Agentic Natural Language Multimodal Communications [27] exemplifies this trend, aiming to establish interoperable interactions among diverse agents (e.g., chatbots, data mining bots) and human analysts. These APIs allow evidence-gathering functions to be "plugged into" existing operational systems, such as data lakes or analyst workbenches, facilitating scalable and flexible integration without requiring wholesale system overhauls.

Complementing APIs, efficient service discovery mechanisms are critical for dynamic, multi-agent intelligence environments. Agents must be able to locate and invoke specialized services on-demand. Frameworks like the one proposed by the Open Voice Network include a Discovery specification for looking up agents providing specific services via a standard Manifest publication [27]. More formally, systems like AgentDNS propose a root domain naming and service discovery system inspired by the traditional Domain Name System (DNS) [28]. AgentDNS is designed to enable LLM agents to autonomously discover, resolve, and securely invoke third-party agent and tool services across organizational boundaries, which is essential for orchestrating evidence collection from distributed, multi-source environments [28].

Capability collaboration is the operational ethos of this paradigm. Instead of a single agent attempting to perform all evidence-processing tasks, a workflow can dynamically assemble a team of specialized service-agents. For instance, one agent service might excel at scraping real-time data from social media, another at validating the credibility of a journalistic source using a knowledge graph, and a third at summarizing findings into a structured format. The CACA Agent framework explicitly employs this concept, integrating a set of collaborative capabilities to implement AI Agents [26]. This collaborative model mirrors the team-based approach advocated for complex analytical tasks, such as in outbreak analytics, where specialized LLM-based agents can divide labor to synthesize information from many different sources [2]. The result is a scalable, adaptable architecture where new evidence-gathering tools or analytical methods can be introduced as new services, immediately becoming discoverable and composable into broader intelligence workflows.

This modular, service-oriented architecture fundamentally enables the dynamic composition of agent capabilities, thereby significantly enhancing the system's capacity for comprehensive evidence coverage. It provides the structural foundation upon which the iterative operational cycles, described in the following subsection, can be effectively implemented and coordinated.

### 2.5 Operational Mechanisms: Perception, Planning, Execution, and Knowledge (PPEK) Cycles

Building upon the modular, service-oriented architecture, the operational efficacy of evidence agents in intelligence systems is governed by structured, iterative cycles that enable autonomous and adaptive evidence processing. These cycles, often conceptualized as Perception, Planning, Execution, and Knowledge (PPEK) loops or similar frameworks like the Monitor-Analyze-Plan-Execute-Knowledge (MAPE-K) model, provide the mechanistic foundation for dynamic evidence validation, adaptation, and continuous learning [29]. This operational logic translates the architectural potential of interoperable agent services into concrete, task-driven workflows.

The perception phase involves the agent's continuous monitoring of its environment, scanning diverse data streams, intelligence feeds, and contextual cues. For instance, in climate services, agents must perceive multi-hazard data and user queries to initiate workflow generation [25]. Following perception, the planning phase entails decomposing high-level intelligence tasks into actionable sequences through semantic reasoning and strategic orchestration. Here, agents formulate plans by interpreting operator intents and selecting appropriate analytical tools or sub-tasks, a process that can leverage the dynamic service discovery mechanisms described earlier [29].

The execution phase operationalizes these plans through tool-augmented actions, where agents interact with external APIs, databases, and computational tools to gather, process, and synthesize raw data. This phase is critical for evidence collection, as seen in outbreak analytics where agents execute code for data analysis or perform literature reviews [2]. Crucially, the knowledge phase encapsulates the learning and refinement loop. Agents evaluate execution outcomes, update their internal knowledge states, and refine future perceptions and plans. This iterative knowledge refinement, supported by memory capabilities, allows agents to learn from experience, correct errors, and enhance the reliability of subsequent evidence coverage [2].

In multi-agent systems, these PPEK cycles are coordinated across specialized agents (e.g., user, decision, and knowledge agents), enabling collaborative planning and execution that ensures comprehensive evidence validation from multiple perspectives [1]. Furthermore, the integration of structured knowledge sources within these cycles provides essential contextual grounding. As demonstrated in medical decision support and climate services, knowledge graphs offer agents a semantic framework to perceive and reason over, thereby improving the accuracy and traceability of generated evidence [11; 25]. This seamless integration of operational cycles with contextual knowledge and tool ecosystems forms a continuous feedback loop, ensuring agents can autonomously manage complex, evolving intelligence tasks. This dynamic adaptation not only sustains but systematically improves the quality and comprehensiveness of evidence coverage, directly setting the stage for the robust supporting infrastructures required to sustain this operational efficacy.

### 2.6 Supporting Infrastructures: Knowledge Graphs, Tools, and Evaluation Sandboxes

To sustain the dynamic, iterative operational cycles described previously, the efficacy of evidence agents in intelligence systems is fundamentally dependent on robust supporting infrastructures that enable contextual grounding, operational capability, and reliable evaluation. These components—domain-specific knowledge graphs, external tool ecosystems, and dedicated evaluation sandboxes—are not peripheral but core architectural elements that determine an agent's capacity to achieve comprehensive evidence coverage.

Domain-specific knowledge graphs serve as critical semantic frameworks for contextual grounding, allowing agents to navigate and reason within complex information spaces. By structuring entities, relationships, and attributes pertinent to a field (e.g., epidemiology, finance, or cybersecurity), knowledge graphs provide agents with a shared, interpretable model of the domain. This structured knowledge mitigates the risk of LLM hallucinations and enables more accurate evidence retrieval and synthesis, directly supporting the knowledge refinement phase of operational cycles. For instance, systems like AGENTiGraph demonstrate how multi-agent frameworks can intuitively interact with and dynamically update knowledge graphs through natural language, facilitating seamless reasoning and evidence integration for complex, multi-step queries [30]. In medical decision support, the integration of knowledge graphs is highlighted as a key architectural pattern for representing clinical knowledge and supporting guideline adherence within agentic RAG systems [11].

Concurrently, external tool ecosystems empower agents to transcend the inherent limitations of their parametric knowledge, operationalizing the execution phase of their workflows. By granting controlled access to APIs, databases, computational software, and real-time data streams, these tools act as the agent's "senses and hands" for evidence gathering. This capability is essential for accessing, processing, and analyzing multi-source evidence, from executing financial data queries to running outbreak analytics code. The shift towards treating agents as interoperable services within modular architectures further standardizes this tool integration, promoting scalability and flexibility [16].

Finally, rigorous evaluation sandboxes are paramount for assessing and ensuring the reliability of evidence agents in handling real-world tasks, closing the loop on the continuous improvement enabled by operational cycles. Traditional benchmarks often fail to capture the ecological validity of interacting with live, complex systems. Frameworks like Agent-Diff address this by introducing a novel state-diff contract for evaluation, which defines task success based on whether the agent achieves the expected change in a system's state rather than through fuzzy output matching [31]. By sandboxing containerized replicas of enterprise APIs, it allows for controlled, reproducible testing of an agent's ability to execute evidence-related workflows accurately. Similarly, the creation of domain-specific benchmarks, such as the Grounded Customer Service Benchmark introduced alongside the SOP-Agent framework, is crucial for evaluating agents against standardized operational procedures that mirror real intelligence and analytical workflows [32]. Together, these supporting infrastructures form the backbone that enables evidence agents to operate effectively, ensuring their evidence-gathering processes are context-aware, operationally capable, and verifiably reliable.

## 3 Effects of Evidence Agents on Evidence Coverage in Intelligence Reports

### 3.1 Conceptual Framework and Metrics for Assessing Evidence Coverage

To systematically evaluate the impact of evidence agents on intelligence reporting, a clear conceptual framework and associated metrics for "evidence coverage" must first be established. In the context of intelligence workflows, evidence coverage refers to the extent to which the information synthesized within a report comprehensively, accurately, and representatively addresses the intelligence question or operational scenario at hand. It is a multidimensional construct that goes beyond mere volume of data to encompass the quality, relevance, and provenance of the integrated evidence. Drawing on principles from agentic AI and decision support systems [1], we propose that evidence coverage can be assessed along several key dimensions: breadth, depth, timeliness, and source diversity.

Breadth refers to the scope and variety of evidence considered, ensuring multiple facets of a problem are examined. Depth measures the granularity and analytical rigor applied to each piece of evidence, including validation and contextual reasoning. Timeliness captures the latency between evidence generation and its integration into the reporting cycle, a critical factor in dynamic situations. Source diversity evaluates the range and independence of information origins (e.g., structured databases, unstructured reports, sensor feeds), which is crucial for mitigating bias and confirming findings.

A conceptual model linking evidence agent functionalities to these coverage dimensions emerges from the literature. For instance, the collaborative planning and specialized roles in Multi-Agent Systems (MAS) [1] directly enhance breadth and depth by enabling parallel evidence gathering and multi-perspective analysis. The semantic reasoning and tool-augmented retrieval capabilities of LLM-based agents [11] improve depth through contextual understanding and source diversity by accessing heterogeneous data repositories. Furthermore, the autonomous, continuous operational cycles of agentic systems fundamentally enhance timeliness by processing incoming information without latency, a capability critical for outbreak analytics [2].

This framework provides a structured basis for analyzing how specific architectural components—such as knowledge graphs for contextual grounding or agentic workflows for iterative validation—contribute to measurable improvements in evidence coverage. It thus establishes the necessary foundation for the empirical assessments of agent impact across diverse domains, as detailed in the following section.

### 3.2 Empirical Evidence from Domain-Specific Case Studies

Empirical studies across diverse domains provide concrete validation for the conceptual framework, demonstrating how evidence agents tangibly impact the breadth, depth, timeliness, and source diversity of evidence coverage in analytical reports. These impacts are evident in several critical application areas.

In healthcare, agentic systems significantly enhance evidence synthesis for clinical decision support and outbreak response. Research detailed in [11] shows how multi-agent systems autonomously coordinate complex clinical workflows, integrating multi-modal patient data, medical literature, and guidelines to produce comprehensive diagnostic and treatment reports with improved accuracy, directly enhancing both the depth and source diversity of evidence. For outbreak analytics, specialized LLM-based agents address the critical need for real-time, multi-source evidence synthesis during epidemics like mpox and COVID-19 [2]. By automating time-intensive manual tasks such as data extraction, cleaning, and code generation, these agents enable parallel processing of diverse information streams. This leads to more timely and complete situational reports, expanding evidence coverage by integrating epidemiological data, genomic sequences, and literature reviews—thereby improving timeliness, breadth, and mitigating bottlenecks inherent to human capacity.

In domains such as climate services and agricultural adaptation, evidence agents improve coverage by dynamically integrating heterogeneous, real-time data. The framework presented in [33] illustrates how LLM-driven agents orchestrate hydrodynamic models, geospatial datasets, and policy documents to generate multi-hazard assessment reports. This provides a more holistic evidence base for flood risk and mitigation planning than static systems, directly contributing to greater breadth and depth. Similarly, in agricultural adaptation, the agentic AI-driven framework using reinforcement learning [7] synthesizes climate, soil, and crop data within a digital twin environment. This results in decision-support reports with evidence covering optimized resource use and yield projections under various climate scenarios, directly addressing the evidence gaps faced by traditional extension services highlighted in [34].

For security and intelligence workflows, evidence agents demonstrably enhance the depth and reliability of analysis in incident reports. Research on root cause analysis (RCA) in cloud systems [35] provides empirical evidence that LLM-based agents equipped with retrieval tools (e.g., ReAct agents) can access and synthesize diagnostic information from logs, metrics, and databases. This capability overcomes a major limitation of prior LLM approaches, substantially increasing the factual accuracy and evidentiary depth of RCA reports by dynamically collecting and integrating operational evidence beyond static incident descriptions. This demonstrates a direct improvement in evidence coverage for post-incident intelligence, particularly in depth and source diversity. Furthermore, the operational scalability of agentic services, as discussed in pricing models like [36], underpins the ability to maintain consistent evidence coverage across multiple, simultaneous intelligence tasks—a capability for parallel, continuous operation also emphasized in healthcare contexts [17].

Collectively, these empirical cases substantiate the proposed framework, showing how agentic architectures translate into measurable enhancements across the key dimensions of evidence coverage. This sets the stage for a synthesized discussion of the core benefits these impacts yield, as explored in the following subsection.

### 3.3 Benefits: Enhancement of Accuracy, Completeness, and Scalability

Building upon the empirical demonstrations of impact across diverse domains, evidence agents demonstrably enhance evidence coverage in intelligence reports by systematically improving three core dimensions: analytical accuracy, informational completeness, and operational scalability. This triad of benefits directly addresses the fundamental requirements for high-quality intelligence reporting.

A primary benefit is the significant enhancement of analytical accuracy and the reduction of errors or hallucinations. By employing structured workflows and iterative validation mechanisms, evidence agents mitigate the risks associated with raw large language model (LLM) outputs. For instance, specialized LLM-based agents formalize team interactions and incorporate multiple review steps before presenting findings to an analyst, yielding higher-quality and more consistent outputs than direct prompting [2]. In medical decision support, agentic Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems integrate knowledge graphs and adhere to clinical guidelines, which grounds responses in verified sources and reduces factual inaccuracies [11]. Furthermore, frameworks that combine LLM-based reasoning with reinforcement learning, as seen in network resource management, allow for dynamic reward shaping based on semantic conditions, leading to more precise and context-aware optimizations [29].

Secondly, evidence agents substantially increase the completeness of evidence coverage through sophisticated multi-source integration and iterative reasoning. These systems are designed to synthesize information from diverse, often unstructured sources, which is a critical capability in complex domains like intelligence analysis and healthcare. Multi-Agent Based Decision Support Systems (MADSS) exemplify this by enabling collaborative decision-making among specialized agents (e.g., user, decision, and knowledge agents) that analyze vast amounts of data and evaluate various alternatives [1]. In healthcare, next-generation agentic AI systems leverage multimodal AI to integrate diverse data sources and iteratively refine outputs, thereby delivering more comprehensive, context-aware, and patient-centric insights [17]. This capability for continuous, loop-based reasoning—perceiving, planning, executing, and refining knowledge—ensures that evidence coverage is not a static snapshot but a dynamically improving representation of the information environment.

Finally, evidence agents enable unprecedented scalability in evidence-gathering operations, a foundational capability for intelligence work. They facilitate autonomous, parallel task execution and provide 24/7 operational capacity, which is essential for managing large-scale or rapidly evolving situations. The deployment of multiple LLM-based agents allows for the simultaneous performance of numerous analytical tasks, overcoming major bottlenecks in delivering analysis at scale during outbreaks [2]. This scalability extends to service innovation, where AI Agents automate manual processes, reducing operational workload and accelerating response times significantly [9]. Architecturally, the agent-service paradigm and modular frameworks allow for the flexible and scalable integration of evidence-gathering functions into existing systems. Visionary proposals for fully autonomous networks further illustrate this scalability, where AI agents can dynamically design, implement, and optimize complex protocols and resource allocations without constant human intervention [23].

Collectively, these benefits of accuracy, completeness, and scalability transform evidence coverage from a labor-intensive, error-prone process into a robust, expansive, and continuously operating function. However, as the following discussion will detail, realizing this potential is contingent upon navigating significant challenges that can undermine these very advantages.

### 3.4 Challenges: Security Risks, Ethical Concerns, and Operational Limitations

Despite the transformative potential of evidence agents in enhancing analytical accuracy, informational completeness, and operational scalability, their integration into intelligence workflows introduces critical challenges that can degrade or compromise the very evidence coverage they aim to improve. These challenges span security vulnerabilities, ethical dilemmas, and operational limitations, each posing substantial risks to the reliability and integrity of intelligence outputs.

Security risks represent a foremost concern, as agentic systems operating in open or networked environments are susceptible to attacks that can corrupt the evidence-gathering process. For instance, agents can be vulnerable to prompt injection or hijacking, where malicious inputs subvert their reasoning to produce biased or fabricated evidence [37]. This is particularly acute in cybersecurity applications, where agents tasked with vulnerability reproduction may themselves become attack vectors if not properly secured. Furthermore, significant user concerns regarding data security emphasize that robust protection measures are crucial to prevent breaches that could leak sensitive intelligence sources or compromise agent operations [20].

Ethical concerns directly impact the perceived and actual reliability of evidence coverage. A primary issue is algorithmic bias, where agents trained on skewed data may systematically overlook or misrepresent evidence from certain demographics or regions, leading to incomplete or prejudiced intelligence assessments [38]. The lack of empathy and contextual nuance in purely automated systems can also result in the mishandling of sensitive human-source information. Transparency, or the lack thereof, is a recurrent challenge; users and analysts often cannot trace how an agent sourced, weighted, or synthesized specific pieces of evidence, which erodes trust and complicates validation [20]. This opacity conflicts with the need for explainable decision-making in high-stakes intelligence workflows, as noted in frameworks for ethical AI governance [39].

Operational limitations present practical barriers to achieving consistent and comprehensive evidence coverage. The computational and infrastructure demands for running advanced multi-agent or LLM-based systems are substantial, potentially limiting deployment in resource-constrained settings [3]. Model drift—where an agent's performance degrades over time as real-world data evolves—can silently erode the accuracy of evidence retrieval and synthesis. Validation remains a persistent difficulty; for example, in outbreak analytics, while agents can accelerate evidence synthesis, their outputs require rigorous validation against established benchmarks to prevent erroneous conclusions that could misguide policy [2]. Moreover, agents often struggle with complex, multi-component environments requiring nuanced authentication and configuration, leading to scenarios where they execute tasks but fail to generate valid evidence, thus creating coverage gaps [37].

These interconnected challenges underscore that evidence agents are not a panacea. Their effective contribution to evidence coverage is contingent upon overcoming significant technical and governance hurdles, which in turn necessitates the evolution of human roles and collaborative structures, as will be explored in the following discussion on workflow implications.

### 3.5 Implications for Intelligence Workflow and Human-Agent Collaboration

Building upon the critical challenges outlined in the previous section, the successful integration of evidence agents fundamentally reshapes intelligence workflows, necessitating a corresponding re-evaluation of human roles and collaborative structures. The primary implication is the evolution from manual, sequential analysis towards a dynamic, hybrid ecosystem where human analysts and AI agents co-exist in symbiotic loops. This shift transcends mere task automation, focusing instead on augmenting human cognitive capabilities to achieve superior evidence coverage. A critical model emerging from this evolution is the "Human-in-Command" (HIC) framework, which positions the human analyst as the ultimate authority overseeing the agentic system's operations [4]. This framework is crucial for maintaining accountability, ensuring ethical alignment, and mitigating risks like automation bias—a direct response to the ethical and validation challenges previously discussed—thereby safeguarding the integrity of the evidence gathered.

Furthermore, evidence agents introduce new operational paradigms into the intelligence cycle. The ability of multi-agent systems to perform parallel, specialized tasks—such as simultaneous data harvesting, validation, and preliminary analysis—compresses the traditional timeline from collection to reporting [15]. This necessitates that human workflows adapt to a more iterative and interactive model, where analysts engage in continuous dialogue with agents to refine queries, validate intermediate findings, and steer the investigative direction based on emerging evidence patterns. The architecture of systems like the CACA Agent, which uses capability collaboration for extensibility, exemplifies how workflows can become more modular and scalable, allowing analysts to dynamically assemble agent teams tailored to specific coverage objectives [26].

However, this enhanced collaboration also imposes new cognitive demands on human analysts, who must develop skills in agent oversight, prompt engineering, and interpreting probabilistic agent outputs. The goal is to achieve a state of "Humanity-in-the-Loop," a holistic concept extending beyond HIC to preserve human dignity and decisional ownership [4]. In domains like outbreak analytics, where timely evidence is critical, this model empowers analysts to focus on high-level interpretation and decision-making by offloading labor-intensive evidence gathering and synthesis to LLM-based agents [2]. Ultimately, the implication is a transformed workflow where evidence agents act as force multipliers, expanding coverage potential, while sustained human collaboration ensures the coverage is relevant, ethically sound, and actionable—setting the stage for a forward-looking assessment of this evolving field.
