# The Role of Screening Agents in Multi-Agent Collaborative Intelligence Services: A Comprehensive Survey

## 1 Introduction to Screening Agents and Multi-Agent Collaborative Intelligence

### 1.1 Defining Screening Agents and Multi-Agent Collaborative Intelligence

Multi-Agent Collaborative Intelligence (MCI) represents a sophisticated paradigm in which multiple autonomous artificial intelligence (AI) agents interact, coordinate, and cooperate to solve complex problems beyond the capability of any single agent. This paradigm, rooted in multi-agent systems (MAS) principles, is designed to achieve adaptability, flexibility, and scalability in dynamic environments [1]. Within an MCI system, agents are typically endowed with specialized capabilities—such as data analysis, decision-making, or task execution—and work collectively toward a common objective through structured communication and coordination protocols. The advancement of this paradigm is closely linked to progress in foundational AI, particularly the integration of large language models (LLMs) and generative foundation models, which endow agents with enhanced reasoning and natural language understanding [2].

As the preceding discussion on the historical evolution indicates, the shift from monolithic decision support systems to distributed, AI-augmented ecosystems created a pressing need for internal coordination mechanisms. This need is addressed within the MCI framework by the specialized role of the Screening Agent. Formally defined, a screening agent is a supervisory or managerial agent whose core purpose is to orchestrate workflows and manage collaboration among a collective of subordinate or peer agents. Unlike general-purpose user agents, decision agents, or knowledge agents that perform specific domain tasks [1], the screening agent functions as an intelligent gatekeeper and coordinator. Its primary responsibilities include filtering incoming information streams, dynamically delegating tasks based on assessed agent capabilities, and monitoring the overall quality and security of the collaborative process. This role is essential for managing complexity and preventing bottlenecks, particularly in scenarios characterized by data overload or requiring scalable service orchestration [3].

The distinction between a screening agent and other agent types lies in its meta-level operational focus. While other agents are engaged in executing substantive work—such as a perception agent analyzing network conditions or a learning agent optimizing a policy—the screening agent manages the process itself. It ensures that the right task reaches the most suitable agent, resolves conflicts, validates outputs, and enforces system-level policies like security and access control. This orchestration function is critical for optimizing resource utilization and maintaining system efficiency, as demonstrated in frameworks designed for complex network management and collaborative production scheduling [4; 5]. Consequently, the screening agent serves not merely as a participant but as the linchpin that enables coherent, efficient, and reliable multi-agent collaborative intelligence services, a role whose historical emergence and functional necessity are further elaborated in the following subsection.

### 1.2 Historical Evolution and Contextual Emergence

The historical evolution of screening agents is inextricably linked to the broader trajectory from isolated decision support tools to modern, AI-augmented collaborative intelligence ecosystems, a progression that underscores their functional necessity as outlined in the preceding overview of the Multi-Agent Collaborative Intelligence (MCI) paradigm. The foundational shift began with traditional Decision Support Systems (DSS), which were largely monolithic and rule-based, designed to assist human analysts in structured problem-solving within bounded domains. The advent of Multi-Agent Systems (MAS) theory introduced a paradigm of distributed intelligence, where autonomous software entities could collaborate. This directly led to the conceptualization of Multi-Agent-Based Decision Support Systems (MADSS), which leverage the principles of multi-agent systems, artificial intelligence, and decision support systems to enable collaborative decision-making among multiple autonomous agents [1]. These early MADSS frameworks highlighted the advantages of agent-based approaches in terms of adaptability, flexibility, and scalability, setting the stage for more sophisticated orchestration and the eventual need for a dedicated managerial agent.

The contextual emergence of specialized screening agents was catalyzed by two convergent pressures: data overload and the demand for scalable, complex service orchestration. As digitalization accelerated, systems in finance, customer service, and healthcare were inundated with heterogeneous, high-velocity data streams, overwhelming traditional analytical and human capacities. Concurrently, the tasks themselves grew in complexity, requiring the coordination of multiple specialized AI modules. For instance, in customer service, the shift from simple rule-based automation to systems supporting complex, multi-turn conversations necessitated architectures where multiple intelligent agents work together [6]. In this environment, a central coordinating intelligence became essential to manage workflow, filter noise, and delegate tasks effectively—functions that define the core purpose of the screening agent.

The rise of agentic AI and large language models (LLMs) represents the latest and most transformative phase in this evolution, directly enabling the advanced capabilities of modern screening agents. LLMs endowed agents with advanced natural language understanding and reasoning, enabling more nuanced interaction and planning. This empowered the development of frameworks where LLM-based agents could formalize team interactions, optimize workflows, and enhance consistency and reproducibility, as noted in contexts like outbreak analytics [3]. In complex, dynamic domains such as space-air-ground integrated networks (SAGIN) resource management, the need for semantic awareness and intent-driven orchestration led to frameworks embedding LLM-based agents into control loops like MAPE-K for autonomous management [4]. Similarly, in finance, the demand for autonomous, real-time credit risk decision-making spurred Agentic AI frameworks that integrate multi-agent collaboration with explainable AI modules [7].

Thus, the screening agent has emerged as a critical architectural component within this historical arc, serving as the linchpin for coherent multi-agent collaboration. It is the evolutionary response to the limitations of earlier systems, acting as the intelligent filter and orchestrator that manages information flow, delegates tasks within a multi-agent team, and ensures quality and coherence in collaborative intelligence services. Its development reflects the ongoing transition from tools that assist human decision-makers to autonomous, collaborative systems that require internal governance and coordination mechanisms to function effectively at scale. This historical foundation sets the stage for the detailed examination of architectural paradigms, functional mechanisms, and applications that follows in the subsequent survey objectives.

## 2 Architectural Frameworks and Enabling Technologies

### 2.1 Foundational Design Paradigms

The architectural design of screening agents within multi-agent collaborative intelligence services is fundamentally shaped by the choice between monolithic and modular paradigms, a decision that directly influences their capacity for context-aware reasoning and semantic filtering. These foundational design decisions dictate the agent's flexibility, scalability, and ability to manage complex, information-dense workflows. A monolithic architecture integrates all reasoning, planning, and execution capabilities into a single, cohesive agent entity. While conceptually straightforward, this approach often leads to structural bottlenecks, such as unconstrained reasoning outputs that inflate task trajectories and complicate learning from sparse, outcome-level rewards [15]. This design can reduce the extensibility of the agent's functionality, making it challenging to adapt to new tasks or integrate specialized tools without significant retooling of the core model [16]. Consequently, monolithic designs may struggle to support the sophisticated knowledge representation and dynamic reasoning required for effective screening in collaborative ecosystems.

In contrast, modular or multi-agent designs explicitly decouple functionalities into distinct, collaborative roles. This paradigm is central to the operation of effective screening agents, which often act as specialized orchestrators within a larger ecosystem. For instance, the **M-ASK** framework decomposes agentic search into separate Search Behavior Agents and Knowledge Management Agents, where the latter effectively performs a screening role by aggregating, filtering, and maintaining a compact internal context [15]. This separation of concerns allows screening agents to focus on semantic prioritization and workflow coordination, reducing interference between exploratory actions and context construction, thereby stabilizing system performance and enabling more precise information filtering.

Representative frameworks illustrate the practical implementation and advantages of these modular paradigms. The **CACA Agent** adopts an open, service-inspired architecture that integrates a set of collaborative capabilities rather than relying on a single LLM for all reasoning [16]. This design inherently supports a screening function, as the architecture facilitates the dynamic assessment and delegation of tasks to specialized capability modules, enhancing both extensibility and robustness. Similarly, **AgentNet** proposes a framework for agentic AI networking that supports interaction and collaborative learning among diverse autonomous agents [2]. Within such an ecosystem, screening agents can emerge as key coordinators, leveraging a generative foundation model-as-agent knowledge base to bootstrap and manage embodied agents according to dynamic task requirements and environmental features.

These modular frameworks underscore a shift towards systems where screening agents operate not as monolithic controllers but as intelligent mediators within a federated, capability-driven architecture. This architectural evolution directly supports the subsequent need for effective knowledge representation and reasoning, as it creates a structure where screening agents can optimally utilize technologies like knowledge graphs and ontologies to interpret context, filter information, and make informed orchestration decisions. By optimizing resource utilization and enabling scalable service orchestration, such designs provide the necessary foundation for screening agents to execute their core functions within complex multi-agent intelligence services.

### 2.2 Knowledge Representation and Reasoning Technologies

Building upon the architectural paradigms that enable specialized orchestration, the operational intelligence of screening agents in multi-agent collaborative services is critically dependent on effective knowledge representation and reasoning. These technologies provide the structured semantic frameworks necessary for agents to interpret complex contexts, filter information streams, and make informed orchestration decisions, thereby bridging architectural design with adaptive learning capabilities.

A primary enabling technology is the knowledge graph, which models domain concepts and their interrelationships as a network of entities and edges. Screening agents leverage these graphs to perform semantic reasoning, enabling them to understand task dependencies, agent capabilities, and contextual constraints. For instance, systems like AGENTiGraph utilize knowledge graphs to allow intuitive, natural language interaction and dynamic knowledge base management, which screening agents can query to understand domain-specific relationships [17]. Similarly, domain-agnostic knowledge graph-as-a-service infrastructures support the creation of curated knowledge for intelligent agents to deploy timely, context-aware actions, such as in active cyber defense [18].

Ontologies provide a complementary, formal logical specification of a domain's concepts and are crucial for standardizing terminology and enabling interoperability among heterogeneous agents. This ontological grounding ensures that a screening agent's semantic filtering aligns with a shared understanding of the domain. In clinical decision support, multi-agent systems rely on medical ontologies to integrate and coordinate specialized knowledge from disparate sources, allowing a screening agent to semantically align tasks with the appropriate specialist agent [19]. This principle extends to climate services, where a curated domain knowledge graph (CS-DKG) provides the structured context for agentic systems to generate accurate workflows [20].

To enable dynamic operation within these structured frameworks, entity embeddings offer a vector-based representation that captures semantic similarities and contextual nuances. These embeddings allow screening agents to perform efficient similarity searches and context-aware policy composition without exhaustive retraining, facilitating rapid adaptation. For example, one approach represents heterogeneous contexts through knowledge graphs and entity embeddings, allowing agents to compose policies on-demand by understanding semantic proximity between different situational contexts [21].

Ultimately, the integration of these representation technologies with advanced reasoning pipelines creates a powerful stack for semantic processing. Techniques like Graph Retrieval-Augmented Generation (GraphRAG) empower screening agents to retrieve relevant subgraphs, reason over them, and augment their decision-making processes, thereby enhancing the accuracy and traceability of their filtering and orchestration functions [20]. This deep, structured understanding provided by knowledge graphs, ontologies, and embeddings is indispensable. It equips screening agents with the semantic foundation required not only for context-aware reasoning but also to effectively support the subsequent learning and adaptation mechanisms that drive dynamic policy optimization in collaborative ecosystems.

### 2.3 Learning and Adaptation Mechanisms

Building upon the foundational semantic frameworks provided by knowledge representation, the operational efficacy of screening agents in multi-agent collaborative intelligence services is fundamentally predicated on their capacity for learning and adaptation. Unlike static rule-based filters, modern screening agents employ sophisticated machine learning (ML) techniques to dynamically refine their policies for information prioritization, task delegation, and system coordination. This adaptive capability forms a critical bridge to the advanced cognitive functions enabled by generative AI, discussed subsequently. Among these ML techniques, reinforcement learning (RL) and its multi-agent extensions constitute a core technological pillar, enabling agents to learn optimal behaviors through interaction with a dynamic environment and feedback from other agents in the collective. These mechanisms allow screening agents to transition from pre-programmed entities to adaptive orchestrators that optimize collaborative workflows for strategic objectives.

Reinforcement learning provides a natural framework for screening agents to learn adaptive policies for sequential decision-making. By framing the screening process—such as filtering data streams or delegating tasks—as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), the agent learns a policy that maps states (e.g., current workload, incoming information quality) to actions (e.g., prioritize, route, reject) to maximize a cumulative reward signal representing system-wide goals like efficiency or accuracy. This enables the agent to discover non-obvious strategies for optimizing collaboration. For instance, in supply chain management, a deep RL agent can learn robust inventory ordering policies that perform well even when collaborating with unpredictable partners, a capability demonstrated in an application to the decentralized Beer Game [22]. The agent's policy, once trained, executes in real-time, showcasing RL's power for adaptive, model-free optimization in complex, multi-agent environments.

In the context of multi-agent systems, single-agent RL is often insufficient due to the non-stationarity introduced by other learning agents. This necessitates Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) and, more specifically, Multi-Agent Deep Reinforcement Learning (MADRL), where agents utilize deep neural networks as function approximators. Screening agents, acting as central coordinators or privileged observers, can leverage MADRL to learn policies that explicitly account for the behaviors and states of subordinate agents. A common paradigm is centralized learning with decentralized execution, where a screening agent learns a global policy during a training phase using system-wide information but deploys localized policies for each agent during operation. This approach is effectively employed for complex resource allocation problems, such as jointly optimizing trajectory, power, and channel allocation in multi-UAV networks to maximize system utility [23]. Similarly, for guaranteeing heterogeneous Quality-of-Service in vehicular networks, a multi-agent deep deterministic policy gradient algorithm enables intelligent resource allocation that adapts to dynamic network conditions [24].

The adaptation mechanisms extend beyond policy learning to include collective intelligence sharing, which enhances the learning efficiency of the entire agent collective. Screening agents can facilitate or govern this process. For example, in edge computing networks, a collective deep reinforcement learning (CDRL) scheme allows distributed edge nodes to share learned intelligence models, thereby improving overall learning performance and service quality without massive repetitive training [25]. The screening agent's role could involve curating which intelligence models are shared and with whom, based on node reputation and current network states. Furthermore, in agricultural decision-support, agentic AI frameworks utilize RL agents trained with algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization to adapt crop management strategies dynamically to climatic variability, optimizing for yield and sustainability [26]. The screening agent in such a system would be responsible for integrating these adaptive agent recommendations into a coherent, actionable plan.

Ultimately, these learning and adaptation mechanisms empower screening agents with strategic optimization capabilities. They learn not just to react, but to proactively orchestrate collaboration by anticipating the needs and potential conflicts within the agent collective. This is evident in frameworks designed for confrontational environments, where improved multi-agent cooperative learning methods enable the integration of individual agent strategies into a cohesive, adaptive group strategy [27]. Through continuous learning from feedback loops—monitoring performance outcomes and system states—screening agents can iteratively refine their filtering criteria, delegation logic, and conflict resolution protocols. This capacity for self-improvement leads to increasingly resilient and efficient collaborative intelligence services, setting the stage for their enhancement through the advanced natural language and reasoning capabilities of Large Language Models.

### 2.4 Integration with Foundational AI and LLMs

The integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) and other generative foundation models constitutes a pivotal technological enabler for screening agents, significantly augmenting their core functionalities in multi-agent collaborative intelligence services. This augmentation directly builds upon the adaptive learning mechanisms discussed previously, elevating screening agents from policy-driven optimizers to sophisticated cognitive orchestrators. By leveraging advanced natural language understanding and generation capabilities, screening agents can interpret complex, unstructured task descriptions from users, comprehend nuanced contextual information from diverse data streams, and facilitate seamless communication within the agent ecosystem [28]. This linguistic proficiency is fundamental for semantic filtering and prioritization, enabling agents to parse intent and meaning rather than relying solely on structured metadata.

Beyond basic comprehension, LLMs empower screening agents with sophisticated planning and reasoning abilities. For instance, an LLM-enhanced screening agent can decompose a high-level user query into a structured workflow, logically sequence subtasks, and reason about the most suitable specialist agents for delegation based on inferred task requirements and agent capabilities described in natural language [29]. This moves beyond simple rule-based or even learned policy-based orchestration towards more adaptive, context-aware, and explainable planning, effectively realizing the strategic optimization potential outlined in the preceding section.

Furthermore, foundation models serve as the cognitive core that allows screening agents to perform integrative reasoning across heterogeneous knowledge sources. By processing retrieved information from knowledge bases, databases, or the outputs of other agents, LLM-powered screening agents can synthesize, summarize, and evaluate content to make informed filtering and routing decisions [28]. This capability is exemplified in systems where a screening agent utilizes an LLM to assess the relevance and quality of intermediate results before channeling them to the next stage in the pipeline. The integration also enables more sophisticated forms of collaboration management; for example, screening agents can use LLMs to generate negotiation prompts for conflict resolution or to formulate feedback for system optimization based on historical interaction logs.

However, as noted in several application studies, native LLMs often lack domain-specific knowledge and precise logical rigor [30]. Therefore, the effective integration for screening agents typically involves augmenting base LLMs through techniques like retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) with domain corpora or fine-tuning on collaborative task logs, thereby creating a hybrid architecture where the LLM's general capabilities are grounded by specialized tools and knowledge. This synergy transforms the screening agent into a dynamic, intelligent conductor of collaborative intelligence workflows, whose enhanced cognitive functions must then be operationalized through deliberate system architectures and interaction protocols, as will be discussed in the following subsection.

### 2.5 System Architectures and Interaction Protocols

Building upon the foundational cognitive capabilities enabled by LLMs, the operational efficacy of screening agents is realized through deliberate system architectures and well-defined interaction protocols. These elements constitute the structural and communicative backbone that enables screening agents to orchestrate workflows, manage knowledge flow, and enforce coordination policies within multi-agent collaborative intelligence services. A prevalent architectural paradigm is the heterarchical or fully distributed model, where agents operate without a central orchestrator, as seen in agent-based manufacturing systems [31]. In such architectures, screening agents often function as specialized nodes that facilitate peer-to-peer coordination by filtering task requests and validating agent capabilities before delegation. Conversely, more centralized or federated architectures may position the screening agent as a supervisory layer, managing a pool of subordinate agents. This is exemplified in complex customer service management systems, where a screening agent (or a module with screening functions) routes user intents to specialized dialogue agents based on semantic analysis of the query [6].

Coordination mechanisms are critical for maintaining system coherence and directly leverage the reasoning capacities discussed previously. The MAPE-K (Monitor-Analyze-Plan-Execute over a shared Knowledge base) loop is a prominent reference model for implementing adaptive coordination. Screening agents often embody the "Analyze" and "Plan" phases, continuously monitoring the system state, analyzing incoming data or task streams, and planning optimal task allocations or conflict resolutions. This closed-loop control is essential for dynamic environments. Communication protocols standardize these interactions. While early multi-agent systems relied on standards like FIPA ACL, newer frameworks leverage protocols tailored for LLM-based agents. For instance, the Agent-to-Agent Protocol aims to standardize communication, but challenges in cross-vendor service discovery persist [32]. Proposals like AgentDNS introduce a root naming system, analogous to DNS, which screening agents could query to discover and resolve trusted external agent services, thereby securely extending the collaborative boundary. In network management, architectures like FlexNGIA 2.0 propose LLM-based agents that autonomously design and deploy protocols, where a screening agent's role could involve validating and selecting proposed protocols from a pool of AI-generated candidates [33].

The interaction layer design must facilitate seamless collaboration by integrating mechanisms for trust and reputation, which are vital for reliable delegation. Screening agents can utilize trust propagation models to assess the reliability of collaborating agents before task assignment, a concept well-studied in social networks and group decision-making [34]. Furthermore, formal coordination mechanisms, modeled mathematically, ensure predictable interactions and verify system properties, which is crucial for critical applications [35]. In advanced systems like CommLLM for 6G, the architecture involves multiple specialized agents (e.g., retrieval, planning, evaluation), and a screening agent would be instrumental in routing tasks between these components based on the phase of the problem-solving workflow [28]. This architectural role is intrinsically linked to the subsequent need for robust data pipelines; the screening agent's routing and filtering decisions are only as sound as the integrated knowledge upon which they act. Ultimately, the synergy between a well-defined architecture, robust protocols, and intelligent coordination mechanisms empowers screening agents to be the linchpins of efficient, scalable, and trustworthy multi-agent collaborative intelligence services.

### 2.6 Data and Knowledge Integration Pipelines

The operational architectures and coordination protocols described in the previous section provide the structural framework for screening agents, but their decision-making efficacy is fundamentally dependent on robust data and knowledge integration pipelines. These pipelines are responsible for the continuous ingestion, processing, and fusion of heterogeneous information, transforming raw data into actionable, context-aware knowledge that fuels the screening agent's filtering, routing, and orchestration functions. A critical technological paradigm enabling this is Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), which dynamically augments the reasoning capabilities of agents, including screening agents, with relevant, external knowledge retrieved from structured or unstructured sources. For instance, in medical decision support, agentic RAG systems integrate evidence from clinical guidelines and research to supply context-aware support [9]. Similarly, in climate services, systems like HACID-CSR employ a GraphRAG approach, where a knowledge graph retrieval sequence feeds a subgraph of related climate entities into a recipe generation agent to create informed workflows [20]. This retrieval mechanism allows the screening agent to prioritize and filter information based on semantically enriched context rather than raw data alone.

However, prompting LLMs with retrieved knowledge alone may be insufficient for domain-specific accuracy. Knowledge-Augmented Fine-Tuning (KAFT) addresses this by finetuning the underlying models with domain-specific data alongside external knowledge, creating agents with deeper, internalized domain expertise. Empirical studies show that KAFT substantially surpasses simple prompting in both RAG and agent-based systems, particularly in improving factual accuracy in knowledge-intensive dialog scenarios like customer service [36]. For a screening agent, a KAFT-enhanced model means more reliable semantic understanding and filtering, reducing the propagation of errors to downstream specialized agents and enhancing the trustworthiness of its coordination decisions.

Beyond RAG and KAFT, the integration of heterogeneous data sources—spanning databases, knowledge graphs, ontologies, and real-time streams—poses a significant challenge. Effective pipelines require mechanisms for semantic alignment and knowledge fusion to create a consolidated knowledge landscape for agentic reasoning. Multi-agent systems often leverage ontologies to provide a common semantic framework, as seen in clinical decision support systems where specialist agents operate knowledge bases aligned with an ontological basis to enable cooperation [19]. In financial e-services, a dedicated method for automatic knowledge integration is crucial to synthesize heterogeneous agent knowledge into a unified decision [37]. Furthermore, architectures like CommLLM for 6G communications employ a multi-agent data retrieval (MDR) component, where specialized agents refine and summarize communication knowledge from a base, systematically expanding the knowledge boundaries for the system [28]. These integrated pipelines ensure that the screening agent operates on high-fidelity, contextually relevant knowledge, enabling precise task delegation, informed conflict resolution, and rigorous quality assurance across the collaborative ensemble. This foundation of integrated knowledge is a prerequisite for the screening agent to effectively fulfill its role, directly impacting the overall performance and reliability of the multi-agent service.

## 3 Functional Roles and Operational Mechanisms

### 3.1 Information Filtering and Semantic Prioritization

In multi-agent collaborative intelligence services, screening agents fulfill a foundational role as intelligent filters and semantic gatekeepers, serving as the critical first layer of information processing. Their primary function is to manage the deluge of information that characterizes modern data environments, sifting through heterogeneous, high-volume, and high-velocity data streams to identify, prioritize, and route the most relevant and high-fidelity information to downstream specialist agents. This process is critical for preventing cognitive overload within the agent collective, ensuring that subsequent analytical, decision-making, and execution tasks are performed on curated, high-signal inputs rather than raw, noisy data [1]. The operational necessity for such a role is acutely evident in domains like outbreak analytics, where synthesizing information from numerous sources in real-time is paramount for an effective response [3].

The core mechanism enabling this function is semantic understanding. Unlike simple keyword-based filters, screening agents leverage advanced natural language processing and knowledge representation technologies to comprehend the context, intent, and relationships within incoming data. By integrating with foundational large language models (LLMs) and structured knowledge bases, these agents can interpret the nuanced meaning of information, assess its relevance to the overarching collaborative goal, and assign a dynamic priority score [38]. For instance, in a financial risk management system, a screening agent might prioritize real-time news articles containing specific entity mentions and sentiment indicators related to a monitored portfolio over general market reports, thereby directing the attention of downstream analysis agents to the most pertinent threats or opportunities.

This semantic prioritization directly contributes to system efficiency and accuracy. By reducing the volume of irrelevant data that downstream agents must process, screening agents allow those specialized agents to dedicate computational resources to deeper analysis, leading to faster response times and more precise outputs [39]. Furthermore, in complex, integrated networks like Space-Air-Ground Integrated Networks (SAGIN), screening agents (often framed as semantic resource perceivers) parse network telemetry and user intents to filter and prioritize resource allocation requests based on semantic service requirements and quality-of-service constraints, a task beyond the capability of conventional rule-based systems [4].

Ultimately, this information filtering and semantic prioritization role transforms screening agents from passive conduits into active, intelligent curators. By establishing a foundation of high-quality, contextually relevant information, they enable the effective dynamic task delegation and workflow orchestration performed in subsequent stages. This preparatory function is essential for scalable, reliable, and effective multi-agent collaboration in data-intensive intelligence services.

### 3.2 Dynamic Task Delegation and Workflow Orchestration

Having established the screening agent's role as an intelligent filter and semantic gatekeeper, its function logically extends to **dynamic task delegation and workflow orchestration**. This capability transforms a multi-agent system from a collection of independent actors into a cohesive, intelligent unit capable of tackling complex, multi-faceted problems. Acting as a central conductor, the screening agent analyzes incoming high-level task requirements—often expressed in natural language—and dynamically decomposes them into a sequence of executable subtasks [1]. This decomposition is a context-aware process that considers the current state of the environment, available data, and the overarching goal.

Following decomposition, the screening agent assesses the capabilities and current workload of all specialized agents within the collaborative network. It matches each subtask to the agent best suited for its execution, considering factors such as functional specialization (e.g., data retrieval, code execution, analytical modeling), historical performance, and resource constraints [16]. The orchestration mechanism is fundamentally dynamic, allowing for real-time adjustments. For instance, if an agent assigned a critical subtask fails or becomes overloaded, the screening agent can re-route the task or decompose it further to distribute the load. This adaptive capability is crucial for maintaining system resilience and efficiency.

Advanced implementations leverage structured frameworks to formalize these interactions. The MAPE-K (Monitor-Analyze-Plan-Execute over a shared Knowledge base) control loop is a prominent paradigm where the screening agent, often acting as a planner or orchestrator, continuously monitors the system state, analyzes progress against objectives, and plans the next steps in the workflow [4]. In communication networks, LLM-based orchestrators interpret high-level operator intents and dynamically shape reward functions for reinforcement learning agents, effectively delegating optimization subtasks to achieve goals like energy reduction and latency minimization [4]. Furthermore, in complex domains like outbreak analytics, screening agents can orchestrate a pipeline of specialized LLM-based agents to perform tasks such as literature review, data extraction, coding, and report generation sequentially or in parallel [3].

The ultimate objective of this dynamic orchestration is to optimize overall execution time and resource utilization. By ensuring that the right agent handles the right task at the right time, screening agents prevent bottlenecks, balance computational loads, and maximize the collective intelligence of the system. This foundational orchestration role, which establishes the operational workflow and agent assignments, directly enables the subsequent critical function of the screening agent: mediating conflicts that may arise from the diverse objectives and actions of these now-delegated specialist agents.

### 3.3 Conflict Detection and Resolution

Building upon its role in dynamic orchestration, the screening agent also functions as a critical mediator to ensure system coherence by managing conflicts that arise from the inherent autonomy and diverse objectives of collaborating agents. This conflict management capability is essential for maintaining the reliability and trustworthiness of the collaborative service, as unaddressed conflicts can lead to contradictory actions, resource contention, or inconsistent outputs that undermine collective goals [1].

The screening agent proactively identifies conflicts through continuous monitoring and semantic analysis of agent states, intentions, and outputs. By maintaining a global view of the workflow, it can detect discrepancies between agents. For example, in a financial risk management system, one agent might advocate for a high-risk investment based on market trends, while another, focused on compliance, could flag it as a violation. The screening agent, synthesizing both perspectives, identifies this conflict in recommended actions. Advanced implementations employ knowledge representation tools, such as ontologies and shared context models, to understand the semantic roots of disagreements beyond superficial output mismatches [1].

Upon detection, the screening agent orchestrates resolution protocols. A primary strategy is to facilitate automated negotiation, guiding agents toward a compromise based on predefined utility functions or shared objectives. For instance, in autonomous network management, where LLM-based agents propose conflicting protocol configurations, a screening agent could orchestrate a negotiation round to synthesize a hybrid solution [33]. When negotiation is impractical or time-sensitive, the screening agent may enact priority-based arbitration, referring to a hierarchy of system goals or rules to make a binding decision. In public health analytics, a conflict between an agent prioritizing rapid intervention and another emphasizing thorough data validation could be resolved by arbitrating based on the epidemic phase's predefined priorities [3].

Moreover, screening agents contribute to conflict prevention by clarifying task specifications and aligning agent understandings from the outset. They can also adaptively learn from past conflicts, refining detection heuristics and resolution preferences to optimize long-term system harmony, as suggested in broader discussions on system optimization [4]. This proactive and adaptive mediation ensures that potential points of failure are transformed into opportunities for robust, consensus-driven outcomes.

## 5 Challenges, Ethical Considerations, and Future Directions

### 5.1 Technical and Operational Challenges

The deployment of screening agents within multi-agent collaborative intelligence services faces significant technical and operational hurdles that must be addressed to ensure robust, scalable, and trustworthy systems. A primary challenge is **scalability and system interoperability**. As collaborative networks grow to handle complex, real-world tasks, screening agents must orchestrate an increasing number of specialized agents and integrate diverse data sources. This demands efficient communication protocols and standardized interfaces to prevent bottlenecks. Frameworks like the one proposed for **Conversational AI Multi-Agent Interoperability, Universal Open APIs for Agentic Natural Language Multimodal Communications** highlight the critical need for universal APIs and discovery mechanisms to enable seamless interaction among heterogeneous agents [63]. Without such standards, system integration becomes arduous, limiting the agility and expansion of intelligence services.

**Security vulnerabilities** present another formidable obstacle, particularly as screening agents often act as gatekeepers and orchestrators. Their central role makes them attractive targets for attacks aimed at privilege escalation or data corruption. The inherent flexibility of LLM-based agents, where behavior is determined by runtime prompts, introduces non-deterministic security risks [64]. This paper identifies how malicious inputs can exploit an agent's reasoning to gain unauthorized access, underscoring the need for robust security architectures like Prompt Flow Integrity (PFI) to enforce agent isolation and guardrails. Furthermore, in applications like automated cybersecurity testing, agents themselves can struggle with complex environments and authentication barriers, revealing gaps in autonomous security operations [65].

**Algorithmic bias and reliability** issues are deeply concerning, as screening agents' filtering, prioritization, and delegation decisions can perpetuate or amplify biases present in their training data or underlying models. This can lead to unfair outcomes or the systematic overlooking of critical information. The challenge is compounded by the "black-box" nature of many deep learning components, making it difficult to audit and correct biased decision pathways. This lack of transparency directly connects to the ethical concerns of accountability and trust addressed in the following discussion. Relatedly, ensuring the **operational reliability and quality assurance** of these automated systems is difficult. Screening agents must monitor the performance of downstream agents, but their own adaptive learning mechanisms may not always converge to stable, optimal policies, especially in non-stationary environments.

Finally, the **management of computational costs** is a pressing operational concern. The advanced reasoning, continuous learning, and real-time coordination required of screening agents, especially those integrated with large foundation models, demand substantial processing power and memory. This creates significant financial and infrastructural barriers to widespread deployment and continuous operation. Optimizing the trade-off between performance and resource consumption remains an open research problem, crucial for making collaborative intelligence services economically viable at scale. Successfully navigating these technical challenges—interoperability, security, bias, reliability, and cost—is a prerequisite for the subsequent ethical and societal integration of these systems, as unresolved technical flaws can exacerbate the ethical dilemmas they introduce.

### 5.2 Ethical and Societal Considerations

The integration of screening agents into multi-agent collaborative intelligence services introduces profound ethical and societal dilemmas that extend directly from the technical challenges previously discussed, such as algorithmic bias and security vulnerabilities. These dilemmas must be rigorously addressed to ensure responsible deployment. A primary concern is the **amplification of privacy risks**. Screening agents, by their operational nature, ingest, filter, and prioritize vast streams of sensitive data. Without robust safeguards, this centralization of information processing creates significant vulnerabilities for data breaches and unauthorized surveillance. The need for clear data governance is underscored by research into user privacy management, which demonstrates that empowering users to understand data practices is crucial for informed consent [66]. Furthermore, the framework for authenticated delegation is critical, as it establishes protocols to restrict agent permissions and maintain clear chains of accountability, thereby protecting confidentiality [43].

**Accountability** represents a second major ethical challenge. In a complex multi-agent system where a screening agent orchestrates tasks and makes filtering decisions, attributing responsibility for erroneous or harmful outcomes becomes ambiguous. This "accountability gap" is exacerbated when agents operate with high autonomy. Governance frameworks must therefore define clear lines of responsibility, ensuring that human oversight remains integral. The "Human-in-Command" (HIC) model is proposed as a critical governance structure to maintain organizational accountability and ethical alignment, especially as systems shift toward greater autonomy [49]. Closely related is the issue of **transparency**, or the "black box" problem. The internal logic governing a screening agent's prioritization, filtering, and delegation decisions is often opaque, even to system designers. This lack of transparency can erode user trust and hinder the detection of algorithmic bias. Studies on conversational agents highlight that perceived opacity in decision-making processes directly hinders user trust, particularly in sensitive domains like healthcare [67]. Efforts to increase "visibility" into agent activities, through measures like activity logging and real-time monitoring, are essential for auditability and trust [44].

Finally, the pervasive deployment of screening agents risks **diminishing human agency and skills**. Over-reliance on automated filtering and decision-support could lead to automation complacency, where human operators uncritically accept agent recommendations, and the erosion of critical analytical expertise. The societal impact extends to labor dynamics and the concentration of power, where control over screening logic and data flows could become centralized within a few entities. Therefore, ethical design must prioritize augmenting human intelligence rather than replacing it, preserving human dignity and decisional ownership within collaborative frameworks [49]. Addressing these interconnected dilemmas—privacy, accountability, transparency, and human agency—is not merely a technical add-on but a foundational requirement for the sustainable and socially beneficial integration of screening agents. This ethical foundation directly informs the subsequent discussion on governance models and regulatory frameworks needed for practical implementation.

Looking ahead, regulatory frameworks must evolve in tandem with these technological and governance advancements to provide a stable foundation for future research and deployment. The integration of these governance mechanisms, standardized protocols, and adaptive regulations will be instrumental in fostering trust, ensuring safety, and unlocking the full collaborative potential of screening agent architectures, thereby setting the stage for the innovative research trajectories explored in the following section.

### 5.4 Future Research Trajectories

Building upon the established governance, standardization, and regulatory foundations, the evolution of screening agents in multi-agent collaborative intelligence services will be propelled by several critical research trajectories. A primary direction involves the deeper integration of hybrid neuro-symbolic reasoning architectures. While current systems leverage large language models for semantic understanding, they often lack robust, verifiable logical reasoning. Future screening agents must seamlessly combine the pattern recognition strengths of neural networks with the explicit, rule-based reasoning of symbolic AI. This fusion will enable agents to perform more reliable conflict resolution, explainable task delegation, and context-aware filtering, particularly in high-stakes domains like finance and healthcare where decisions require both data-driven insight and logical justification. Research into frameworks that allow screening agents to dynamically switch between or integrate these paradigms is essential.

Concurrently, to ensure that governance and performance standards are met, the development of robust, standardized evaluation benchmarks is a pressing need. Current assessments are often domain-specific or lack ecological validity. Future work must create comprehensive benchmarks that measure not only the functional performance of screening agents—such as delegation accuracy or filtering precision—but also system-level properties like resilience, scalability, and collaborative efficiency. Frameworks like Agent-Diff, which evaluates agentic LLMs on enterprise API tasks via code execution with state-diff-based evaluation [68], provide a valuable foundation. However, benchmarks must expand to assess the unique orchestrating and gatekeeping roles of screening agents within complex, multi-agent workflows, ensuring evaluations are both controlled and representative of real-world operational environments.

Furthermore, advancing beyond static protocols, the creation of self-evolving and self-optimizing agent systems represents a frontier. Inspired by visions of networks where AI agents autonomously design and adapt protocols [33], future screening agents should possess meta-cognitive abilities to analyze their own performance, reconfigure collaboration protocols, and learn novel optimization strategies without explicit human reprogramming. This requires advances in meta-reinforcement learning and the design of safe, constrained exploration mechanisms for autonomous adaptation. Related to this is research into improving cross-domain generalization and transfer learning, allowing screening agents trained in one domain (e.g., cybersecurity) to more efficiently adapt their orchestration policies to another (e.g., public health outbreak response) [3].

Finally, to ensure practical viability, interdisciplinary research must address the synergy between advanced AI capabilities and practical system constraints. This includes optimizing the trade-off between reasoning accuracy and the energy efficiency of distributed agentic networks [62], as well as developing lightweight yet powerful agent architectures suitable for deployment in resource-constrained or latency-sensitive environments. The integration of these trajectories will be crucial for realizing the full potential of screening agents as the intelligent, adaptive, and reliable core of next-generation collaborative intelligence services.
