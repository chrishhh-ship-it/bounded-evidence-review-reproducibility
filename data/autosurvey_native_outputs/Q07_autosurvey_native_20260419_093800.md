# Enhancing Report Quality through Reviewer Agents in Multi-Agent Collaborative Systems: A Comprehensive Survey

## 1 Introduction and Background

### 1.1 Defining Multi-Agent Collaborative Systems and Reviewer Agents

Multi-Agent Systems (MAS) constitute a foundational paradigm in artificial intelligence, built upon networks of autonomous, interacting software entities known as agents. These agents perceive their environment, reason about goals, and execute actions, often operating in complex domains where centralized control is inefficient. The collaborative strength of MAS arises from coordination and communication protocols that enable agents to work collectively, leveraging distributed knowledge and specialized capabilities [1]. This architecture provides significant advantages in adaptability, flexibility, and scalability, making it well-suited for intricate tasks such as automated customer service, where multiple intelligent modules must cooperate to manage complex interactions [2]. The evolution toward more autonomous, goal-directed systems has given rise to "Agentic AI," where agents exhibit proactive problem-solving, as seen in networking frameworks that aim to create ecosystems of collaborating autonomous AI agents [3].

Within such collaborative ecosystems, a critical functional specialization is the **reviewer agent**. These are autonomous agents dedicated to evaluating, refining, and ensuring the quality of outputs produced by other agents or the system overall. In the specific context of analytical report generation—a key output in high-stakes domains like healthcare, intelligence, and customer service—reviewer agents act as systematic quality assurance modules. They assess reports across critical dimensions such as factual accuracy, internal consistency, completeness, adherence to standards, and contextual relevance. This role is a direct instantiation of agentic system principles, where different agents assume specialized roles within a coordinated workflow. For example, in multi-agent decision support systems, one can conceptualize dedicated "evaluation" agents that scrutinize the analyses produced by "decision agents" [1].

The operationalization of reviewer agents is increasingly enabled by advanced foundation models. Generative foundation models, for instance, can be instantiated as interactive knowledge-base agents capable of overseeing the development and outputs of other agents, thereby incorporating an inherent review capacity to ensure task alignment and quality [3]. Moreover, the formalization of agent interactions and workflows—a hallmark of sophisticated MAS—allows for the structured integration of reviewer agents. By establishing clear communication protocols and role specializations, these systems institutionalize review steps, transforming ad-hoc checking into an automated, systematic process that enhances output consistency and reproducibility [4].

Thus, reviewer agents emerge not as an external addition but as an integral, specialized component within the multi-agent collaborative fabric. Their primary function is to uphold and enhance the integrity and utility of critical system outputs, directly addressing the multifaceted quality requirements—accuracy, consistency, completeness, and timeliness—that are so vital for operational efficacy, safety, and trust, as established in the following discussion.

### 1.2 The Imperative of Report Quality in Critical Domains

The quality of analytical reports, characterized by their accuracy, consistency, completeness, and timeliness, is a critical determinant of operational outcomes in high-stakes domains. As established in the preceding discussion of multi-agent systems, these systems are often deployed in complex environments where their collaborative outputs—such as reports—directly impact efficacy, safety, and trust. Substandard reports, marred by factual errors, inconsistencies, omissions, or delays, can precipitate severe consequences, including misdiagnoses, flawed strategic decisions, financial losses, and erosion of confidence. This underscores the foundational imperative for integrating specialized reviewer agents within collaborative systems to systematically enhance these quality dimensions.

The acute consequences of poor report quality are vividly illustrated in healthcare. Diagnostic inaccuracies or incomplete patient summaries directly compromise patient safety and treatment efficacy. The deployment of next-generation agentic AI systems aims to transform this landscape by delivering context-aware, patient-centric care with heightened precision [5]. Similarly, in medical decision support, the integration of evidence through agentic systems is crucial for generating reliable reports that inform clinical workflows [6]. Timeliness is equally vital, particularly during public health emergencies. As highlighted in outbreak analytics, generating real-time, evidence-based situational reports is essential for limiting pathogen spread, yet manual processes create significant bottlenecks [4]. Delays or errors in these reports can have profound public health implications.

This imperative extends to intelligence and security domains, where reports must synthesize complex, multi-source information to support critical decisions. Inconsistencies or gaps in intelligence assessments can lead to catastrophic policy failures. In the commercial sphere, report quality underpins organizational performance and user trust. AI agents enhance service efficiency and user experience by automating processes and ensuring accurate, consistent customer interactions [7]. The quality of service claims processing, especially in reaching rural populations, significantly impacts economic and market performance, where efficiency and security in reporting are paramount [8]. Furthermore, in broader operations management, the effective application of artificial intelligence and multi-agent systems is key to optimizing performance, a goal inherently tied to the quality of generated operational reports and analyses [9].

Collectively, these domains demonstrate that report quality is a multi-faceted requirement integral to system success. Accuracy ensures factual correctness; consistency enforces standardization for reliable interpretation; completeness guarantees the integration of all relevant contextual data; and timeliness enables dynamic responsiveness. The failure to meet any of these criteria can trigger a cascade of adverse outcomes. Therefore, the core motivation for deploying reviewer agents—as specialized components within the multi-agent fabric—is to institutionalize rigorous, automated mechanisms for evaluation and refinement. This transforms report generation from a potential vulnerability into a source of robust, decision-grade intelligence, directly setting the stage for the subsequent survey of their architectures and impacts.

## 2 Architectures and Mechanisms of Reviewer Agents

### 2.1 Foundational Architectures and Agent Typologies

Building upon the foundational principles of multi-agent collaborative systems, the architectural design and specific roles of the agents themselves are critical determinants of a reviewer agent's operational efficacy. The architectures of multi-agent systems (MAS) that underpin reviewer agents can be broadly categorized into hierarchical and heterarchical (or decentralized) paradigms, each offering distinct advantages for collaborative report generation and review. Hierarchical architectures organize agents in a top-down structure, where a central controller or manager agent delegates subtasks to specialized subordinate agents and synthesizes their outputs. This paradigm is particularly effective in domains requiring strict adherence to protocols and centralized oversight, such as clinical workflows where a master agent might orchestrate diagnostic reasoning and report generation [6]. Conversely, heterarchical architectures feature a more decentralized, peer-to-peer network of autonomous agents that negotiate and coordinate through mutual communication protocols. This design enhances flexibility and robustness, allowing the system to adapt dynamically to new information or agent failures, which is crucial in complex, evolving environments like automated customer service management [2].

The choice of architecture directly influences the emergence and orchestration of specialized reviewer agent typologies, each fulfilling a specific quality assurance function. **Evaluation Agents** are tasked with assessing the initial output against predefined metrics such as factual accuracy, logical coherence, and adherence to format. They often employ cross-referencing mechanisms against trusted knowledge sources to identify inconsistencies or hallucinations. **Validation Agents** take a more stringent role, verifying that the report's conclusions are supported by the integrated evidence and that the reasoning process aligns with domain-specific rules or guidelines. For instance, in a clinical multi-agent system, a validation agent might check diagnostic suggestions against established medical ontologies or patient data [12]. **Transparency Agents** constitute a critical typology focused on generating explanations for the system's outputs and decisions. They enhance trust and interpretability by elucidating the reasoning chain or highlighting the evidence used, which is essential for human-in-the-loop oversight in high-stakes domains [12]. Other typologies include **Integration Agents**, which synthesize inputs from multiple specialized agents (e.g., lab analysis and contextual reasoners) into a cohesive narrative, and **Refinement Agents**, which orchestrate iterative improvement loops based on feedback from other reviewer agents or human supervisors.

Ultimately, the selection and orchestration of these agent typologies within a chosen architectural paradigm fundamentally shape the review process. This structural and functional foundation is a prerequisite for the effective deployment of the advanced knowledge management mechanisms—such as those leveraging knowledge graphs and retrieval-augmented generation—that will be discussed next. These mechanisms provide the essential evidence base upon which the reviewer agents perform their critical validation, cross-referencing, and enrichment tasks.

### 2.2 Knowledge Integration and Management Mechanisms

The operational efficacy of a reviewer agent, as established in the preceding discussion of architectural paradigms and specialized agent typologies, is fundamentally contingent upon its capacity to access, integrate, and manage relevant, high-fidelity knowledge. This subsection analyzes the core knowledge management mechanisms that enable reviewer agents to ground their evaluations and refinements in verifiable evidence, ensuring reports are both accurate and contextually informed—a prerequisite for the subsequent communication and coordination processes.

A primary mechanism is the integration of structured knowledge representations, such as knowledge graphs and ontologies. These provide a semantic framework that allows agents to reason over relationships between entities, directly supporting the validation and cross-referencing functions of reviewer agents. For instance, in clinical decision support, multi-agent systems leverage medical ontologies to integrate heterogeneous knowledge bases from semiology, pharmacology, and clinical cases, enabling coherent and semantically grounded reasoning [13]. Similarly, the HACID-CSR system utilizes a domain-specific knowledge graph (CS-DKG) for climate services, which maps relationships between climate variables, hazards, and datasets, providing a curated knowledge backbone for workflow generation [14].

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is another pivotal mechanism, dynamically fetching relevant information from external corpora to augment an agent's contextual knowledge before generation or review. This is crucial for mitigating factual inaccuracies or "hallucinations," a core concern for evaluation agents. Research in dialog systems demonstrates that integrating RAG with agent architectures significantly improves factual accuracy by providing agents with real-time, domain-specific knowledge [15]. The agentic RAG paradigm extends this by orchestrating autonomous agents to coordinate complex workflows, such as retrieving and integrating evidence into clinical decision-making cycles [6]. Advanced implementations like GraphRAG further refine this by retrieving and reasoning over subgraphs from a larger knowledge structure, as seen in the recipe generation agent of HACID-CSR, which combines LLM reasoning with retrieved graph context [14].

Furthermore, specialized multi-agent data retrieval (MDR) systems are designed to manage knowledge acquisition at scale, preparing the high-quality information that coordination frameworks will later distribute. These systems employ dedicated agents to refine, summarize, and infer knowledge from vast bases, effectively expanding the knowledge boundaries of the core LLMs. The CommLLM system for 6G communications, for example, uses condensate and inference agents within its MDR component to process and summarize communication-specific knowledge, ensuring the subsequent planning and evaluation agents operate on high-quality, relevant information [16]. In financial e-services, effective knowledge integration across heterogeneous agent knowledge bases is identified as a critical challenge, necessitating automated methods to synthesize diverse information into a coherent basis for final decisions [17].

Ultimately, these integrated knowledge management mechanisms—spanning graphs, RAG, ontologies, and multi-agent retrieval—form the essential evidence base that empowers reviewer agents to perform their critical functions of evidence-based validation, cross-referencing, and contextual enrichment. This foundation directly enables the observable improvements in report quality, while also setting the stage for the sophisticated agent-to-agent communication and coordination required to operationalize this knowledge within a collaborative workflow.

### 2.3 Inter-Agent Communication and Coordination Frameworks

Building upon the foundational knowledge management mechanisms, effective communication and coordination are critical for enabling reviewer agents to function within multi-agent collaborative systems. These frameworks ensure that specialized agents, including those tasked with evaluation and refinement, can reliably exchange information, align their tasks, and collectively contribute to enhancing report quality. This subsection examines the key protocols and models that underpin this critical functionality, bridging the gap between isolated knowledge access and structured collaborative reasoning.

A primary enabler is the development of standardized APIs and agent-to-agent protocols, which provide a common language for interaction. Initiatives like the Open Voice Interoperability initiative propose Universal Open APIs based on Natural Language to establish interoperable interactions among diverse conversational AI agents [18]. Similarly, protocols such as the agent-to-agent protocol aim to standardize communication, though challenges in cross-vendor service discovery persist [19]. To address this, service discovery systems like AgentDNS introduce a structured mechanism for agents to autonomously discover, resolve, and securely invoke third-party services across organizational boundaries, drawing inspiration from traditional DNS principles [19]. This is complemented by frameworks for authenticated delegation, which extend established protocols like OAuth 2.0 to manage authorization and accountability for AI agents, ensuring secure and scoped interactions [20].

Beyond syntactic interoperability, reliable coordination requires robust mathematical and logical models. Mathematical modeling using coordination mechanisms is employed to validate and verify the accuracy of responses in cross-platform communications within Service-Oriented Architectures, addressing a critical need in complex, multi-participant systems [21]. For higher reliability in knowledge exchange, some systems move beyond prompt-based communication. The paradigm proposed in [22] utilizes a shared knowledge base mediated by formal reasoning tools like Answer Set Programming (ASP). This ensures communication consistency and security, as information is encapsulated and not directly exposed in unreliable prompts, a method demonstrated in collaborative service management systems.

These communication and coordination layers are seamlessly integrated into the advanced planning and reasoning workflows discussed in the following subsection. For instance, in systems designed for complex domains like 6G communications, multi-agent collaborative planning (MCP) components rely on the underlying coordination frameworks described here to allow multiple planning agents to generate solutions from different perspectives [16]. The coordination mechanisms ensure that the outputs from these planning agents, as well as data from retrieval agents, are effectively synthesized and passed to subsequent evaluation and refinement agents. This creates a seamless pipeline for producing high-quality, evidence-based reports, where the reviewer agent's role is fully embedded within a coherent collaborative process. Thus, the interplay of standardized interfaces, discovery services, formal models, and integrated workflows forms the operational backbone that allows reviewer agents to perform their quality assurance role effectively within a collaborative ecosystem.

### 2.4 Planning, Reasoning, and Collaborative Workflow Engines

The operational efficacy of reviewer agents within multi-agent collaborative systems is fundamentally enabled by sophisticated planning, reasoning, and collaborative workflow engines. These higher-order cognitive mechanisms translate the foundational communication and coordination protocols into actionable strategies, systematically decomposing complex review tasks, formulating coherent action plans, and orchestrating collaborative reasoning among specialized agents. This layer of intelligence is what allows reviewer agents to move beyond simple reactive checking and engage in structured, evidence-based evaluation.

A core architectural paradigm enabling this is multi-agent collaborative planning (MCP). In this approach, multiple planning agents generate and evaluate solutions from diverse perspectives, ensuring comprehensive coverage of a problem space [16]. This is exemplified in systems like CommLLM, where planning agents leverage retrieved domain knowledge to devise feasible solutions for communication tasks, ensuring plans are grounded in specialized information. The outputs of such collaborative planning form the primary material for subsequent review. Similarly, frameworks such as P1GPT implement a **structured reasoning pipeline** that systematically fuses multi-modal insights through coordinated agent communication, moving beyond simple role simulation to enforce a logical workflow for analysis [23]. This structured approach is critical for reviewer agents, as it ensures the material they assess originates from a transparent, reproducible, and defensible chain of logic.

These planning engines often rely on capability collaboration models, where the overall review-oriented task is decomposed into subtasks handled by agents with distinct, complementary skills. The CACA Agent framework operationalizes this by using an open, service-inspired architecture that integrates a set of collaborative capabilities, reducing dependence on a single LLM and enhancing the extensibility of both planning abilities and available tools [24]. This allows for the dynamic assembly of review teams where, for instance, one agent validates factual claims, another checks logical consistency, and a third assesses adherence to reporting standards. Furthermore, these planning and reasoning engines are deeply integrated with external tools, databases, and Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) to ground abstract plans in executable actions. In geoscientific applications, LLM-driven agents orchestrate the execution of specialized models (e.g., HEC-RAS for flood simulation) and retrieve data according to predefined operational workflows, synthesizing insights into coherent reports [25]. Similarly, in outbreak analytics, the formalization of agent interactions within a pipeline is highlighted as a key advantage for optimizing workflows, enhancing consistency, and enabling quality control through multiple review steps [4].

By providing a structured, tool-augmented, and logically sound foundation for report generation, these advanced planning and reasoning engines create the necessary preconditions for effective review. They ensure that the inputs to the reviewer agent are the product of a rigorous, multi-perspective, and evidence-based process. This sets the stage for the dedicated critical assessment and iterative refinement mechanisms, where the reviewer agent's role transitions from a passive recipient of information to an active arbiter of quality within a closed-loop system.

### 2.5 Evaluation, Reflection, and Iterative Refinement Loops

Building upon the foundational planning and reasoning engines that orchestrate collaborative workflows, the efficacy of a reviewer agent is concretely realized through its structured capacity for critical assessment and iterative improvement. This subsection delineates the specific architectural components and processes that enable reviewer agents to evaluate, reflect upon, and refine outputs, thereby directly enhancing the quality of final reports. These mechanisms often manifest as dedicated modules or specialized agents within a larger workflow, forming closed-loop systems for quality assurance that bridge the orchestrated planning described earlier with the human oversight detailed subsequently.

A foundational component is the multi-agent evaluation module, which operationalizes the collaborative planning into a formalized review process. Here, outputs from generating agents are systematically assessed against predefined criteria such as factual accuracy, logical coherence, and adherence to format. As illustrated in the CommLLM system for 6G communications, a dedicated evaluation agent is employed to assess proposed solutions generated by planning agents [16]. This modular separation of generation and evaluation roles ensures an objective review, preventing confirmation bias that might arise if a single agent performed both tasks. The evaluation can leverage cross-referencing against retrieved knowledge bases or involve multiple reviewer agents for consensus-building, a process that enhances consistency and reproducibility in outputs like outbreak analytics reports [4].

Beyond a single-pass assessment, sophisticated systems incorporate reflection and iterative refinement loops to enact the logical workflows planned by the system's reasoning engine. Here, the evaluation output is not merely a score but actionable feedback. A reflection agent analyzes the evaluation results to diagnose root causes of deficiencies—such as missing data, flawed reasoning, or ambiguous language—and generates specific improvement suggestions. Subsequently, a refinement agent, or the original generating agent, uses these suggestions to revise the output. The CommLLM system explicitly chains an evaluation agent with a reflection agent and a refinement agent to provide and implement improvement suggestions for communication task solutions [16]. This creates a multi-step iterative cycle where reports are progressively enhanced, embodying the structured reasoning pipelines critical for transparent and defensible analysis.

Furthermore, reinforcement learning (RL) and continuous learning paradigms provide a mechanism for embedding long-term improvement feedback cycles into reviewer agent operations, allowing the system to evolve beyond its initial configuration. Agents can learn from the outcomes of their review actions; for instance, a reviewer agent's policy for identifying errors can be optimized based on whether its flagged issues lead to measurable improvements in the final report's downstream utility. Research on intelligent learning techniques for multi-agent systems proposes improved cooperative learning methods where agents share strategies through continuous learning [26]. In a credit risk context, an Agentic AI framework employs continuous feedback learning cycles, allowing agents to adapt their decision-making and review criteria over time based on new data and outcomes [27]. This enables reviewer agents to evolve and improve their assessment heuristics autonomously, moving from static rule-based checking to adaptive, context-sensitive quality control. The outputs and confidence metrics from these automated review cycles then serve as critical inputs for the human oversight interfaces, ensuring that human reviewers are engaged with material that has already undergone rigorous, multi-layered automated refinement.

### 2.6 Human-Agent Interaction and Oversight Interfaces

While the automated evaluation and refinement cycles performed by reviewer agents constitute a powerful mechanism for quality enhancement, the integration of human judgment into the review process remains a critical architectural consideration for ensuring the reliability, ethical alignment, and ultimate acceptance of multi-agent systems. Reviewer agents, therefore, are often embedded within frameworks that feature dedicated Human-Agent Interaction (HAI) and oversight interfaces. These components are designed not merely for passive monitoring but for active, strategic collaboration, enabling a "Human-in-Command" (HIC) paradigm where human oversight is a systemic property of the interaction design [28].

A core architectural element facilitating this integration is the interface for strategic handoffs. This interface defines clear operational triggers—such as low-confidence assessments from the automated review cycle, detection of potential ethical dilemmas, or requests for domain-expert validation—that prompt the reviewer agent to cede control or seek input from a human operator. This ensures that critical decisions retain human decisional ownership, a principle emphasized in high-stakes domains like customer service and clinical support [28]. The handoff mechanism thus creates a seamless bridge between the automated refinement loops and human expertise.

To make these handoffs meaningful and to build user trust, explainability modules and transparency agents are integral components of the reviewer agent ecosystem. These components translate the often-opaque multi-agent reasoning and review processes into human-interpretable justifications. For instance, a transparency agent can be tasked with generating audit trails that document the contributions, validation steps, and refinement history of each specialized agent in the workflow [12]. This directly addresses user concerns about the opacity of AI decision-making, which is a known barrier to trust in conversational systems [29].

Furthermore, overarching governance frameworks provide the structural backbone for this oversight, establishing formal protocols for accountability, compliance, and ethical alignment. These frameworks operationalize concepts from regulations like the EU AI Act by embedding requirements for human oversight, data provenance tracking, and bias mitigation directly into the agent communication and review protocols [28]. Ultimately, these architectural components—strategic handoff interfaces, explainability tools, and governance layers—work in concert to create a robust human-in-the-loop system. This system leverages the scalability and consistency of automated reviewer agents while preserving essential human judgment and oversight in the final report refinement process, ensuring the output is not only high-quality but also trustworthy and accountable.

## 3 Observable Improvements in Report Quality

### 3.1 Enhancement of Accuracy and Factual Correctness

A primary and empirically demonstrable improvement conferred by reviewer agents in multi-agent collaborative systems is the significant enhancement of report accuracy and factual correctness. This is achieved through structured multi-agent validation and cross-referencing mechanisms that systematically reduce errors, including the notorious "hallucinations" prevalent in single large language model (LLM) applications. By formalizing review steps within a collaborative workflow, reviewer agents act as specialized validators that scrutinize the outputs of generation agents against authoritative knowledge sources and the reasoning of peer agents.

The core mechanism underpinning this improvement is the distributed cross-referencing capability. Reviewer agents can access different data modalities or knowledge repositories, enabling them to challenge assumptions, fill informational gaps, and flag contradictions that a single agent might overlook. For instance, in clinical diagnostics, an agentic retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) system can deploy a dedicated validation agent to cross-check a diagnostic report generated by a reasoning agent against integrated knowledge graphs and clinical guidelines [6]. This multi-layered verification process ensures that medical assertions are evidence-based, directly mitigating the risk of factual inaccuracies that could lead to detrimental patient outcomes. Empirical evidence supports this architectural advantage; a study implementing a multi-agent system for clinical decision support demonstrated superior prediction accuracy for patient mortality compared to a single-agent system, a gain attributable to the validation and integration functions performed by specialized reviewer agents [12].

The mitigation of hallucination risks is particularly critical in customer-facing and analytical domains where erroneous information can erode trust and lead to operational failures. A multi-agent architecture designed for customer service explicitly employs reviewer agents with fuzzy logic to assess and verify responses before they are communicated to users, thereby containing the propagation of incorrect information generated by primary LLM agents [30]. This creates a "safety net" that enhances the factual precision of final service reports. Similarly, in the high-stakes domain of outbreak analytics, the integration of specialized LLM-based agents into pipelines allows for multiple review steps before an analytical report is finalized. This formalized team interaction, where one agent's output becomes the input for a reviewing agent, provides more opportunities to catch inconsistencies and factual errors than direct prompting of a single LLM, leading to higher-quality, more reliable situational reports [4].

This collaborative scrutiny ensures that the final synthesized report is not merely a compilation of data but a vetted, coherent, and factually robust document, thereby substantially elevating the reliability of AI-generated content in critical applications. The resultant improvement in accuracy and error reduction thus forms a foundational pillar for the overall quality enhancement delivered by reviewer agents, complementing the gains in consistency and standardization detailed in the subsequent discussion.

### 3.2 Improvement of Consistency and Standardization

Building directly upon the foundational enhancement of accuracy and factual correctness, a primary and observable improvement facilitated by reviewer agents in multi-agent collaborative systems is the significant enhancement of report consistency and standardization. In high-stakes domains where reports must be uniform, reproducible, and adhere to strict protocols, the formalized workflows and structured communication enforced by reviewer agents are instrumental. This function addresses a key limitation of direct human or single-model generation, which can suffer from stylistic drift and terminological inconsistency. Reviewer agents operationalize predefined standards by acting as custodians of format and terminology, systematically evaluating intermediate and final outputs against established templates and knowledge schemas.

This function is critical in fields like public health and emergency management, where consistent reporting is foundational for coordinated action and longitudinal analysis. For instance, in outbreak analytics, the manual synthesis of diverse information sources often leads to bottlenecks and variability in report structure [4]. The integration of specialized LLM-based agents, including dedicated reviewer agents, formalizes team interactions to optimize workflows and enforce quality control, directly enhancing the consistency and reproducibility of situational reports [4].

The underlying mechanism for this improvement is twofold. First, reviewer agents often leverage or enforce standardized communication and service discovery protocols that govern inter-agent interactions. Frameworks like the proposed AgentDNS system aim to provide a root domain naming and service discovery layer, which, by creating a structured mechanism for service registration and semantic discovery, inherently promotes standardized data exchange formats and terminologies across heterogeneous agents [19]. This reduces ambiguities and ensures that information passed between a data retrieval agent, an analysis agent, and a reviewer agent maintains a consistent semantic structure. Second, reviewer agents are frequently architected to work within or enforce templated workflows and knowledge representations. In disaster management, for example, converting semi-structured Disaster Management Plans (DISPLANs) into a unified repository of knowledge units using agent-based analysis creates a standardized foundation for information retrieval and report generation [31]. A reviewer agent operating within such a system can validate that generated response reports correctly map to the formalized concepts and relationships within this repository, ensuring terminological precision and structural alignment with the official plan template.

The resultant benefits are profound. In service management, the application of AI Agents leads to improved service consistency by automating previously manual interaction processes [7]. A reviewer agent within such a service pipeline can ensure that customer interaction logs, incident reports, or performance summaries follow identical formatting rules and use approved classification codes, making them easily comparable and analyzable at scale. This standardization is equally vital in scientific and operational contexts. The Microsoft Academic Services (MAS) platform, which employs AI for large-scale scholarly analysis, relies on consistent extraction and assembly of factoids into a knowledge graph to ensure the reliability of its outputs [32]. A reviewer agent in a similar multi-agent research synthesis system would enforce consistency in how data points are extracted, coded, and reported, directly contributing to the reproducibility of the analytical findings.

Ultimately, by institutionalizing standards through automated review checkpoints, reviewer agents transform ad-hoc, variable reporting into a streamlined, uniform, and highly reliable process. This established consistency and standardization, in turn, provides the necessary structural foundation for reviewer agents to further augment the completeness and contextual depth of reports, as explored in the following subsection.

### 3.3 Augmentation of Completeness and Contextual Depth

Reviewer agents play a pivotal role in augmenting the completeness and contextual depth of reports generated by multi-agent systems, building directly upon the foundational consistency established by their standardization functions. This improvement is achieved primarily through two interconnected mechanisms: the systematic integration of multi-modal data sources and the intelligent orchestration of specialized knowledge agents. By ensuring that reports are not merely standardized summaries but are deeply informed by a rich tapestry of relevant information, reviewer agents elevate the utility and reliability of the final output, particularly in complex, dynamic domains.

A key function of reviewer agents is to oversee the fusion of diverse data types—text, structured databases, sensor data, and imagery—into a coherent narrative. This process mitigates the risk of reports being based on incomplete or siloed information, thereby enhancing completeness. For instance, in emergency medical settings, a comprehensive clinical decision requires integrating patient vitals, medical history, real-time symptoms, and pharmacological data. A reviewer agent within a multi-agent clinical decision support system can validate that all these data streams have been consulted and synthesized. The system described in [33] exemplifies this, where agents emulating a Triage Nurse, Emergency Physician, and Pharmacist contribute specialized perspectives. The reviewer agent (potentially embodied by the ED Coordinator role) ensures the final triage and treatment plan is comprehensive, checking for critical omissions in diagnosis, medication interactions, or resource needs, thereby producing a contextually deep report tailored to the specific emergency.

Furthermore, reviewer agents orchestrate the contributions of specialized knowledge agents to fill informational gaps and apply domain-specific reasoning, which directly enriches contextual depth. This is crucial in fields like agriculture, where effective planning depends on hyper-localized context. A reviewer agent can manage a workflow where one agent analyzes soil sensor data, another consults pest management ontologies, and a third retrieves market forecasts. By coordinating these agents, the reviewer ensures the final agricultural advisory report is complete, addressing not just immediate crop health but also long-term sustainability and economic factors. The importance of integrating such diverse, context-specific knowledge is highlighted in participatory agricultural approaches [34]. The knowledge base in a [35], built as a semantic network, provides the structured information that specialized agents can draw upon. The reviewer agent's role is to verify that the report has effectively tapped into this knowledge base and that the recommendations are coherent across all integrated domains, from precision farming techniques to climate adaptation strategies [36].

Ultimately, this orchestration leads to reports that are not only more complete but also possess greater contextual depth. They move beyond generic, standardized templates to reflect the nuanced realities of the specific situation, whether it's a unique patient presentation in the ED or a farm's particular microclimate and pest pressures. This capability for dynamic, context-aware composition is a core advantage of agent ensembles, as noted in research on policy composition [37]. By managing this process, reviewer agents are fundamental to transforming raw, multi-modal data into actionable, comprehensive, and deeply contextualized intelligence. This enhancement in depth and completeness, in turn, sets the stage for the subsequent subsection's focus on how reviewer agents further contribute to the efficiency and scalability of the entire report generation workflow.

### 3.4 Gains in Generation Efficiency and Scalability

The integration of reviewer agents within multi-agent collaborative systems demonstrably enhances the efficiency and scalability of report generation processes, a critical progression from their role in ensuring depth and completeness. A primary mechanism for this improvement is the automation of manual review tasks. In traditional workflows, human experts are required to meticulously check reports for errors, consistency, and completeness, which constitutes a significant bottleneck. Reviewer agents automate these evaluative functions, performing continuous validation and cross-referencing at computational speeds. This automation directly reduces the operational workload on human personnel. Empirical evidence from service innovation contexts indicates that AI agents can reduce operational workload by up to 40% and accelerate service response time by up to 60% [7]. In the domain of incident management, the automation of root cause analysis (RCA)—a critical and time-intensive component of incident reports—through LLM-based agents presents significant time savings for engineers [38]. By dynamically collecting diagnostic information via retrieval tools, these agents perform RCA without constant human intervention, thereby streamlining the overall incident reporting pipeline.

Furthermore, the multi-agent architecture inherently enables parallel processing and optimized workflows, which are key to scalability. Unlike linear, human-dependent processes, a collaborative system can deploy specialized agents—including dedicated reviewers—to operate concurrently on different sub-tasks of report generation. For instance, while one agent drafts a section, a reviewer agent can simultaneously validate the factual claims of a previously completed section, and another agent can ensure formatting consistency. This parallelization drastically cuts down the total generation time. The formalized interactions between LLM-based agents are noted to optimize workflows, enhance consistency, and allow multiple tasks to be performed simultaneously and at scale [4]. This capability is crucial in high-pressure domains like outbreak analytics, where rapid situational updates are required. Similarly, in operational environments, the use of multi-agent systems for optimization and load balancing demonstrates how decentralized coordination can improve resource utilization and reduce service execution delays, principles that directly translate to more efficient report generation workflows [39].

The scalability gains are also evident in the ability of reviewer agents to manage increasing volumes and complexity of data without a proportional increase in human labor. As systems grow, the reviewer agent's role in orchestrating knowledge integration from diverse sources—such as logs, metrics, and databases—ensures that the report generation process does not degrade in speed or quality [38]. This is complemented by the learning and memory capabilities of advanced agents, which allow them to become more effective over time, further optimizing the workflow for subsequent reporting cycles [4]. Consequently, organizations can scale their reporting operations to meet fluctuating demands, such as during major incidents or periodic financial reporting cycles, while maintaining high quality and timeliness [10]. This foundation of enhanced efficiency and scalability is essential for supporting the next evolutionary step: enabling the dynamic adaptability and real-time responsiveness that reviewer agents bring to the report generation lifecycle.

### 3.5 Enhancement of Adaptability and Dynamic Responsiveness

Building upon the efficiency gains outlined previously, the integration of reviewer agents imbued with learning capabilities and real-time data integration fundamentally enhances the adaptability and dynamic responsiveness of report generation systems. This evolution allows for outputs that are not static documents but living artifacts, continuously updated and tailored to rapidly evolving operational contexts. In critical domains where situational awareness is paramount, this capability transforms reports from retrospective summaries into proactive decision-support tools. Reviewer agents achieve this by orchestrating continuous data streams, applying context-aware reasoning, and initiating iterative refinement cycles autonomously.

The value of this dynamic responsiveness is evident in domains like network management and disaster response. Here, reviewer agents integrate live sensor data, model outputs, and situational reports to maintain report currency. For instance, in natural hazard assessment, LLM-driven agents synthesize real-time data from hydrodynamic models, geospatial datasets, and policy documents [25]. A reviewer agent in such a system can continuously validate new model runs against incoming sensor data, update risk projections in an ongoing situational report, and highlight discrepancies requiring expert attention, creating a responsive reporting loop crucial for emergency command centers. Similarly, the agent-based conversion of static Disaster Management Plans (DISPLANs) into active knowledge repositories allows reviewer agents to dynamically map real-time incident data to relevant plan clauses and generate tailored action reports [31]. This moves beyond static document retrieval to context-aware report generation that adapts to the specific phase and scale of an unfolding disaster.

This need for real-time, adaptive reporting is equally acute in financial services due to volatile markets and evolving risk profiles. Reviewer agents within agentic AI frameworks enable autonomous and real-time credit risk decision-making by continuously absorbing new transaction data, market news, and borrower information [27]. A reviewer agent can monitor this pipeline, dynamically updating a borrower's risk assessment report to reflect new liabilities or market shocks, thereby providing lenders with a constantly current view rather than a periodic snapshot. This concept extends to investment management, where LLM-based agents must adapt reports to swiftly changing financial contexts [10]. A reviewer agent could orchestrate specialized agents analyzing news sentiment and time-series data, ensuring the final investment report synthesizes the latest information and revises prior conclusions based on new evidence.

The underlying mechanism enabling this high degree of adaptability often involves advanced policy composition and agent ensembles. Reviewer agents can request or assemble context-aware policies on-demand, allowing the report generation system to switch seamlessly between different operational modes—such as shifting from routine monitoring to crisis response—without lengthy retraining [37]. Furthermore, by incorporating feedback loops and learning from historical interactions and corrections, reviewer agents improve their future review heuristics. This learning capability makes the overall system more adept at handling novel scenarios and reduces the need for manual intervention over time, culminating in systems that are not only reactive to real-time data but also proactively adaptive to new patterns. This progression towards intelligent, self-optimizing workflows sets the stage for further exploration of how reviewer agents contribute to the overarching quality and reliability of the final report.

## 4 Challenges and Ethical Considerations

### 4.1 Security Vulnerabilities and System Integrity Risks

Building upon the foundational role of reviewer agents in enhancing report quality, their deployment within multi-agent collaborative systems also introduces a complex attack surface, raising significant security vulnerabilities and system integrity risks. These risks stem from the autonomous, interconnected nature of agents and their reliance on dynamic, often natural language-driven, workflows. A primary concern is the threat of adversarial attacks, where malicious inputs are crafted to manipulate an agent's reasoning or output. This is particularly acute for systems where reviewer agents assess and refine reports based on LLM-generated content. For instance, research on automated web vulnerability reproduction highlights that LLM agents exhibit high sensitivity to input guidance, with performance degrading significantly under incomplete or manipulated information [40]. This susceptibility suggests that reviewer agents could be similarly misled by poisoned data or adversarial prompts, leading to the validation of erroneous or malicious content within a final report, thereby compromising its factual correctness and overall quality.

Furthermore, the multi-agent architecture itself creates vectors for unauthorized access and privilege escalation. The flexible, prompt-driven behavior of LLM agents introduces non-deterministic execution paths that can be exploited. As noted in security analyses, an attacker could potentially hijack the communication flow between agents to escalate privileges and gain unauthorized access to sensitive data or system functions [41]. This risk is amplified in systems handling confidential information, such as financial or healthcare reports. The need for authenticated delegation is critical, as unauthorized agent actions can lead to data breaches. Frameworks for authenticated and authorized AI agents emphasize that without clear chains of accountability and robust access control, agents acting on behalf of users could perform inappropriate actions, accessing or exfiltrating sensitive data contained within reports [20].

Data breaches also pose a substantial risk, arising from insecure inter-agent communication and data storage. Standardized communication frameworks aim to enhance interoperability but must be designed with security as a core principle. Insecure APIs or protocols can become points of data leakage. The integration of multiple specialized agents, each potentially accessing different data sources (e.g., knowledge bases, real-time feeds), increases the number of endpoints that must be secured. A compromise in any single agent or communication channel within the collaborative chain—from data retrieval to final review—can jeopardize the confidentiality and integrity of the entire report generation process. Therefore, ensuring system integrity requires a holistic security approach encompassing adversarial robustness, strict access control, and secure communication protocols to protect the multi-stage workflow that reviewer agents oversee. These security and integrity challenges are intrinsically linked to the subsequent issue of system transparency and trust; if a reviewer agent's operations or the data it processes are compromised, the resulting opacity and potential for erroneous outputs fundamentally undermine user confidence and the reliability of the final report.

### 4.2 Transparency, Explainability, and Trust Deficits

The deployment of reviewer agents within multi-agent collaborative systems introduces significant challenges concerning transparency, explainability, and the consequent erosion of user trust, a concern that emerges directly from the preceding security and integrity risks. If a reviewer agent's operations or the data it processes are compromised, the resulting opacity and potential for erroneous outputs fundamentally undermine user confidence and the reliability of the final report. A core issue stems from the inherent opacity of the complex decision-making processes employed by these AI agents, particularly when built upon large language models (LLMs) or intricate multi-agent reasoning pipelines. The "black-box" nature of such systems makes it difficult for human users, including domain experts and end-users, to understand *how* a reviewer agent arrived at a specific critique, validation, or refinement suggestion. This lack of interpretability directly impedes trust, as stakeholders cannot verify the reasoning chain or identify potential flaws in the agent's logic. As highlighted in research on conversational systems, users express a clear need for a clearer understanding of how LLMs process information and make decisions, with perceived opacity hindering trust, especially in sensitive applications [29].

This trust deficit is critically amplified in high-stakes domains like healthcare, where reviewer agents are tasked with validating clinical reports or diagnostic suggestions. Without transparent and explainable operations, healthcare professionals may be reluctant to rely on agent-generated feedback, potentially negating the system's intended benefits [6]. The challenge is further compounded in multi-agent settings where a reviewer agent's output is the result of interactions with other specialized agents (e.g., data retrieval agents, analysis agents). Tracing responsibility and understanding the collaborative workflow becomes exponentially more difficult. While frameworks like Multi-Agent-Based Decision Support Systems (MADSS) coordinate various agent types, the complexity of their interactions often lacks built-in mechanisms for comprehensive explanation generation [1]. This opacity can lead to a cascade of negative effects. In conversational customer service, for instance, a lack of transparency in how a reviewer agent assesses and improves response quality can frustrate users and damage the perceived reliability of the service [42]. Furthermore, in contexts requiring rigorous audit trails, such as intelligence analysis or outbreak analytics, the inability to explain a reviewer agent's modifications to a report compromises accountability and reproducibility [4].

Addressing these deficits requires the development of integrated explainability modules and transparency agents within the system architecture, which can articulate the "why" behind agent decisions in a human-comprehensible manner, thereby rebuilding the essential foundation of trust for effective human-agent collaboration. This foundational issue of transparency and trust is intrinsically linked to the subsequent and parallel risks of algorithmic bias and fairness; without clear insight into the reviewer agent's decision-making process, it becomes impossible to audit for and correct systematic biases that may be propagated into the final report.

### 4.3 Algorithmic Bias and Fairness Concerns

Building upon the challenges of transparency and trust, the integration of reviewer agents into multi-agent collaborative systems introduces profound and parallel risks related to algorithmic bias and fairness. These risks, if unmitigated, can systematically propagate through the review process and become embedded within final reports, leading to discriminatory outcomes and undermining the system's integrity. The origins of such biases are multifaceted, often stemming from skewed training data and the inherent architectural design of the underlying agents. For instance, if the large language models (LLMs) powering these agents are trained on historical datasets containing societal prejudices or non-representative samples, the agents may inadvertently learn and replicate these patterns. In a multi-agent review process, a reviewer agent tasked with validation might fail to correct, or even actively reinforce, biased assertions generated by a primary reporting agent, thereby institutionalizing unfairness in the finalized document. This is particularly perilous in high-stakes domains where reports directly influence critical decisions, mirroring the trust concerns raised in sensitive applications like healthcare.

The manifestation of these biases is evident across diverse application domains. In financial contexts, biased reviewer agents could produce investment or credit reports that systematically disadvantage certain demographic groups. Research on [10] highlights the transformative potential of LLMs in finance but also implicitly underscores the need to scrutinize the data and methodologies behind these models to prevent perpetuating existing inequities. Similarly, in operational settings like inventory management, an agentic system might generate reports reflecting suboptimal or biased ordering decisions. The study [43] provides direct empirical evidence, revealing that LLM agents exhibit human-like cognitive biases, such as the pull-to-centre effect, in inventory replenishment scenarios. A reviewer agent lacking mechanisms to detect and mitigate these biases would allow flawed reasoning to be presented as an authoritative final report, potentially exacerbating supply chain inefficiencies.

The healthcare domain presents perhaps the most critical context for such biases, directly linking to the preceding discussion on high-stakes applications. Reviewer agents in clinical support systems that assess diagnostic or treatment reports risk amplifying biases present in non-representative medical training data. This could lead to reports with varying accuracy or recommendations across different patient populations, directly impacting care quality and equity. The framework proposed in [44] categorizes applications and identifies fundamental limitations like lack of real-world understanding, which can serve as a conduit for bias. If a reviewer agent operates without sufficient oversight (Category 1 in the framework), biased outputs may reach patients or providers unchecked. Furthermore, the survey on [6] discusses architectural patterns and the imperative for clinical validation, which must include rigorous bias auditing to ensure fairness.

Addressing these concerns requires a multi-faceted approach that parallels the need for transparency solutions. It involves curating diverse and representative training datasets, implementing dedicated bias detection and mitigation modules within the reviewer agent's evaluation loop, and ensuring structured human oversight, especially for reports impacting marginalized groups. Without such safeguards, the deployment of reviewer agents risks automating and scaling discrimination under a veneer of objective, systematic review—a failure that not only compromises report quality but also erodes ethical foundations. This focus on bias and fairness naturally leads to a consideration of the closely intertwined challenges of data privacy and confidentiality, as the sensitive data required for agent operation and refinement itself can be a source of bias and must be protected.

### 4.4 Data Privacy and Confidentiality Risks

The deployment of reviewer agents within multi-agent collaborative systems inherently amplifies data privacy and confidentiality risks, a critical concern that follows directly from the preceding discussion on algorithmic bias, as the sensitive data required for agent operation can itself be a source of bias and must be rigorously protected. These risks permeate the entire data lifecycle—from initial collection and processing to inter-agent sharing and final output generation. In high-stakes domains like healthcare, where reviewer agents are employed for tasks such as diagnostic support and report generation [6], the systems must handle protected health information (PHI). The autonomous coordination and evidence integration performed by these agents create multiple points of vulnerability where PHI could be exposed through insecure communication channels, unauthorized access by a compromised agent, or improper logging within the collaborative workflow. The survey on agentic RAG systems further highlights that deploying such technology in resource-limited settings complicates ensuring robust data protection [6].

Beyond healthcare, in customer service and retail contexts, the interaction between AI agents and consumers generates vast amounts of personal data. Research indicates that while consumers may express lesser immediate privacy concerns with AI agents compared to human agents [45], this perceived lower risk does not eliminate the actual technical vulnerabilities. The very architecture enabling multi-agent collaboration—featuring service discovery, inter-agent communication protocols, and shared knowledge bases—can become a vector for data breaches if not meticulously secured. The data practices of these collaborative systems, often opaque to users, necessitate transparent management. This is underscored by work on LLM-based agents designed to parse privacy policies, which demonstrates the complexity of data usage even in systems built to enhance user privacy awareness [46]. The agent's own need to process policy text and user queries introduces another layer of data processing that must be safeguarded.

Furthermore, the fundamental mechanisms that empower reviewer agents, such as retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) and continuous learning from feedback loops, pose significant confidentiality challenges. A RAG system's external knowledge retrieval could inadvertently leak query context or internal data. Feedback cycles used for refinement may store sensitive corrections or user interactions. Studies on conversational agents reveal that user trust is heavily influenced by perceptions of data security and transparency in how information is processed [29]. The opacity of multi-agent decision-making complicates providing users with clear explanations of data flows, potentially violating principles of data minimization and purpose limitation. Moreover, as noted in broader analyses of AI systems, the large-scale data collection required for training and operating these agents carries inherent risks of misuse, re-identification, and profiling [47].

Therefore, mitigating these risks requires a holistic approach combining technical safeguards like federated learning or homomorphic encryption, rigorous access controls within the agent network, and ethical governance frameworks that enforce privacy-by-design principles. Without such measures, privacy breaches not only violate ethical and legal standards but also directly undermine the integrity and trustworthiness of the final report. This focus on securing data flows and agent interactions logically precedes an examination of the technical coordination failures that can arise from these very architectural complexities, as fragile communication and unreliable environment adaptation—topics addressed in the following section—can exacerbate data exposure and compromise system-wide confidentiality.

### 4.5 Coordination Failures and Operational Inconsistencies

Building upon the data privacy and confidentiality challenges outlined in the previous section, the technical architecture of multi-agent systems introduces another layer of risk through coordination failures and operational inconsistencies. These limitations can severely degrade system performance and directly undermine the reliability of the final reports generated. The issues stem from the inherent complexities in orchestrating autonomous agents, which must reliably communicate, align their tasks, and adapt to dynamic environments.

A primary challenge lies in the reliability of inter-agent communication protocols. While frameworks like the agent-to-agent protocol aim to standardize interactions [19], practical implementation in complex, real-world scenarios can be fragile. Breakdowns in message delivery, misinterpretation of shared context, or latency in synchronization can lead to agents operating on outdated or conflicting information, directly introducing errors and inconsistencies into the collaborative report generation process. This is corroborated by research in service-oriented architectures, where ensuring accurate cross-platform communication is fundamental, and mathematical modeling of coordination mechanisms is required to validate system interactions [21].

Furthermore, the challenge of environment adaptation poses a significant risk to operational consistency. Reviewer agents depend on a stable and correctly configured operational environment to access tools, data, and other agents. However, as highlighted in studies on automated vulnerability reproduction, agents can fail dramatically when faced with complex, multi-component environments requiring specific configurations or authentication [40]. An agent tasked with validating data within a report may thus produce incorrect assessments if it cannot properly interface with necessary external resources, leading to a false sense of security in the report's conclusions.

Coordination failures also manifest in task decomposition and workflow alignment. In systems employing multi-agent collaborative planning [16], a misalignment in sub-task dependencies or a failure in handoff procedures between specialized agents can result in incomplete analyses or the unchecked propagation of errors through the pipeline. Without robust fault-tolerance mechanisms, such a failure can corrupt the entire report generation workflow.

Ultimately, these technical coordination shortcomings directly translate to observable deficits in report quality, including internal contradictions, missing sections due to dropped tasks, or conclusions based on a partial and unintegrated view of the evidence. As the following section will elaborate, these operational failures not only impact report quality but also exacerbate profound ethical governance and accountability gaps, as the opacity and fragility of agent coordination complicate the attribution of responsibility for flawed outputs. Ensuring reliable coordination is therefore a foundational prerequisite for both trustworthy automated report generation and effective systemic governance.

### 4.6 Ethical Governance and Accountability Gaps

The profound technical coordination failures and operational inconsistencies detailed in the previous section create a fertile ground for significant ethical governance and accountability gaps, which current organizational and legal frameworks are ill-equipped to handle. A primary and direct consequence of these opaque and fragile technical processes is the ambiguity surrounding liability. When a reviewer agent, or the collective system it operates within, produces a flawed report leading to adverse outcomes, the distributed and often inscrutable nature of decision-making across multiple autonomous agents complicates the attribution of responsibility. This creates a "responsibility gap" where neither the developers, the deploying organization, nor the AI agents themselves can be clearly held accountable.

Addressing this gap is exacerbated by the lack of robust, standardized governance frameworks specifically designed for such agentic systems. As highlighted in the context of customer service, a sustainable model requires a transition toward frameworks like "Human-in-Command" (HIC), which aim to preserve human dignity and decisional ownership by mandating meaningful human oversight as a systemic property, not merely a technical feature [28]. The HIC framework underscores that effective governance must integrate interaction design, cognitive load management, and psychological trust to maintain organizational accountability and ethical alignment.

Furthermore, achieving this mandated oversight is critically contingent on sufficient transparency into agent operations—a requirement that technical coordination failures directly obstruct. The current lack of "visibility" — information about where, why, how, and by whom AI agents are used — represents a core governance deficit [48]. Without technical measures such as agent identifiers, real-time monitoring, and comprehensive activity logging, it becomes impossible to audit reviewer agents' decisions, trace the root causes of coordination failures, or detect emergent biases. This opacity, stemming from the system's architectural complexity, directly undermines the trust and capability necessary for human reviewers to effectively exercise their oversight role.

The severity of these governance challenges is particularly acute in high-stakes domains. In healthcare, for instance, next-generation agentic AI promises transformative benefits but also introduces significant ethical and regulatory challenges that demand interdisciplinary collaboration to address [5]. Similarly, in outbreak analytics, while LLM-based agents offer efficiency gains, the development of necessary governance structures and oversight is explicitly noted as a prerequisite to prevent misuse and unintended consequences [4]. These examples underscore that the technical risks of coordination and the ethical risks of governance are inextricably linked.

Collectively, these gaps point to an urgent need for the co-development of technical visibility measures and ethical governance protocols. Such an integrated approach must clearly define liability, enforce human oversight triggers, and ensure the entire multi-agent system remains aligned with human values and societal norms. As the following section will argue, failure to close these governance and accountability gaps does not merely pose an organizational risk; it amplifies profound societal and psychological impacts, as opaque and unaccountable systems erode trust and destabilize the informational foundations of collective decision-making.

### 4.7 Societal and Psychological Impacts

Building upon the profound ethical governance and accountability gaps outlined previously, the deployment of reviewer agents within multi-agent collaborative systems also poses significant societal and psychological risks that demand careful scrutiny. These risks are deeply intertwined with the governance deficits concerning opacity and oversight, extending their impact beyond organizational boundaries into the broader social fabric. A primary concern is the potential for these agentic systems to exacerbate epistemic instability, particularly among vulnerable populations. As highlighted in [49], AI agents designed to maximize engagement and affirmation may inadvertently mirror, validate, or amplify delusional or grandiose content. In the context of reviewer agents tasked with evaluating and refining reports, this risk translates into a dangerous feedback loop where flawed or biased informational outputs are systematically reinforced rather than corrected. This process blurs the boundaries between reliable and unreliable information, undermining collective trust in information systems and eroding the shared factual foundations necessary for societal decision-making.

Furthermore, the increasing delegation of complex evaluative tasks to autonomous agents raises critical questions about human agency and decision-making autonomy. The pervasive integration of reviewer agents could lead to automation complacency, where human operators over-rely on automated assessments, diminishing their critical oversight and analytical skills. This deskilling effect is particularly perilous in high-stakes domains where final accountability remains human, directly linking back to the "responsibility gap" discussed earlier. The paper [48] underscores that increased delegation without sufficient transparency may exacerbate societal risks, emphasizing the need for governance structures that preserve meaningful human oversight. Moreover, the interaction patterns enforced by multi-agent systems could subtly shape human behavior and organizational processes, potentially privileging efficiency and standardization over nuanced judgment and ethical deliberation. For instance, in financial services, while agentic systems promise enhanced decision-making [27], they also risk creating opaque decision pathways that users feel powerless to question or override, thus negatively influencing human autonomy.

The psychological impact extends to workplace dynamics and trust within professional ecosystems. As Multi-Agent Based Decision Support Systems (MADSS) become more prevalent, the traditional roles of human experts may be reconfigured, potentially leading to job displacement anxieties or a devaluation of human expertise. The dynamics of trust and opinion propagation, as discussed in [50], become crucial in this context; if the "decisions" of reviewer agents are not transparent or are perceived as biased—issues stemming from the visibility deficits noted previously—it can fracture trust within teams and organizations. Ultimately, mitigating these societal and psychological impacts requires a sociotechnical approach that prioritizes human-in-command paradigms, invests in digital literacy, and designs systems that augment rather than replace human cognitive and social capacities, thereby addressing the interconnected challenges of governance, accountability, and societal well-being.

## 5 Future Directions and Conclusion

### 5.1 Standardized Validation and Benchmarking Methodologies

A critical frontier for advancing reviewer agents in multi-agent collaborative systems is the development of robust, standardized validation and benchmarking methodologies. Currently, the field lacks comprehensive, domain-specific frameworks capable of holistically assessing the nuanced dimensions of report quality that reviewer agents are designed to enhance—such as factual consistency, reasoning transparency, and adversarial robustness. To build trustworthy systems, future research must prioritize creating multi-faceted benchmarks that move beyond simple accuracy metrics to evaluate the entire collaborative process and its outputs. This need is particularly urgent in high-stakes domains. For example, in outbreak analytics, while integrating specialized LLM-based agents promises efficiency gains, rigorous validation against established benchmarks is essential to ensure reliability and prevent erroneous conclusions [4]. Similarly, in clinical decision support, multi-agent systems show improved prediction accuracy, yet consistent and transparent evaluation of their collaborative reasoning processes remains a key challenge [12].

Effective benchmarks must be multi-dimensional, assessing not only the final report's factual correctness but also the traceability and explainability of the inter-agent review process. Frameworks like Agent-Diff, which evaluate agentic LLMs on real-world API tasks via code execution with a state-diff-based contract, offer a promising direction by separating process from outcome and providing a controlled, sandboxed environment for evaluation [51]. This paradigm can be extended and specialized for report-generation systems. Specifically, validation frameworks should incorporate stress tests for adversarial robustness, probing how reviewer agent coordination withstands attempts to inject misinformation or manipulate communication channels—a concern highlighted in discussions of security vulnerabilities in multi-agent networks [52]. Additionally, benchmarks should measure consistency and standardization enforcement, quantifying how effectively reviewer agents apply formalized workflows, such as those defined by Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs), to produce uniform outputs across different instances and operators [53].

The establishment of such standardized methodologies is foundational for building trust, facilitating regulatory compliance, and enabling fair comparisons between different architectural paradigms. It provides the empirical grounding necessary to transition from promising prototypes to reliable, deployable systems. Crucially, these benchmarks create the measurable evidence needed to demonstrate how reviewer agents enhance report quality through concrete improvements in accuracy, transparency, and resilience. This rigorous evaluation framework naturally sets the stage for the subsequent integration of human oversight, as quantified system performance and identified limitations inform the design of strategic handoff protocols and human-in-command interfaces discussed in the following section.

### 5.2 Advanced Human-Agent Collaboration Paradigms

Building upon the need for rigorous validation, the demonstrated performance and identified limitations of reviewer agents under standardized benchmarks provide a critical foundation for their effective integration with human oversight. The evolution of reviewer agents within multi-agent collaborative systems therefore necessitates a parallel advancement in human-agent collaboration paradigms. Moving beyond simplistic human-in-the-loop models, future research must focus on developing sophisticated "Human-in-Command" (HIC) frameworks that strategically integrate human oversight to preserve accountability and enhance system performance [28]. These frameworks are not about constant human monitoring but about designing intelligent systems that recognize their own limitations and proactively solicit human judgment at critical junctures, informed by the performance boundaries quantified through benchmarking.

A core research direction involves the formalization of strategic handoff protocols. These are dynamic mechanisms that determine when and how control should be transferred between autonomous agents and human reviewers. Triggers for handoffs must be context-aware, moving beyond simple confidence thresholds to include factors such as novelty detection, ethical ambiguity, potential for high-impact errors (as identified in stress tests), or requests for human expertise as seen in collaborative cognitive architectures [54]. Developing standardized, interpretable signaling for these handoffs is crucial for seamless integration into collaborative workflows and ensures human intervention is both necessary and efficient.

Furthermore, managing the cognitive load on human overseers is paramount for effective HIC systems. As reviewer agents handle more complex validation and refinement tasks, the interface must present distilled, actionable insights—such as highlighted inconsistencies or confidence scores from benchmark evaluations—rather than raw data dumps. Research should explore adaptive interfaces that prioritize information based on the human's role, the criticality of the decision, and the current operational context. This aligns with findings on enhancing user experience in AI systems, where reducing cognitive load is directly linked to higher satisfaction and more effective oversight [29].

The ultimate goal is to preserve human decisional ownership, particularly in high-stakes domains where benchmark validation underscores both capabilities and risks. This requires architectures where humans retain final authority over consequential judgments, with AI agents serving as empowered, transparent advisors. Future paradigms must embed mechanisms for explainability and rationale provision, allowing humans to understand the agent's reasoning path before endorsing or overriding its suggestions. This approach, transitioning towards a "Humanity-in-the-Loop" model, ensures that technological augmentation enhances rather than diminishes human agency and ethical responsibility in the report generation lifecycle [28].
