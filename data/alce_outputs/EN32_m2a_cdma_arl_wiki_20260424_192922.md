Machine learning (ML) methods can detect reporting bias and publication bias in biomedical evidence synthesis primarily by leveraging statistical algorithms that learn from data [1]. A key application is in the context of systematic reviews and meta-analyses, which synthesize quantitative data from multiple studies to compute a combined effect size [3][5]. ML can automate the identification and assessment of studies, helping to detect if the available literature is skewed due to selective publication of positive results (publication bias) or incomplete reporting of outcomes (reporting bias).

Specifically, deep learning, a subdiscipline of ML that uses multilayered neural networks [2], can be trained on high-quality labeled datasets [4] to classify study characteristics and outcomes. These models can analyze patterns in published research, such as the distribution of effect sizes or the presence of missing data, to flag potential biases that might otherwise be missed in manual reviews [1][2]. The availability of such training datasets is crucial, as major advances in ML often depend on high-quality labeled data [4]. By integrating these automated tools into the systematic review process [5], researchers can more efficiently and objectively assess the risk of bias, thereby improving the reliability of evidence-based conclusions [3][5].
